Built on a documentary source almost nobody has read. Two volumes of Henry Salt's handwritten account of the first modern excavation of the Sphinx were found in spring 2002 in the archives of the British Museum's Department of Ancient Egypt and Sudan, during a move. They had been catalogued but never studied in depth, and many of the illustrations were otherwise unknown. They were published in 2007, in a small edition, as **British Museum Research Publication 164, *The Sphinx Revealed: A Forgotten Record of Pioneering Excavations*** 02:34–09:37.

He read the quotations from a copy he obtained in 2019, and says in the postscript that he scanned the pages while making the video and will share them with supporters.

The book's cover is held on screen and names its editors — Patricia Usick and Deborah Manley — who are not named in the narration, although their introduction is what he quotes at length. The cover image is one of Salt's own drawings, captioned in Salt's hand "A Sketch of the Sphinx & Captain Caviglia's excavations. April 1817", which is also where the spelling of Caviglia's name can be read directly.

Salt, Caviglia, and what the volumes are

Henry Salt was British Consul General in Egypt from 1816 until his death in 1827. The excavation was his friend Giovanni Battista Caviglia's, a Genoese merchant ship captain who spent decades working in Egypt. Salt prepared the account for publication and sent it to London, where colleagues edited it; it was never fully published, which is why neither Caviglia nor the work is well known today.

The researchers' own introduction is read out and describes the objects physically: both volumes bound in maroon buckram with an imitation sharkskin finish and brown calf spines and corners, the smaller lettered in gilt "Department of Antiquities: Salt memoir on pyramids and sphinx — text", the larger "— atlas".

Caviglia's other firsts, as listed: the first in modern times to find the connection between the well shaft and the descending passage in the Great Pyramid, the first to clear out and enter its subterranean chamber, and the first person in thousands of years to excavate the Sphinx. The previous full clearance given is the Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius, around AD 160; before that the 18th Dynasty, as recorded by Thutmose IV.

Napoleon's campaign sets the scene 05:03–07:10: roughly 170 savants, the Description de l'Égypte, the discovery of the Rosetta Stone, and the beginning of the modern field — though at the Sphinx his forces uncovered only the back. A digression follows on the Internet Archive, where the Description can be borrowed, and on the publishers then suing it, which he describes in strong terms as the digital equivalent of burning the library of Alexandria.

The dig, in Salt's words

The quoted passages are the substance of the entry, and they are about labour rather than mystery 09:37–17:47.

Salt sets the scale of it first: Caviglia's work in the Great Pyramid's chambers and passages "may be considered as sufficiently arduous, but the whole of these united exertions cannot in any degree be compared with the extreme laboriousness of his operations at the Sphinx."

The first trench, on the northern side opposite the shoulders, was abandoned. It was "not more than 20 feet across at the top and not above three feet wide at the bottom", and "the sand was so loose that the wind drove back at night more than half of what he could excavate in the day". The only result was a measurement from the top of the head to the base — and a description of what the body's surface was like there: "composed of irregularly sized stones built up with much care and covered with red paint, with no very clear indications of the form, but having three protruding ledges one below the other sufficiently broad for a man to walk upon." Salt read those as the lines of a mantle or dress and drew a restoration of the statue "as I conceive it to have been by the Romans".

The second excavation, in front, ran from early March to the end of June with 60 to 100 persons constantly employed, and advanced "more than a hundred feet from the Sphinx, measuring it on the surface". The sand is described at length: "the slightest breath of wind or concussion of any kind set all the surrounding particles of sand in motion… till the whole surface took no unapt resemblance to a cascade"; "if the labourers suspended their work for about an hour they found on their return that they had the greater part of their labour to do again"; and seven days on the southern side of the right paw "without making any sensible advance, the sand rolling down in one continual and regular torrent."

The finds: fragments of the uraeus, several segments of the beard — some still in the Cairo Museum — and a chamber under the chin. Salt gives it as "a small and tolerably regular chamber about ten feet long by five broad, situated immediately under the chin", with walls of carved and inscribed limestone, "which if the account of Pliny may be believed is not unlikely to have contained the body of Amasis, one of the kings of Egypt." That chamber no longer exists.

Its back wall turned out to be the Dream Stele. Salt describes it before anyone could read it: "a large block of granite… highly embellished on the face fronting the east with sculpture and bas-relief representing two sphinxes seated on pedestals with priests holding out offerings, and a long inscription beneath in hieroglyphics beautifully executed, the whole design being canopied with the sacred globe, serpent and wings." He adds that it "constitutes part of a small open temple, the two sides of which were composed of other tablets of calcareous stone" — one still in place, the other fallen on its face and since removed.

The transcription held on screen carries the editors' apparatus, and none of it is read out. The 60-to-100 passage is printed as "from sixty to one hundred persons {82} were [daily] constantly employed" — a footnote marker, a manuscript page number, and the word "daily" supplied by the editors in square brackets, where the narration gives the sentence plain. The same kind of printed cross-reference went unread in the Tanis entry, where it was Petrie's plate numbers, and in the Sphinx Temple entry, where it was Selim Hassan's page references.

Salt's drawings are shown from the book and carry their own handwritten titles. One is captioned "Restoration of the front of the Sphinx" and sketched as a complete seated figure with a full nemes headdress, a beard and a tiered mantle over the body — a conjecture drawn to show the three ledges as clothing, not a record of anything excavated. Another, the drawing of the stele in situ, carries a scroll lettered "TABLET of … Granite standing … between the fore legs of the Sphinx, Discovered by Captain Caviglia, April 1817" and is signed H. Salt.

The stele, and the inheritance argument

The Dream Stele is given as a 15-ton slab erected by Thutmose IV, cut from granite that was already part of an Old Kingdom structure and repurposed 17:47–20:44. Half the inscription is now missing. The dream it records: the prince slept in the Sphinx's shadow and was told that if he cleared the sand and restored the monument he would become king of all Egypt.

Two things are drawn from that. The first is the burial fact he returns to later — that 3,500 years ago the Sphinx was already buried to its neck. The second is his general reading of Egyptian chronology: that the earliest period had the highest capability in granite with copper and flint tools, that the Middle and New Kingdoms with better metals "could never match the works of the Old Kingdom in scale or grandeur and they were quick to repurpose and claim the mighty stonework of their ancestors", and that the likelier account is inheritance from an earlier civilisation — "after all, this is precisely what the Egyptians themselves say." The arithmetic behind the Fourth Dynasty half of that is the Old Kingdom timeline entry; the rebadging half, documented out of Petrie, is the Tanis entry.

What is under the paws

Between the paws Caviglia found sculptures of lions and a granite altar, part of which still stands. The observation he draws out is one visitors miss 20:44–26:41: the surface beneath the paws is not bedrock but a constructed stone platform, probably Roman. Whether anything lies beneath it he says is not publicly known, and he states his own guess — that it was laid straight onto bedrock and that any chambers would be much deeper.

The case that chambers under Giza are ordinary rather than exotic is made by listing them: the Osiris Shaft, up the causeway, descending some 100 feet with multiple levels — the subject of the Osiris Shaft entry; test shafts and areas beneath the Great Pyramid; miles of bedrock tunnels on several levels at Saqqara around the Step Pyramid; and the Serapeum.

Then the surveys, as he reports them:

  • Granite blocks reported deep underground in front of the Valley Temple, found by a test hole.
  • A Japanese seismic survey in the late 1980s using electromagnetic waves, indicating multiple cavities and tunnels beneath the Sphinx. The institution is not named.
  • The covert Schor expedition of 1996, also indicating cavities.
  • At least three known entrances into the Sphinx itself, most now concreted or bricked over, with one still open near the left hip at the rear; and first-hand accounts over the last couple of centuries of people entering and finding chambers and "the remains of boxes".
  • Lehner and Hawass supervised drilling in 2008 and 2009, after decades of refusing such tests, and declared nothing was found.

His objection to the last is about disclosure rather than about the result: "while I really have no reason not to believe that they didn't find anything with their drilling, this isn't exactly a scientific or comprehensive conclusion. All we have to go on from this is their declaration that nothing was found." He says no results and no information on methods have been made publicly available, and that this is "the real issue here — there just doesn't seem to be enough data." The same material, with the Edgar Cayce Foundation documents and Hawass's denials attached, is the Sphinx Temple entry.

One claim here rests on nothing shown: that the wooden boardwalk over the pavement is not merely for visitors, because "we've been told by an inside source that the opening or entrance to the tunnel system under the Sphinx is under this boardwalk." No source is named and no evidence is offered for it.

The Roman staircase

Salt's account settles a question his own excavation had raised 26:41–30:06. The stone platform terminated abruptly about two feet south of the last digit, which "led to the conjecture that the whole body of the Sphinx might be placed upon a pedestal." Carrying the work north disproved it: "it was discovered that the platform there continued on an uninterrupted level, and soon after, to our great surprise, an ascending flight of steps became visible, which in contradiction to our supposition proved the Sphinx to be placed in a hollow."

The steps belonged to a Roman staircase and esplanade running up and away from the paws to where the ground falls off the plateau, with raised viewing platforms he describes as a corporate box for the emperor, presumably for ceremonies held between the paws. Salt's sketch of it, numbered 45 in the published volume, carries his own title for the structure in his own hand: "View of Steps, Spectatory, back turned to the Sphinx." All of it has been demolished and no trace remains at Giza, except possibly the pavement.

Two other entries stand at the same spot. Selim Hassan's answer to the same pedestal question — reached from the stelae rather than from the trench, and concluding that the pedestal depicted on them is the Sphinx Temple itself — is the Sphinx Temple entry. And Curious Being's reading of the same Roman masonry, dismantled in the 1930s, is the Sphinx Temple's secret entry, where 1926 photographs are used to argue the temple was reassembled piecemeal as it was dug.

He adds that the limestone cut out to make the enclosure — blocks of over 200 tons — went into the Valley Temple.

The age argument

Four strands, and the first is the one with no fringe label on it 30:06–41:30.

The repairs contradiction. The paws and much of the lower body have been repaired over the last two centuries; the neck was previously much thicker and part of the headdress is reconstructed; the Romans repaired it and so did the dynastic Egyptians, several times. And: "it's also on record that there are Fourth Dynasty repairs to the Sphinx. How exactly does this work? The Fourth Dynasty was not a particularly long time, and if you made your monument in this period, why are there records of it also being repaired in the same time frame?" He stresses that this one is not contested — "it's stated by establishment Egyptologists like Lehner and Hawass."

The erosion. John Anthony West and the geologist Robert Schoch read the vertical channelling on the enclosure walls as rainfall erosion requiring extended heavy rain, which the region has not had since roughly 9,000 to 11,000 years ago. He adds that Wikipedia labels this a fringe theory and says so with some heat. He also says the 9,000–11,000 figure is the youngest estimate — "a very conservative figure, intended I think to engage the establishment" — and that the patterns could indicate 30,000 years or more. The channel's own survey of Giza erosion by block size and casing is the erosion study; Curious Being sets a published Sahara-extent paper against the wet-period requirement in the buried-secret entry, and reads the enclosure as a stormwater catch basin in the water erosion entry.

Göbekli Tepe against Hawass's counter-argument. The original objection to Schoch, still on the Wikipedia page, was Hawass's: that no megalithic civilisation or works predate the Egyptians to that degree. Ben grants it as reasonable when it was made — "to be fair, this argument was made only a couple of years after the dig in Turkey at the now famous Göbekli Tepe site had begun, and back then in the late 90s his observation was most likely correct." He then argues the site now contradicts it and is not mentioned on that page. A clip from the 2015 Egypt tour is played: Hawass stating he had no knowledge of Göbekli Tepe and that he does not believe in radar and has never found anything with it, and Graham Hancock answering that a major megalithic site "which is 11,600 years old", excavated by a respected institute, removes the absence-of-context argument. Göbekli Tepe's own excavation record is the Göbekli Tepe entry.

The head. His reading is that the head was recarved: that it does not carry the same degree of age as the rest, that it is too small for the body, and that older photographs showing the original neck dimensions make it obvious. He puts the recarving early — dynastic, probably early Old Kingdom and well before Thutmose IV, because depictions on the stelae already show the current human head — and notes the suggestion, credited to Matt at the Ancient Architects channel, that the face could be a Fourth Dynasty figure, possibly Khufu.

The argument he finishes on is the one that joins the burial record to the erosion debate. The Sphinx has been buried to its neck for most of recorded history — in Thutmose IV's time, before the Greco-Roman period, again before 1817, and again within decades of Caviglia's dig, as the early photographs show. The head is the one part that was never buried, and it does not carry the erosion that the body and enclosure walls do — while the wind-blown sand explanation for that erosion requires the opposite. "In what world does this explanation for what we can see with our own eyes make any sense?"

His conclusion: thousands of years of rainfall on a fully exposed enclosure, before the Sahara — back to the heavy rain period after the Younger Dryas of some 13,000 years ago, or earlier.

What you can check yourself

  • The source is a published book with a number. British Museum Research Publication 164, The Sphinx Revealed: A Forgotten Record of Pioneering Excavations, 2007, containing a full transcription of Salt's account and his drawings.
  • **The *Description de l'Égypte*** can be read at the Internet Archive, as the video says.
  • The beard fragments and parts of the uraeus are in the Cairo Museum and the British Museum and can be looked at.
  • The Dream Stele stands between the paws, and the loss of half its inscription is visible.
  • The platform beneath the paws is constructed stonework rather than bedrock — a thing to look at rather than take on trust, where the boardwalk allows.
  • Fourth Dynasty repairs to the Sphinx are in the mainstream literature, including Lehner's, and are not a fringe claim.
  • The erosion on the head against the erosion on the body and enclosure walls is a comparison anyone can make from photographs, and the antique photographs showing the head alone above the sand are widely published.
  • Göbekli Tepe's dating is published by its excavators, independently of this argument.

What the video does not settle

  • Nothing is measured, and almost nothing is first-hand. The evidential weight is carried by a nineteenth-century manuscript and by other people's surveys. The footage is illustration.
  • Salt's own readings are quoted as observation and are interpretation. The three ledges read as a mantle, the chamber under the chin read through Pliny as possibly holding the body of Amasis, and the restoration drawing made "according to my ideas of what the statue must have appeared" are all Salt's conjectures, and the video does not separate them from his descriptions. The drawing is captioned "Restoration" on its own sheet.
  • The published transcription is an edited text and the edits are visible. Footnote markers, manuscript page numbers and at least one word supplied in square brackets appear in the frames and are read past silently, so what is heard as Salt's sentence is in places the editors'.
  • The chamber under the chin no longer exists, so the single most striking find in the account cannot now be examined by anyone.
  • The seismic and radar results are reported, not shown. The Japanese survey of the late 1980s is not attributed to an institution, no report, trace or plot appears for it or for the Schor work, and the claim that cavities were indicated is relayed rather than evidenced.
  • The 2008–09 drilling is criticised for an absence of published data, which is a statement about disclosure. It leaves the question of what was found exactly where it was.
  • The boardwalk claim is anonymous. "We've been told by an inside source" is the whole of the support offered for it.
  • The video gives two different dates for the same event. Thutmose IV's clearance is dated "around 1550 BC" at 04:35 and "around 1450 BC" at 17:56; neither is sourced on screen. The channel also dates Caviglia's dig differently in different videos — 1817 throughout here, matching the April 1817 on Salt's own drawings, but 1816 in the Sphinx Temple entry. The Schoch work is placed in "the late 1990s", and the Marcus Aurelius clearance "around 160 AD", both as spoken figures.
  • The 15 tons, the 200 tons and the 100 feet are stated, not weighed or surveyed in this video; the measured figures in the quoted passages are Salt's, from 1817.
  • The head-erosion comparison is made by eye. No measurement of loss, no rate, and no account of the different limestone beds the head and the body are cut from appears; the comparison is offered from photographs.
  • The extension from 9,000–11,000 years to "30,000 years or more" is given as a possibility with no erosion rate, measurement or source attached, and is explicitly his own.
  • The Orion correlation is reported in two sentences — Bauval, Leo rising due east at the vernal equinox about 10,500 years ago, on the presumption the Sphinx was originally a lion — and the presumption is noted but not tested.
  • Wikipedia's treatment is the target of several passages, and what is established there is how the claims are labelled, not whether they are right.
  • Several names are inferred from mangled auto-captions. Caviglia, Schoch, Bauval, Lehner, Amasis, Posnansky and Pouillard are reconstructed; the channel credited for the Anubis reading is unintelligible in the audio; and the researcher credited for remarking on the pavement, rendered "Brian Forrester", is most likely Brien Foerster. All need checking.
