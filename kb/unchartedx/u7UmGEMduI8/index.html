The Improbable Timeline of the Old Kingdom Mega-Pyramid Builders!

The argument here is arithmetic and chronology rather than tool marks, which makes it the most checkable of the channel's big claims. Ben is explicit that he is setting out the mainstream account in order to test it: "I want to stress that this is the Orthodox interpretation of these times, the stuff that is taught in schools and it's in the textbooks."

He also opens by crediting the discipline he goes on to dispute 03:20–03:51: that any coherent picture of these periods exists at all is "something of a miracle and it's a real credit to the professions of archaeology and Egyptology. There is scant real firm evidence for any of this."

The sources, and what they say

03:51–08:03. Two king lists are named as primary: the Abydos king list in the temple of Seti I, and the Turin king list, a damaged papyrus from the Ramesside period. Ben notes the Turin papyrus was originally a tax roll with the king list written on its back — a detail he takes from the Wikipedia article, which he quotes, including its description of "mythical kings such as gods, demigods and spirits as well as human kings".

His objection to that phrasing is structural: the papyrus is one list, and "there isn't a special category labelled mythical kings and another labelled real kings" — the division is made by modern readers, not by the document.

He reads the last two lines:

"Venerable Shemsu Hor, 13,420 years. Reigns before the Shemsu Hor, 23,200 years. Total: 36,620 years."

Where the 36,620-year figure comes from.

The source for that quotation is on screen and is not the papyrus. What is shown is a block of web-page text over a photograph of the document in its museum case, with a browser address bar beneath it reading ancient-code.com/turin-royal-canon-ancient-papyrus-proves-gods-ruled-ancient-egypt/, and the photograph credited in the corner to saharazul.blogspot.com. The page's own wording is more cautious than the reading: it says the final two lines "seem to represent a resume of the entire document" and compares them to the Sumerian King List. Neither the hedge nor the site is spoken; the narration introduces it as "let me read the last two lines of the Turin papyrus".

A second caution he raises against his own case as much as anyone's 08:03–08:43: before about 650 BCE the only source for Egyptian history is the Egyptians themselves. After that it can be cross-checked against Greek records. The pyramid builders sit roughly two thousand years before that line.

The timeline, laid out

08:43–11:00. The run-through is conventional: Early Period from about 3150 BCE; the Old Kingdom from the 3rd Dynasty around 2686 BCE, lasting some 500 years to about 2181; a First Intermediate Period of roughly 150 years; the Middle Kingdom about 2060–1690; a Second Intermediate of 140; the New Kingdom from about 1549; the Third Intermediate from about 1069; then the Late Period to 332 BCE and the Ptolemaic period to Cleopatra.

The line he draws from it 10:30–10:43: we are closer in time to Cleopatra than she was to the Old Kingdom.

And the compression that the argument turns on 11:38–11:58: "dynasty" and "Old Kingdom" suggest centuries and many generations, but every surviving mega-pyramid is attributed to rulers spanning "a mere three to four generations of kings… all members of the same family", who largely lived concurrently.

The pyramids in order, and what is underneath them

12:22–25:21. The sequence is given as the mainstream one — Step Pyramid, Meidum, Bent, Red, Great, Second, Third — and at each site Ben's interest is in what sits below.

The Step Pyramid of Djoser, 3rd Dynasty, a reign of about 19 years around 2668 BCE. The structure above he calls an understandable progression from stacked mastabas. Below it are miles of tunnels and the more than 40,000 hard-stone vessels, held as the vases entry. He notes the contradiction he thinks they carry on their own: Egyptologists do not credit the Egyptians with the wheel until around 1500 BCE, and the vessels are, in his reading, lathe work. The measurement work done on them two decades later is held as the Danville inspection entry.

The granite burial chamber 15:37–17:25 is the find he flags as the strongest, and says is rarely filmed. Four levels down beneath the centre of the Step Pyramid, in a chamber he gives as about 28 m / 90 feet high, stands a freestanding precision granite box at least 15 feet tall, built from 32 blocks — most around 3 tons, some over 10 — dry-fitted. The granite had to come hundreds of miles from Aswan, be taken deep underground, and be shaped and laid there. His conclusion is one of sequence: "this giant chamber and the box simply must have been constructed here before the Step Pyramid was built on top."

What the footage of the Step Pyramid chamber actually shows.

The footage does not show the box. The sequence is shot looking up through dense red and blue scaffolding filling the shaft, lit by a single work lamp, and the granite is at best glimpsed behind the poles — which is what Ben says at the time, "you can see its granite walls if you look closely behind the scaffolding". Every dimension given for the chamber and the box is spoken over footage in which neither can be seen whole.

Zawyet el-Aryan 18:17–20:17. The great pit, explored by Barsanti in the early 1900s, who removed layers of interlocking limestone to reach granite blocks and a circular coffer found empty except for a black residue. Ben shows Barsanti's own early photographs, and then a clip from the 1955 film Land of the Pharaohs, filmed on the site — noting the prop sarcophagus moves with no tension in the ropes while "the scenery that's all around it is entirely legitimate". The site is now, he says, an army tip.

The pit as its excavator photographed it, with scale.

Barsanti's plates carry printed captions that date the work precisely. One reads, in French, "Fouilles de Zaouiét-el-Aryân. — Déplacement des blocs de granit. (Février 1912)" — the excavation, the moving of the granite blocks, February 1912 — against the narration's "early 1900s". Another shows the sunken coffer with a man in a suit and hat standing beside it for scale. In the photographs the coffer is plainly oval, noticeably longer than it is wide, where the narration calls it circular. The editor's own caption spells the site "Zaywet El-Ayran".

Meidum and Mastaba 17 20:32–23:22. The pyramid shares the Great Pyramid's 51-degree slope. Mastaba 17 beside it is mudbrick, over 300 feet long, and more precisely aligned to the compass than the pyramid next to it. Under it, through a robbers' tunnel, is a megalithic chamber with blocks that curve around corners, and the earliest known single-piece precision granite box — the start of the sequence that runs through the Lahun box entry and the Serapeum boxes of the Serapeum entry.

He states the evidence against his own reading of that box: Petrie found it with the lid prised open and mummified remains inside. His answer is that this shows the last use rather than the purpose.

The five-minute calculation

29:39–33:05. The centre of the video, and it is ordinary arithmetic done out loud.

The accepted construction time for the Great Pyramid is 20 to 27 years. Taking 25 years — the generous end — gives a little over 13 million minutes. The superstructure holds somewhere between two and three million limestone blocks averaging 2 to 3 tons. Taking 2.5 million:

one block quarried, cut, transported, shaped and precisely laid without mortar every five minutes, 24 hours a day, seven days a week, with no breaks, for 25 years.

And he lists what that figure does not include: the planning and design; the levelling of the bedrock and its foundation tiles; the granite inner chambers with single blocks up to 70 tons, lifted and laid high above the ground; the 300-foot shaft sunk to the subterranean chamber; and the vanished structures around it, of which the basalt floor remains. He adds a seasonal constraint — granite could only come down the Nile during the two to three months of flood each year, and other quarries such as Wadi Hammamat are overland. The moving of loads at that scale is the subject of the logistics entry.

The monument the five-minute calculation is about.

The comparisons he sets against it 33:05–34:02: Teotihuacan's pyramid, supposedly 150 years; the Hoover Dam about 5; the Suez Canal about 10; the Sydney Opera House about 14; the Panama Canal 33, from 1881 to 1914. And a case taken from the film Revelation of the Pyramids: a clay quarry in France filling a hole of roughly Great Pyramid volume with trucks running 24/7, which took about 12 years.

The paper he argues with by name 34:34–35:16. Frank Monnier, Journal of Egyptian Archaeology 2018, on the satellite pyramids of Meidum, whose conclusion he quotes:

"Contrary to widespread opinion, it was quite possible for Sneferu to build all three giant projects at Meidum and Dahshur, if one can accept that Khufu completed his huge funerary complex in around 27 years. There is nothing to say that Sneferu could not complete his three pyramids in 40 years, following a similar system of quarrying, transport and installation of stone blocks."

His response is that the conclusion rests on accepting the 27-year figure, which is the thing in dispute. The same arithmetic is run from the other direction on another channel in the containment-structure entry, where the pyramid's 2.6 million cubic metres is set against modern dam projects of 10 to 20 million and the build estimated at two to four years with modern methods — a reading that makes the volume unremarkable and the method the whole question.

What connects Khufu to the Great Pyramid

25:43–28:19. Ben's position is that the evidence is two items and both are weak.

  • A small statue that may or may not depict Khufu, and which he says was found near the Valley Temple rather than at the pyramid.
  • The painted marks in the relieving chambers above the King's Chamber, whose provenance he calls "dodgy at best", naming the dispute over whether they are original or were added by Howard Vyse. What he asks for is a test rather than a verdict: a chemical analysis of the paint to establish whether it dates from Vyse's time or is thousands of years older.

Between the two he cuts a clip of Zahi Hawass dismissing Robert Bauval — "the man is an amateur… those type of people, they look for two things: number one is to try to be famous… number two to make money" — and comments on Hawass's control of access to the sites. Bauval's own documents on the Giza permits are the spine of the middle section of the Sphinx Temple entry, where Hawass's denials are quoted alongside them.

The inheritance reading

40:14–44:05. The alternative he offers, and the specific observations he rests it on:

  • The mud lids. Many of the hard-stone vessels were reused as canopic jars and sealed with mud lids, still displayed as such. His question: why would people able to make the vessels make lids of a different material and form?
  • The writing is never polished inside the cuts, where the objects themselves are, and machining marks appear on the objects but never on the inscriptions. The same argument is made object by object in the colossal statues entry, and from Petrie's own reports in the Tanis entry. He is careful to grant that the dynastic Egyptians did develop high-quality granite inscription as their civilisation progressed.
  • Petrie on Mastaba 17, whose chamber took him 19 years to find: "it was as if there was never any communication between the builders of the chamber and the builders of the mastaba."
  • The oldest layers are the most advanced, at Meidum, under the Step Pyramid, and inside the pyramids themselves.

The wider frame is the familiar one — Younger Dryas cataclysm, a legacy civilisation, the Piri Reis and Oronteus Finaeus maps — and he marks it as interpretation: "I can't claim that any of what I'm saying here is definitive proof. It's merely an interpretation of the evidence as I see it."

What you can check yourself

  • The arithmetic. 25 years is 13.1 million minutes; 13.1 million divided by 2.5 million blocks is 5.3 minutes a block. Every input — the 20–27 year range, the two-to-three-million block count, the 2–3 ton average — is a published mainstream figure and each can be substituted.
  • Monnier's paper is in the Journal of Egyptian Archaeology and the quoted conclusion can be read in context.
  • The Turin papyrus is in Turin, published, and its final lines can be checked against the reading, as can the Wikipedia text quoted.
  • The dynastic dates given are the standard ones and are in any reference work.
  • Mastaba 17's alignment against the Meidum pyramid's is a published survey comparison.
  • The relieving chamber marks are photographed and documented, and the chemical test Ben asks for is a test anyone with access could specify.
  • The comparison projects — Hoover Dam, Suez, Panama, the Sydney Opera House — all have documented construction dates.

What the video does not settle

  • The calculation measures effort, not possibility. It establishes a required average rate given the stated assumptions. It does not establish what rate was achievable, because no figure for workforce size, parallel quarry faces or simultaneous working faces is introduced anywhere.
  • The block count and the time span are both ranges, and the choice within them moves the answer. Two million blocks over 27 years is a materially different rate from three million over 20, and only one combination is worked through.
  • "Quarried, cut, transported, shaped and laid" are treated as one serial operation. Whether they were sequential or concurrent is the crux of the claim and is not addressed.
  • No alternative timeline is proposed. The Old Kingdom attribution is argued to be implausible; no date, method or builder is offered in its place beyond "inherited", and nothing at any of these sites is independently dated.
  • Nothing is measured on camera. The 28-metre chamber, the 15-foot box, the 32 blocks and their tonnages are all stated; no source is given for the block count or the weights.
  • The Khufu evidence is characterised rather than examined. The statue is not shown with its excavation record, and the relieving-chamber marks are discussed without being displayed or sourced; the Vyse forgery claim is referred to as controversial and left there.
  • The Hawass clip is used as character evidence, not as a claim about the pyramids, and its source, date and context are not given.
  • The Turin quotation is sourced from an alternative-history website, whose address is legible on screen and is not named aloud, and whose own hedge — that the lines "seem to" summarise the document — is dropped in the reading. No translation, edition or Egyptological source for the figures appears.
  • The object called the strongest find is not visible in the footage of it. The granite box under the Step Pyramid is behind scaffolding throughout; the 28-metre chamber height, the 15-foot box, the 32 blocks and their weights are all spoken and none is sourced.
  • The Zawyet el-Aryan coffer is oval in the photographs and circular in the narration, and the plates' printed dates (February 1912) are more precise than the "early 1900s" given.
  • The king lists are read as records. That the Turin papyrus makes no internal division between gods and kings is a fact about the document; that its 36,620 years should therefore be treated as history is the inference, and the question of what kind of text it is goes undiscussed.
  • The ancient maps are asserted. Piri Reis and Oronteus Finaeus are introduced as containing coastlines mappable only with modern technology, with no analysis, source or counter-reading.
  • The Monnier quotation is answered with a verdict rather than a rebuttal — the conclusion is called "horse dung piled most high" — and the paper's own reasoning is not engaged beyond the circularity Ben identifies in its first clause.
