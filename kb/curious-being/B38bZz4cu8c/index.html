Sanxingdui Masks & Red-Haired Tarim Mummies: China's Secret

Tina · 18 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-24

This continues the metallurgy video of five weeks earlier, and it says so 00:30–00:51. Having eliminated what she considered the improbable candidates for who built Sanxingdui, she arrives at one: the Andronovo culture of the Altai Mountains in southern Siberia.

Her grounds for that shortlist, stated at the top 00:51–01:26: Andronovo dates from as early as 2200 BC and Sanxingdui runs 2100–1200 BC; Andronovo was geographically close to ancient China; it had bronze before the Chinese Bronze Age; it had a tradition of gold foil masks and a comparable tree-of-life symbol; and its men wore ear piercings. It is almost universally agreed among scholars, she says, that Andronovo was Indo-Iranian, and they mined copper and tin in the Altai. The Seima-Turbino culture used the same homeland from 2300–1700 BC, and its distinctive spearheads and axes — and local imitations of them — have been found across China including the Sanxingdui region 01:40–02:59.

The rest of the video is an attempt to add non-metallurgical evidence to that. It is honestly framed as an attempt: "Could they be the creators of the Sanxingdui civilization? Let's keep digging."

The argument from style

Sanxingdui bronzes are schematic and abstract. Most ancient cultures preferred realism, and she puts Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Greek and Roman sculpture up against the Sanxingdui pieces to show the gap — "a bit like comparing a renaissance painting to a later Picasso work" 02:59–03:35.

That Sanxingdui is schematic where the famous ancient traditions are realistic.

The Andronovo and their successors the Scythians also preferred the schematic, which she offers as a shared aesthetic rather than a shared workshop: "although the artifacts of Sanxingdui and the steppe culture are not exactly alike, these two cultures do share a similar aesthetic taste" 03:35–03:54.

The specific comparison is exaggeration. Scythian gold plaques of stags carry swollen, swirling, overlong antlers; Sanxingdui animal masks carry a large ornate projection at the nose and eyes that protrude on cylinders. Both, she argues, overemphasise a feature for effect 03:54–04:41.

Exaggeration as the shared trait.

Then the strongest of her visual pairings, and the one the video is built around: the Pazyryk horse masks — headpieces made for horses, fitted with tall artificial antlers or wings 04:41–05:07.

The pairing the argument leans on hardest - and the date she puts on it.

Have you ever seen anything like this? It seems these extravagant masks were made for special occasions. I am curious if the Sanxingdui craftsmen had similar ideas when they made the protruding eyes on the animal masks for dramatic effects.

Two Pazyryk textiles follow 05:54–06:27. One shows a seated goddess holding an item she reads as comparable to the tree of life and to the Sanxingdui bronze trees. The other, dated in the video to 500 BC, shows a creature with a human head, stag antlers, a leopard's body and wings — and she asks the reader to look at its ear and antlers against the ears and nose projection on the Sanxingdui animal masks.

The creature whose ears and antlers are set against the Sanxingdui animal masks.

Out of that comes her own proposal, offered as one 06:27–06:48: that the Sanxingdui animal masks were made to be mounted on wooden statues of mythical creatures, and that the rectangular openings cut in them are the fixings. She notes it would explain the ceremonial character of objects found burnt and buried in layers.

The fixings the wooden-statue proposal depends on.

Two smaller points in the same register. Six further ritual pits have recently been excavated at Sanxingdui, and no bronze masks were found in them; one of the new pits, she says, dates almost a thousand years later than the two 1986 pits, which she suggests fits ceremonies repeated over a long period 06:48–07:14. And the gold-foil covered cane she reads as a sceptre, a rank symbol in steppe and Indo-Iranian societies rather than a Chinese one, shown beside steppe objects wrapped the same way in patterned gold foil 07:19–07:59.

The gold cane read as a rank symbol, and where the comparison is drawn from.

The archaeological chain

Underneath the style argument is a route, and it is made of ordinary archaeology.

The Tarim Basin 08:46–09:25. Red-haired and light-haired remains of people who lived there between about 4,100 and 2,000 years ago. She gives a specific genetic result: paternal Y-DNA R1a1a, indicating an Indo-European origin, with maternal ancestry mixed and mostly from southern Siberia and central Asia — which she says matches the DNA from Andronovo burials.

The paper is on screen and she boxes its title in red, though she never names it aloud: Evidence that a West-East admixed population lived in the Tarim Basin as early as the early Bronze Age, BMC Biology 8:15, published 17 February 2010, open access, by Chunxiang Li and eleven co-authors including Victor H. Mair. Its samples are from the Xiaohe cemetery. The finding in the title is admixture; what she reads out is the paternal half of it.

The genetic result, and the paper it comes from.

The same passage illustrates the Andronovo themselves with three modern facial reconstructions.

What the Andronovo people are said to have looked like.

The steppe cultural package 11:00–11:31. Bronze ornaments, weapons and tools; wool capes; conical felt hats; braids — and, she notes, several Sanxingdui bronze masks also have braids. Cheese in the Tarim burials, and four-wheeled wagons.

Braided hair, offered as a shared custom.

What was found with the Tarim dead.

The Hexi corridor 11:31–12:45. The strip between the Tibetan Plateau and the Gobi that joins the Tarim Basin to the Chinese central plain, dotted with bronze-working oasis cultures — Siba and Qijia, the latter radiocarbon dated 2200–1600 BC. The spearheads, knives and axes recovered there point, she says, to early contact with Siberian and Central Asian cultures and with Seima-Turbino in particular, and some Chinese researchers hold that Qijia and Siba took their metallurgy from steppe peoples. Chinese records place pastoral steppe populations in the Hexi corridor and westwards until around 200 BC.

Qijia and Sanxingdui 12:50–13:47. The two were roughly 500 years contemporaneous and not far apart, and share artefact types: Seima-Turbino-style spearheads, plaques with turquoise inlay, jade objects, pottery — including three-legged clay vessels which she says are attributed to nomadic pastoral cultures west of ancient China.

These are the comparisons in the video that do what a comparison has to do. Both date ranges are printed on the frame — Sanxingdui 2100–1200 BC, Qijia 2200–1600 BC — and they overlap. The turquoise plaques share shape, inlay technique and the four corner loops she arrows as fixings; the tripod pots share a construction, not a mood. Two neighbouring cultures with overlapping dates and matching objects is an argument that needs nothing from Pazyryk. It runs for about a minute.

The comparison that is dated on screen and matches in period.

Sichuan 13:24–14:00. A basin ringed by mountains, settled for millennia, with artefacts showing Qijia influence — which implies cross-regional routes between the Hexi corridor and Sichuan as far back as the Neolithic, and therefore a way in through the mountain valleys.

The geography the whole migration argument runs on.

The conclusion is put as a possibility and hedged throughout 15:03–16:29: that over 4,000 years ago a group of central Asians or Altai nomads entered the Tarim Basin, crossed the Hexi corridor and reached Sichuan, over many generations, adopting skills and marrying locally along the way — and that Sanxingdui may equally be a composition of steppe and local culture rather than a transplant.

The suppression claim, again

It returns and is put more strongly than in the first video 09:25–10:20. The genetic studies of burials at Sanxingdui and Jinsha are, she says, kept secret. Two further cemeteries nearby, dated Neolithic to early Bronze Age, one said to be very well preserved with DNA testing "on the way" — five years ago, with no follow-up since.

This further adds to the suspicion that someone is hiding information. Therefore the DNA results must be controversial and may challenge common belief.

No source is shown for any of it in this video, and the forum post that carried it in the first one is not repeated.

What you can check yourself

  • Every culture named is a real archaeological culture with a literature: Andronovo, Seima- Turbino, Pazyryk, Scythian, Qijia, Siba. So are the dates she attaches to them, and they can be compared with published chronologies.
  • The Pazyryk horse headdresses are excavated objects from the frozen kurgans in the Altai, and they are published and photographed. So are the Pazyryk textiles, including the felt hanging with the seated figure.
  • The Scythian animal-style plaques are in museums in quantity, and whether the exaggeration she describes is there is a matter of looking.
  • The dates are the check. She gives Sanxingdui as 2100–1200 BC and dates one Pazyryk textile to 500 BC on screen. Anyone can lay her own figures side by side and see which way the comparison runs.
  • The Qijia radiocarbon range, 2200–1600 BC, is a published one and the Qijia–Sanxingdui artefact overlap she describes is catalogued material.
  • The Y-DNA claim is specific enough to look up. R1a1a as the paternal lineage of the Tarim population, with mixed maternal ancestry, is a stated result rather than an impression.
  • The braids are on the objects. Whether Sanxingdui bronze heads carry braided hair at the back is visible on the pieces themselves.
  • The rectangular openings are on the masks and can be examined for wear, position and whether they would work as fixings.

What the video does not settle

  • The central comparisons run backwards in time, on her own captions. She labels the horse mask "Pazyryk Horse Mask (600–300 BC)" and the textile "Pazyryk Tapestry (500 BC)". She gives Sanxingdui as 2100–1200 BC. The two images the argument leans on hardest are therefore between six hundred and sixteen hundred years younger than the thing they are offered to explain, and the figures are printed on the same screens. The video never addresses it, and the chain it uses to bridge the gap — Scythians succeeded Andronovo, Andronovo were Indo-Iranian — is descent, not evidence that the practice existed at the earlier end.
  • The sceptre comparison has the same problem and is not dated at all. The staffs she shows are from an Achaemenid-style relief, which would put them around 550–330 BC, and from a modern line drawing with a Getty Images watermark still on it.
  • The bright version of the textile is a modern redrawing. The felt as it survives is a worn, cracked, two-colour fragment; the image beside it is a complete full-colour reconstruction with a stock-image watermark across it. Both are shown, neither is distinguished, and the detail she asks the reader to look at is clearest in the one nobody excavated.
  • "Schematic" is not a diagnostic. Stylisation is one of the commonest things in world art and appears independently almost everywhere. The video establishes that two traditions are both non-realistic and treats that as a shared taste; no attempt is made to say what would count as distinctive rather than general.
  • The comparison class is chosen. Realism is represented by Mesopotamia, Egypt, Greece and Rome — four Mediterranean and Near Eastern traditions — and everything schematic then looks like a family. No schematic tradition unrelated to the steppe is put in the frame to test it.
  • Exaggeration is not a shared feature, it is a shared category. Swollen antlers on a stag and cylindrical eyes on a mask have nothing in common except that both depart from life. The argument needs the departures to be alike and does not show that they are.
  • The wooden-statue proposal has no evidence offered for it. No wood, no mount, no wear on the openings, no comparable mounted mask from anywhere. She presents it as a thought and it should be read as one.
  • The tree-of-life link is asserted through a resemblance to a resemblance. A figure on a Pazyryk textile holds something read as tree-like, which is then set beside the Sanxingdui bronze trees. Neither object is identified and the intermediate step is doing all the work.
  • A paternal-lineage match is not a population match, and the paper's title says so. The study she boxes in red is called Evidence that a West-East admixed population lived in the Tarim Basin. Her own account has the maternal ancestry mixed and mostly Siberian and Central Asian. What that describes is admixture, which cuts against a transplanted people and for the local-composition reading she offers at the end and does not pursue.
  • The paper is never named aloud and its sample size is never given. It is fully identified on screen — journal, volume, date, twelve authors, the Xiaohe cemetery — and the narration reduces it to "DNA analysis revealed". How many individuals carried R1a1a is the difference between a population and an individual, and it is not said.
  • The Andronovo appearance is illustrated with sculpture. Three forensic facial reconstructions stand in for the population, uncredited and with no indication of whose skulls they were built on. A reconstructed face carries as much of the sculptor as of the skull, and the video treats them as photographs of people.
  • Tarim genetics are used to argue about Sanxingdui, and nothing connects the two. No burial at Sanxingdui has been sampled — that is her own complaint. The genetic evidence is from a population 1,500 km away, and the link between them is the route, not a result.
  • The suppression claim has lost its source and gained confidence. In the first video it came from a forum post that disclaimed itself, marked "not sure if it is true". Here it is unsourced and the conclusion is put as necessity: the results must be controversial.
  • "No follow-up in five years" is not evidence of concealment, and no attempt is made to check whether anything was published in Chinese, whether funding continued, or whether the samples yielded anything.
  • The new-pit claims are uncited and the excavation was live. Six pits with no bronze masks, one dated almost a thousand years later than the 1986 pits — both are stated flat, without a source, in April 2021, while the excavation was still being reported week by week.
  • The Qijia–Sanxingdui link is the strongest thing here and is given the least time. Shared spearhead types, turquoise-inlaid plaques and hollow-legged tripod pottery between two contemporaneous neighbouring cultures is ordinary, checkable archaeology that would support contact without needing any of the style argument. Those are also the only comparisons in the video with both date ranges printed on the frame, and the ranges overlap. The section runs under a minute; the Pazyryk material runs to five.
  • Nothing distinguishes contact from descent, and she knows it. Her closing alternative — that Sanxingdui was a composition of steppe and local culture — is a different claim from a migration, and the two are left standing side by side without a test that would separate them.

What we have not checked

  • One source is identified on screen and has not been read. BMC Biology 8:15 (2010) is fully legible in the frame, and what is reported of it here — its title, its date, its authors, and that its samples come from Xiaohe — is what the screenshot shows. The paper itself has not been opened, so its sample size, its R1a1a counts and its own conclusions are unknown.
  • Everything else is as she gives it. The Qijia radiocarbon range, the Pazyryk dates, the Andronovo range, the recent excavation claims and the DNA secrecy have no source on screen.
  • The Achaemenid identification of the sceptre relief is recognition, not evidence. The video gives no date, site or period for it. The style reads as Persepolis-type Achaemenid work, and the date offered here follows from that identification, which has not been confirmed.
  • The Pazyryk coordinates recorded with the horse mask are inferred, from the culture's name, not from anything the video states about where the object was found.
  • Several images carry other people's watermarks — a Getty Images credit and number on the sceptre drawing, and a repeating stock-image mark across the redrawn textile.
  • The chronologies used in the honesty section are the video's own figures, taken from the narration and the screen, not from published chronologies. Where a date is wrong the criticism moves with it.
  • The transcript is a clean uploaded caption track, so proper nouns are as the channel wrote them. Two exceptions appear in the file itself: Andronovo is spelled "Androvono" twice and Pazyryk as "Pazyruk" once. Both are transparent slips and the corrected forms are used here.
  • No object in the video is located by museum, excavation or accession number — not the horse masks, not the textiles, not the plaques, not the Qijia material.
  • The three-legged vessel attribution has not been checked. That li and related tripod pottery are held to come from pastoral cultures west of ancient China is stated by her and is a claim about a large and contested literature.
