Enduring Mysteries of the Huashan Grottoes: Built by a Lost Ancient Civilization?

Tina · 13 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-23

whyhowscalemainstreamrock-cut-sitesquarriesmachining-and-tool-marks

The Huashan Grottoes are in Anhui province, on a hill above the Xin'an river. The name means something like mystical grottoes in Flower Hill. In 2000 a farmer gathering firewood fell into one of them; a government survey followed and found 36 artificial caverns spread over about five kilometres, and a second group of 36 has since been found nearby. They are described as the largest underground ancient structure in China, and only three — grottoes 2, 24 and 35 — are open 00:00–01:52.

The dating rests on the stalactites inside, which give at least 1,700 years. She is careful about what that means: it is a floor, so the caverns could be older. There are no historical records at all, and no consensus among archaeologists, architects, engineers or geologists on who cut them or why 00:00–00:33.

Her first figure is the one the rest of the video keeps returning to: at least 100,000 tonnes of rock was removed, and no spoil has been found 01:19–01:29.

Inside

Grotto 2, also called Huanxi cave, has a hall 140 m long covering over an acre, wide enough to drive a car through, on two levels with the lower one unexcavated. The layout she calls organic — rooms and pillars placed almost haphazardly, pillars in unusual shapes and sizes, ceilings averaging 4 m, a steady 15 °C year-round, and permanent pools of mineral-rich water with no fish in them 01:52–03:24.

The scale of a single hall, the marks on the pillars, and the light everything in the video is seen under.

Grotto 35, the 'Underground Palace', is 170 m long and covers 12,600 m², more than three acres, with a grand hall, pools, pillars and rooms on both sides and a two-storey H-shaped stone structure near the entrance. She puts the stone removed at 50,000 m³ and the internal height difference at 20 m 05:25–06:52.

Grotto 24 is reached by boat: four storeys with three of them submerged, six pillars on each level, twenty-four in all, more rectangular and more uniform than elsewhere. Each, she says, would take four people with outstretched arms to reach around 09:07–09:49.

The flooded grotto, and the scale of the pillars in it.

The features she reads as designed

The sloped ceilings. Both grotto 2 and grotto 35 have stretches of ceiling raked at about 45°, and she reports that the slope follows the slope of the hill above exactly, so that the workings never break through to the surface. She calls this an amazing architectural accomplishment showing sophisticated knowledge 06:59–07:31.

How she pictures the workings inside the hill, and the water levels the argument turns on.

The water. The lowest level of grotto 35 is the Clear Water Pool, and its surface sits 2 m below the level of the nearby Xin'an river. Her inference is that the pool cannot be connected to the river, or the two would stand level — so where does the water come from, and why is it mineral-rich when the river is fresh 06:07–06:37?

No smoke. One pillar shows signs of torch smoke. Everything else is clear of any trace of oil lamps or torches 10:00–10:16.

The one trace of fire in the whole complex, and what it sits beside.

The finds, and what she makes of them

Excavation in grotto 2 produced porcelain plate fragments and iron tools. She describes the tools as small, the size of a rice bowl, and asks whether anyone believes such things could have cut caverns on this scale and left marks this large. Her reading is that someone found the cave later, brought in plates and tools, and used it as a retreat 03:24–03:55.

The excavated material, and whether it matches the description given of it.

Also in grotto 2: dinosaur footprints dated 65–120 million years, and a petrified wood log dated 150 million years. Dinosaurs lived in the region in the Cretaceous, so the footprints are local. Of the log she says the original builders worked around it and made a chamber for it 03:55–04:31.

The fossil log she says the builders made a chamber for.

From there she asks a mining question. Petrified wood forms by silicification, which needs sustained contact with mineral-rich water, and it is usually found in groups — the Petrified Forest in Arizona is her example. So where is the rest of the petrified wood at Huashan, and were the grottoes a mineral or fossil mine 04:31–05:25?

The tool marks

There are many different marks inside, she says, as though the builders were trying out various tools, and they are distinct and large — very similar to the oversized uniform marks at Longyou, Yangshan quarry, Baalbek and Petra. The closest match she offers is the Bazda caves in Turkey, where she says the pillars, the layered marks and the stone finishes are much the same 07:31–08:19.

Her closest cross-continental match, with both halves shown at once.

Her conclusion is that these marks match modern machine marks, that hand tools such as chisels or pickaxes cannot have made them — argued in an earlier video rather than here — and that the sites were cut by one or more lost civilisations with advanced machinery, which mainstream researchers have not considered because they will not look further back than about six thousand years 08:19–09:07.

The four explanations, and where she says each stops

This is the section that earns the entry 10:16–12:23.

A quarry. How was stone cut in the dark? The sandstone here is not high quality and does not match the stone used at nearby archaeological sites. Even if it were useful, why not take it from the surface as an open pit? And why carve levels, separate chambers and pools if the point was to carry the stone away?

A tomb. No tombs and no funeral offerings were found. And the grottoes are full of water, which in Chinese mausoleum design is a serious fault and is strictly avoided.

Storage. The same damp rules it out.

Barracks. Chinese records do place troops in the area. But cutting all this to hide and train soldiers would be enormously time-consuming; there is no daylight and no large dry open ground; the video asks how an army would eat and cook; and a single entrance with no second exit is exactly what a garrison does not want in a siege.

Her closing position is that the grottoes are an archaeological conundrum, and that they belong with Baalbek, Petra, Yangshan, Longyou and Bazda as the work of lost civilisations with machinery comparable to our own 12:23–13:00.

What you can check yourself

  • Three of the grottoes are open to visitors, and everything the argument turns on — the pillar forms, the pools, the raked ceilings, the marks on the walls — is in ordinary tourist photographs and video.
  • The finds are on display. Her own frame shows them laid out in a case: a round ceramic object with a hole through it, a small forked iron piece, three long slender iron bars, and around twenty blue-and-white porcelain sherds, several painted. Blue-and-white is a datable class of ware, and what the excavation report says about the assemblage is a matter of record rather than of interpretation.
  • The petrified wood has a site placard beside it, bilingual, visible in her frame and not read out. It carries the site's own account of the fossil.
  • The stalactite date is a minimum, and she says so. A reader can hold her to that in both directions: it does not license a much older date any more than it forces a younger one.
  • The water-level claim is a measurement. Whether the Clear Water Pool sits two metres below the Xin'an river, and whether its water is chemically different, are questions with numerical answers.
  • The soot is photographable. Whether one pillar or many carry burn marks, and whether the sockets cut into the pillars held lamps, beams or something else, is settled by looking.
  • Bazda is a real site in Turkey and is photographed by visitors, so her strongest comparison can be checked by anyone with an internet connection rather than taken on trust.

What the video does not settle

  • The tools shown are not the tools described. She calls the iron finds small, the size of a rice bowl. The frame shows three long slender iron bars laid out across the case, each running the best part of its width — quarry tools by their proportions. The only bowl-sized object in the picture is the round ceramic piece, which is not a tool and is not identified.
  • The dating evidence found inside is dismissed with an untested story. Porcelain and iron in a cavern are the strongest dating material available, and the reply — someone found the cave later and brought them in — is offered without anything to support it. It is the same move the video objects to when the other side makes it.
  • "No rock debris was found" is asserted without saying who looked. A hundred thousand tonnes spread over seventeen centuries beside a river, in a farmed and terraced valley, is not obviously something one would expect to find in a heap. No survey, spoil-tip search or sediment study is cited.
  • The raked ceilings have a plain reading that is never stated. A working that follows the hillside at a constant depth is what keeps a safe roof over the workers' heads and stops the ceiling breaking out. The video treats the match with the surface slope as evidence of sophistication without considering that it is also the obvious requirement of cutting into a hill.
  • The water argument is hydrology done without a hydrologist. A pool standing below river level can be perched, spring-fed, or separated from the river by impermeable rock, and mineral content differs between groundwater and surface water as a matter of course. The video draws a conclusion from a level difference without addressing any of that.
  • The petrified log is described as housed rather than encountered. In the frame the fossil lies in a horizontal bed at the back of a cut recess, which is what exposing a fossil in a rock face looks like. That the builders made a chamber for it is an inference about intention, and the question of where the rest of the petrified wood went assumes there was more of it.
  • The rejections of tomb, storage and barracks are arguments from plausibility. "Far-fetched", "too time-consuming", "wouldn't it make more sense" — none of it is evidence, and the absence of burials in three grottoes out of seventy-two is not the absence of burials.
  • The single-entrance objection is not established. Grotto 2 is described as having an unexcavated lower level, most of the caverns are untouched, and how many entrances any of them has is not stated.
  • Whether the local sandstone matches nearby sites is a petrographic claim with no source. It is the sharpest objection to the quarry reading and it rests on an assertion.
  • The "impossible for hand tools" step is imported. The argument is made in an earlier video and is not made here, so the claim that machine tools are required arrives as a premise.
  • A metric conversion is wrong on screen. The hall of grotto 2 is given as "140 meters or 160 feet". A hundred and forty metres is around four hundred and sixty feet.
  • The lighting is not neutral. Almost every interior in the video is under coloured floodlighting installed for visitors. Saturated light of that kind flattens surface texture and changes how tool marks read, and no note is made of it while surfaces are being compared with other sites.

What we have not checked

  • No excavation report has been located or read. The porcelain, the iron tools, the dinosaur footprints and the petrified wood are known here only from her frames and her account of them.
  • The 1,700-year stalactite date is unsourced. No laboratory, method or publication is named, and it is the only date in the video.
  • The figures are as she gives them and have not been traced: 36 grottoes over 5 km and a second group of 36; 100,000 tonnes removed; grotto 2 at 140 m and over an acre; grotto 35 at 170 m, 12,600 m² and 50,000 m³; ceilings averaging 4 m; 15 °C year-round; a 20 m internal height difference; and the 2 m gap between pool and river.
  • The claim that no spoil has been found is not attributed to any survey.
  • The identification of the round ceramic object in the finds case has not been made. It is not named in the video and nothing here establishes what it is.
  • The site placard beside the petrified wood is legible in part only — enough to see it is bilingual and mentions a figure around 150 million years — and its text has not been read.
  • Bazda has not been examined independently. The comparison is judged from her two panels alone, and the Bazda photograph is not credited.
  • The transcript's "Yanghsan" is corrected to Yangshan, and "Baalbeck" to Baalbek.
