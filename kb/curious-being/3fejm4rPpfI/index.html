Ancient Egyptian Stone Vases: Lost Machining Techniques?

Tina · 26 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-09-28

deflatedmainstreamhowconvergenceprecision-stone-vesselsmachining-and-tool-marksdating-and-chronology

The claim, stated as its authors make it 00:25–01:12: Ben of UnchartedX and Adam Young argue that Egyptian stone vases, predynastic ones especially, hold tolerances measured in thousandths of an inch — rivalling or exceeding modern precision engineering. Ben's inference is that precision like that in granite implies advanced, possibly mathematically-driven machine manufacturing, and that nothing comparable survives from later Egyptian history because the skills were lost.

Against that she sets a 2025 paper 01:12–02:05. The physicist Dr Max Fomitchev-Zamilov published in Heritage Science a study of 3D scans of 68 stone vessels in three groups — 19 predynastic museum pieces, 25 modern machine-made and 24 contemporary handcrafted — scored on concentricity and circularity. His conclusion was that the archaeological pieces are entirely the work of human craftsmanship with ordinary tools. He also observed that the "ultra-precise" vessels appear only in private collections, never among authenticated museum pieces, and read that as a sign they are modern forgeries.

Her own position on method follows from that, and she states it 02:59–03:12: she uses only museum pieces and published reports, precisely to keep possible forgeries out of the argument.

What the video adds is not another measurement. It is a sequence.

The vases are late, not early

The point the debate usually leaves out 03:24–06:31. Egyptian stone vessels appear with the predynastic Naqada culture (4000–3100 BC), a Chalcolithic society, and become common and sophisticated from about 3500 BC.

But fine stone vessels are far older than that. 11,000–12,000 years ago, at Göbekli Tepe, Karahan Tepe and Körtik Tepe, people were making stone pots, plates, bowls and troughs — round, thin-edged, smoothly finished, some carrying geometric and animal carving, some with small drilled holes in the sides, in limestone, chlorite and harder greenstone. Körtik Tepe is the oldest known Neolithic site in Turkey, occupied from 10,700 BC through much of the Younger Dryas for over a millennium. These vessels predate the earliest Egyptian ones by 5,000 to 6,000 years.

Fine stone vessels were being made 5,000 years before the Egyptian ones.

The tools found with them are the other half of the point. At Karahan Tepe: a flint and obsidian assemblage, chisels and adzes of river pebble, flywheels, grindstones and pestles. At Körtik Tepe, perforated stone pieces came up alongside the stone bowls — and from their size and the placement of the holes she reads them as flywheels and handholds for pump drills.

A pump drill uses a weighted flywheel and a crossbar pushed up and down to produce rapid oscillating rotation; a bow drill wraps a string round a spindle to turn back-and-forth motion into continuous rotation at higher speed 05:23–06:10. With a hard bit and an abrasive slurry, either will cut stone. Both stayed in working use into the Industrial Revolution and in places into the twentieth century.

What is on the vessels

Egyptian vessel surfaces are usually polished, which erases the tool marks 07:16–07:31. What survives is on the unfinished and the interiors 07:31–08:16:

  • A basalt jar of Naqada I–II in the British Museum, whose own catalogue description records striations made by stone drill bits inside the jar.
  • Interiors showing rotary drill or lathe marks from at least Naqada II (3500–3200 BC), including predynastic basalt jars with lug handles.
  • An unfinished Old Kingdom basalt vessel with circular rotary abrasion marks on the outside that were never polished away.

The British Museum label is on screen, and it carries a figure she never reads out. The object is EA 32520, and under the striations sentence the label continues: "Experiments suggest it took between 10 and 40 hours for a trained worker to make a small vessel like this, depending on the hardness of the stone." That is the mainstream estimate of the labour involved, printed by the museum beside the object, and it is the most directly relevant number in the video.

The insides of these vessels carry the marks of a rotating tool, and the museums say so.

The objects that follow carry their accession numbers in frame, which matters given the standard she set herself: the lug-handled jar with striations is UC 4978 and a second striated vessel is UC 15475, both Petrie Museum pieces — so the British Museum jar and the ones shown seconds later are different objects, not one.

The mace heads, and then the gemstones

Two bridging arguments, and they are the strongest part of the video.

Mace heads 09:07–10:09. Disc-shaped stone mace heads with precisely drilled holes date to 4500–4000 BC, before Naqada — a date she takes from The Met's own collection page, shown on screen with that line highlighted.

Precisely drilled stone discs predate the vessels.

Their conical holes are the signature of a stone drill bit. Naqada mace heads, one in diorite, carry concentric lines inside the drilled hole — the same striations found inside the vessels. The operations are near-identical: shape stone to a symmetrical smooth form, then drill a hole through the middle. And the mace heads are about the size of the smaller granite vessels.

Gemstones 10:09–12:19. Predynastic Egyptians were drilling materials harder than granite. Her figures, all on the Mohs scale:

hardness
Granite6–7
Quartz7 — harder than granite
Carnelian (a form of quartz)6.5–7
Red aluminous garnet6.5–7.5, surpassing quartz
Lapis lazuli5–6, the same as basalt

The objects: a string of beads dated 3300–3100 BC in carnelian, garnet, quartz and glazed steatite — its catalogue page shown in full, with a findspot as specific as they come, Abadiya, Cemetery H, Tomb H129, EEF excavations 1898–99; a 3300 BC predynastic diadem of turquoise, garnet, gold and malachite; lapis beads from Naqada sites dated 3650–3100 BC, the lapis imported from Central Asia. Red garnet beads are small and often drilled from both ends.

Her conclusion from it: if they could drill beads in carnelian, quartz and red garnet, basalt and granite vessels are not the anomaly they are presented as. They may well have used quartz itself as drill bits.

They were drilling stones harder than granite, and the objects are provenanced.

She adds that the Naqada culture was not primitive in any other respect either 12:19–13:04 — siltstone cosmetic palettes, and a flint dagger with an ivory handle carved with war and hunting scenes.

The mechanism underneath all of it is abrasives 13:04–13:47: flint, bone, bow and pump drills, stone and copper chisels, and sand and emery mixed with water or oil. Without them, copper or bronze alone does nothing to carnelian or garnet. The tool is a carrier; the grit does the cutting.

Why the tools are missing

A passage worth lifting out of this video entirely, because it applies far beyond it 13:47–14:38. Rotary drills were demonstrably in use in predynastic Egypt at least 6,000 years ago — the perforated objects and the drilled beads prove it. Yet the earliest archaeological evidence of bow drills is 2,500 years later, in the New Kingdom.

Her explanation is preservation and recognition, not absence:

  • the shafts, bows, leather and strings are perishable
  • flywheels and stone handholds are ignored or misidentified when they do survive
  • stone and copper bits are small and hard to tell apart from other worked stone

"Drills are nearly invisible in many assemblages."

The tool, drawn by the people who used it

The depictions run the same way — late, and no guide to when the tool began 14:38–16:17.

The earliest known bow drill images are in the Tomb of Rekhmire, New Kingdom, 1479–1400 BC, whose reliefs show several distinct drilling operations: a bow drill for carpentry, a different one for drilling beads, and a weighted drill with a curved handle used specifically for hollowing the interior of a stone vessel.

That last tool is depicted over a thousand years earlier, in a 5th dynasty tomb relief of stone vessel production from the late 26th to 25th century BC. What it shows: two workers holding the angled top handles of hand drills weighted with bags of stones, hollowing two vessels; two more polishing the outsides with stone; one polishing the inside of a large jar.

And one detail that is worth the whole section — above the drill the ancient artist added a small explanatory drawing of the part you cannot see: the hidden bottom component, a horizontal drill bit mounted on a forked shaft. A cutaway diagram, drawn in the Old Kingdom.

That detail also answers the specific objection the vase argument usually rests on, and she notes the capability without labouring it: with bits of different sizes on a shaft like that, an artisan can work the narrow neck or the wider body — cutting sideways, wider than the opening. Bits were made in flint, diorite, quartzite, limestone and copper tubes.

The people who used the tool drew it, including the part you cannot see.

The experiments

Two, and both are checkable 16:17–18:19.

In 1983, two researchers ran a bow drill with a copper tube on red granite, with and without abrasives, and found that abrasive slurry — with water, or a lubricant such as olive oil — produces concentric cutting lines.

Recently the YouTube channel Scientists Against Myths used a weighted drill with a copper tube of the ancient Egyptian pattern to drill granite with abrasives. The mechanics she draws out: the stone weight supplies constant downward pressure so the artisan does not have to push, and its inertia damps speed fluctuations into a steady rotation — a flywheel by another name. The copper tube is not the cutter; it is a carrier for the harder mineral grains in the slurry, which do the microscopic cutting. It went through red granite and produced a core with tight concentric lines resembling those on the granite cores found at Egyptian sites. She sets the two side by side, and the ancient core in that comparison carries its accession number in frame, U.C. 16036.

A copper tube and abrasive slurry produce the same core as the ancient ones.

She also reports Olga Vdovina, working breccia and diorite with rock, wood, rope, emery, sand and water 19:39–20:43. Cutting, shaping and polishing were done with another piece of rock and with ropes carrying abrasive slurry; the interior was hollowed with wooden drills and abrasives — more primitive than dynastic stone bits and copper tubes. Measured against a comparable museum piece, the diorite replica holds equal or tighter tolerances than the Egyptian original, which matches Fomitchev-Zamilov's scan result that modern handcrafted vessels sit on a par with predynastic ones.

The exterior, which is where she is honest

From the reliefs we learn the order of work: the exterior shape was carved first and the interior hollowed afterwards 19:01–19:19.

And then the admission 19:13–19:19: "till today there is no surviving illustration on how the highly symmetrical exterior shape was achieved." ![[unfinished-exterior]]

Her inference — from the Old Kingdom basalt bowl with rotary abrasion marks on its outside, another Petrie piece, UC 42075 — is that some kind of lathe or turning tool existed, with the stone rotating rather than the tool, which would account for the tight tolerances and low circularity deviations. She supports it by noting that predynastic Egyptians already used turning devices for pottery: a rough-shaped stone secured to a single-axis lathe, a potter's wheel or an Egyptian stone drill, with a worker holding hard stone or a flint-tipped tool against it and feeding abrasive slurry 20:50–22:10. That is, she says, what a modern stone worker does with a motorised lathe and carbide-tipped tools. The fundamentals have not changed; the power source has.

The "lost skills" claim, directly refuted

Stated as false 22:23–23:30. Precise stone vessels were made throughout ancient Egyptian history and into Roman Egypt; there are plenty of artefacts. They were luxury goods restricted to elites, and frequently imitated in cheaper materials. Styles and uses shifted — status symbols, funerary and ritual display, cosmetic containers, canopic jars.

Her explanation for the decline is economic, not technological: metal vessels took over the status-symbol role, at which point a time-consuming stone vessel stopped being profitable. She adds that Egyptian stone vessels were prized and collected around the eastern Mediterranean in the Bronze Age, and may have prompted the Cyclades and the Minoans to make their own.

Her conclusion

Unusually explicit, and the second sentence is the part that matters 25:20–25:39:

"I think the precise Egyptian stone vases were made by traditional tools, not lost ancient high-tech. There remains the possibility of lost advanced civilizations with machinery; just the Egyptian stone vessels were not it."

The other side of this argument, made by the people whose work is discussed above: The Tiny Ancient Artifacts Changing History — UnchartedX's February 2025 status report on the vase scan project, which presents Mark Qvist's design analysis and Max Zamilov's comparative study as standing results, quotes the Imhotep Museum against the idea that the tomb scenes explain hard-stone vessels, and answers the forgery charge directly.

The drilling argument from the other side: The Story of the Enigmatic and Mysterious Tube Drills of Ancient Egypt narrows the same dispute to a single object — whether the grooves on Petrie's granite core number 7 form a continuous spiral or a stack of horizontal rings. The two arguments do not quite meet. This video asks whether abrasive drilling was available to predynastic Egypt and capable of the materials; that one asks whether abrasive drilling produced these particular marks.

What you can check yourself

  • The British Museum jar, EA 32520. Its catalogue entry is public, and so is the 10-to-40-hour experimental estimate printed on its label.
  • The other vessels, by number. UC 4978, UC 15475, UC 42075 and the granite core U.C. 16036 are all Petrie Museum pieces with online records.
  • **Fomitchev-Zamilov, 2025, Heritage Science.** The paper, its sample of 68 vessels in three groups, and its concentricity and circularity metrics can be read directly.
  • Körtik Tepe and Karahan Tepe. The dates, the stone vessels and the perforated stones are published excavation material.
  • The 5th dynasty relief and the Rekhmire reliefs. Both are photographed and published; whether the explanatory cutaway sits above the drill is visible in the relief itself.
  • The Mohs values. Quartz at 7, carnelian 6.5–7, garnet 6.5–7.5, granite 6–7, lapis 5–6 — all standard reference figures.
  • The experiments. The Scientists Against Myths drilling footage and Olga Vdovina's vessels are both online and can be watched rather than taken on report.
  • Later stone vessels. The claim that production stopped is settled by walking through any major Egyptian collection and looking at the dates on Middle Kingdom, New Kingdom and Roman-era stone vessels.

What the video does not settle

  • The exterior remains unexplained, and she says so. The lathe is an inference from abrasion marks on one unfinished bowl. No lathe, no bearing, no turning fixture and no depiction of one has been found, and the video offers none.
  • The tolerance figures are never engaged directly. The claim being answered is a numerical one — microns of concentricity, wall-thickness uniformity, axis alignment — and no measurement is quoted, recomputed or compared here. She defers entirely to Fomitchev-Zamilov's scans.
  • The forgery finding is reported, not tested. "Ultra-precise vessels appear only in private collections" is his observation; the video neither checks it nor asks what follows if a provenanced museum piece did turn out to be ultra-precise.
  • The 1983 experiment is credited to "two doctors" with no names, title or journal.
  • The replication comparison is one replica against one museum piece. Vdovina's diorite vessel holding equal or tighter tolerances is a striking result and a sample of one.
  • The mace-head bridge is an analogy, not a demonstration. Drilling a hole through a disc and hollowing an undercut body through a narrow neck are different problems. The forked-shaft tool in the relief is the answer to that, and the video shows it but does not connect the two explicitly or test the capability.
  • No dates are argued for the vases themselves. The chronology used throughout is the accepted one; this is an argument about method, not about when.
  • "Nature Heritage Sciences" is how the journal is named on the narration; Heritage Science is a Springer Nature title, and the exact reference is not shown.
  • A highlight that stops one character short. The Met's page for the disc-shaped mace head gives its date as "ca. 4500–4000 B.C. (?)". Her yellow highlight covers the date and ends before the question mark, so the uncertainty the museum flags does not carry over into the narration. It does not damage the argument — the mace heads are predynastic on any reading — but the video presents a queried date as a settled one.
