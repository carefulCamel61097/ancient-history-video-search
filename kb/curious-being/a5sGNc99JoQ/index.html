Göbekli Tepe: New Excavations Rewrite Everything We Thought We Knew

Tina · 21 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-23

mainstreamdeflatedwhygobekli-tepeshared-symbolshandbags

The oldest bag-shaped carvings anywhere are at Göbekli Tepe in south-eastern Turkey, a site almost 12,000 years old whose discovery overturned the teaching that pre-pottery Neolithic people were nomadic hunter-gatherers who built nothing permanent. Among its oval enclosures stand T-shaped pillars, some 5.5 m tall. Pillar 43 carries three bag-like reliefs along with animals and patterns 00:00–01:35.

The carving the whole handbag argument starts from, and where it sits.

Her opening move is methodological and it is the right one 01:35–02:44. You cannot read one pillar in isolation. Pillar 43 is a surrounding pillar, not a centre pillar, in Enclosure D, which is one of over twenty stone circles at the site. Whatever it means, it is part of the narrative of its own enclosure and probably connected to the others. So the carvings have to be read as a story, with their context — and the context comes from excavation.

Which is where the video actually goes. Most existing theories about the Göbekli Tepe bags rest on the initial excavation results, and in the last few years those have changed 02:44–03:30.

What was thought

The bag theories she lists 03:31–04:44: that the bags held seeds sent from heaven to replant the earth after the flood; that they were batteries carried by ancient astronauts. On the pillars generally: that the carvings are constellations; that researchers from the University of Edinburgh published a paper reading Pillar 43 as a record of the Younger Dryas comet impact and the enclosures as observatories; and Andrew Collins' argument that the pillars, being roughly north-oriented, were aimed at the setting of Deneb, which marks the entrance to the Milky Way's Great Rift.

What the comet-impact reading is cited from.

All of those readings — astronomical, celestial, symbolic — fit the original interpretation by Klaus Schmidt, the site's discoverer, who called it a cathedral on a hill 04:44–05:43. Schmidt's reasons were specific: across more than 25 years of excavation he found no hearth, no domesticated plants and no domesticated animals, so he concluded nobody lived there full-time, even though the site was used and expanded for 2,000 years. It became known as the oldest temple in the world. And the uniform fill over the enclosures suggested they had been deliberately buried as new circles were built.

That left the puzzle sharper, not softer: why would nomads move, carve and erect megaliths they did not live in, and then bury them?

One more piece pointed the same way 06:03–06:50. The excavators found a wide range of stone points, the oldest of them pre-pottery Neolithic A types — el-Khiam, Helwan and Aswad — which suggests people came to Göbekli Tepe from across the Levant and the Fertile Crescent. If they travelled to a place that had no practical purpose, it must have been a destination for ritual.

What the new trenches found

Schmidt died in 2014. His successors, Lee Clare and his team, dug several keyhole trenches down to bedrock, several metres below the floors of the large oval buildings — and found something different 06:50–09:01.

Evidence of domestic houses and year-round settlement. A large cistern with channels to collect rainwater, which is what makes living on a dry hilltop possible. A series of round-oval structures built on or just above the natural limestone bedrock, bordering a small activity area that produced a sequence of hearths. The flint assemblage and the stratigraphy put them in an early occupation phase, most likely Pre-Pottery Neolithic A, about 9,500–8,700 BC.

The evidence for permanent occupation, as the excavators published it.

And across the site: stone animal sculptures, hundreds of stone vessels, thousands of grinding tools, troughs for processing wild grain into porridge, and chemical residue in some of the large troughs pointing to fermentation and beer brewing. Great numbers of animal bones read as food remains. Human bone, and a human burial.

The beer claim, and what its own source says about it.

So the people of Göbekli Tepe lived there permanently, and were effective hunters and cultivators — or at least had a way of gathering wild grain in quantity.

And the deliberate burial is now doubted 09:01–09:55. A review of the evidence makes other explanations look increasingly likely: buildings collapsing, and eroded deposits washing down from the higher knolls and slopes of the mound. From the air, the excavated part of Göbekli Tepe is mid-hill, not on top of the plateau, so it would accumulate sediment from above. Collapse and erosion would also account for the highly fragmented state of the human and animal bone in the fill, for the mixture of material from different Neolithic periods, and for the heterogeneity of the earlier radiocarbon measurements taken from that matrix.

Where the excavation sits on the hill - the basis for reading the fill as hillwash.

Clare, quoted: "a fully-fledged settlement with permanent occupation. It's changed our whole understanding of the site."

The wider picture

Göbekli Tepe is not unique 12:59–13:52. Turkish archaeologists have found at least a dozen similar hilltop sites with T-shaped pillars and oval enclosures — Karahan Tepe among them — concentrated in southern Turkey between the upper Balikh and upper Khabur, and dating to about the same period. They have been given the collective name Taş Tepeler, Stone Hills. On the new reading these are large permanent settlements with water cisterns, built by hunter-gatherers.

Between the two halves she puts her own reasoning about nomadism 10:15–12:59, which is common sense rather than evidence: a band contains children, pregnant women and the old as well as hunters, and also needs people to gather, cook, make clothes and tools and care for the sick. They had no draft animals and no wheels, so everything kept had to be carried. Where would you process and store a large kill or a big harvest of wild cereals if you were always moving? Finding a place with fresh water, wild grain and year-round game and then staying is the sensible thing to do.

Her own proposal

Then the carvings 13:52–20:21. Most are animals, naturalistic and matching the fauna of the period: boars, snakes, foxes, wildcats, aurochs, gazelles, birds, scorpions, spiders. Many are shown with big fangs. Schmidt called the site a Stone Age zoo. Some pillars are anthropomorphic, with arms, hands, belts and clothing; one appears to have a fox at its elbow. Some researchers argue the iconography is exclusively male and the enclosures were therefore for male hunters.

That the pillars are figures, not supports.

Her hypothesis is that the enclosures are a hall of fame: each pillar commemorating a hunting trip, a notable kill, or a great hunter, with the animals recorded as a storyboard. Her analogy is modern trophy display — the mounted heads in traditional hunting camps, and the Explorers Club in New York, whose members have included a former US president, princes and an astronaut, and whose rooms are hung with cheetahs, deer heads, a tiger, a lion, a polar bear, elephant tusks. "Hey their polar bear and lion show their fangs too." The explorers wanted to show how ferocious the animals were, and so what it took to overcome them. Human nature, she says, is very consistent.

The modern comparison her own proposal rests on.

She allows a ritual function alongside it — hunters praying to hunting gods for game and a safe return, which she finds more natural than praying to constellations.

Her objections to the astronomical readings 18:33–19:31: she has not seen a satisfactory pairing of the carvings with constellations in their sky positions; she cannot see a comet on Pillar 43; the comet proposal says nothing about the carvings on the pillar's other three sides; the buildings may originally have had roofs, which would rule out observatories; and a hunter-gatherer community investing that much effort in observatories that do not represent what they do seems counter-intuitive to her.

She ends by asking whether, in this new picture, the reliefs on Pillar 43 are bags at all — and defers that to another video.

What you can check yourself

  • The new excavation results are published and the images are the excavators'. Her frames include an overhead photograph of round-oval structures around a hearth, and a set of bone tools from the same sounding, photographed against black with a 5 cm scale.
  • The beer claim has a named source on screen — a post of 24 April 2016 by Oliver Dietrich, headed Out for a beer at the dawn of agriculture, with a limestone trough captioned "tentative evidence for oxalate" and credited to the DAI. Both the date and the caption can be read off the frame.
  • The comet claim's source is also on screen, and it is a news report: Phys.org, 24 April 2017, by Bob Yirka, headed Ancient stone pillars offer clues of comet strike that changed human history.
  • Pillar 43 is photographed in situ and the three bag shapes are countable: a semicircular top over a rectangular body, in a row along the upper edge, each with a small quadruped above it.
  • The anthropomorphic pillars are unambiguous. Two of her frames show arms bent at the elbow running down the pillar sides, hands with long fingers meeting over a belt, and a fox-pelt element hanging from it.
  • Schmidt's reason for the temple reading is a factual claim — no hearth, no domesticates in 25 years — and so is the new one. Both are checkable in the excavation literature.
  • Taş Tepeler is a live research programme and the sites are named.

What the video does not settle

  • The beer evidence is weaker than the narration. The caption she displays says tentative evidence for oxalate. The narration says the residue "shows signs of fermentation which points to ancient beer brewing". Oxalate is consistent with cereal processing and is not the same thing as demonstrated fermentation, and the word "tentative" is on screen and not spoken.
  • A news report stands in for the comet paper. The Phys.org piece is shown, the paper is not named, and the argument she rejects is therefore rejected in its journalistic summary.
  • The two hearth findings are about different layers and that is not drawn out. Schmidt found no hearths in the enclosures; Clare's trenches went beneath the enclosure floors, to bedrock, into an earlier phase. Her own account contains that distinction and does not make it, which leaves the reversal looking sharper than it is.
  • "Rewrite everything" is the title, not the finding. What the new work changes is whether people lived at the site and whether the fill was deliberate. It does not change the dates, the pillars, the carvings, or the question the video is nominally about.
  • The hall-of-fame proposal explains less than it appears to. It accounts for the animals. It does not account for the T-shape, the arms and belts that make the pillars figures rather than supports, the burial, the deliberate arrangement of the enclosures, or the bag shapes on Pillar 43 — which is where the video started.
  • The Explorers Club analogy is an analogy. "Human nature is very consistent" is the whole of the argument connecting a New York club to a Neolithic enclosure, and no test is proposed that would distinguish a trophy hall from a shrine.
  • "I don't see an obvious comet indication on that pillar, do you?" is an appeal, not an argument. The stronger objection in the same passage — that the proposal ignores the carvings on the other three sides — is made once and not developed.
  • The roofs are asserted. "These buildings might have had roofs originally" is offered as a reason the observatory reading fails, with nothing behind it.
  • The exclusively-male iconography claim is attributed to nobody. "Some researchers argue" is all there is, and it carries the hunters' club reading.
  • The nomadism section is reasoning from plausibility. It is sensible and it is not evidence, and it sits in a video whose whole strength is that it goes to the excavation data.
  • The Taş Tepeler distribution is described and not mapped. A dozen sites between two named rivers, and the map shown is a general map of Turkey with one dot on it.
  • Nothing here touches the handbags. Twenty minutes of excavation reporting, and the question the video opens with is handed to the next one.

What we have not checked

  • No excavation report or paper has been read. The keyhole trenches, the cistern, the hearth sequence, the PPNA dates of 9,500–8,700 BC, the stone vessels, grinding tools and troughs, the human burial and the revised view of the fill are all as she reports them.
  • The Edinburgh comet paper is not named in the video and has not been identified here. What is shown is a Phys.org news report of 24 April 2017 by Bob Yirka.
  • The Dietrich post is identified by date, author and headline but has not been retrieved, and the oxalate result has not been traced to a laboratory report.
  • The Clare quotation is not sourced to an interview, paper or press release.
  • Andrew Collins' Deneb argument is described from the narration only.
  • The claim that the iconography is exclusively male is attributed to unnamed researchers and has not been checked; it is contested in the literature and should not be repeated as settled.
  • Schmidt's 25 years without a hearth or a domesticate is as stated.
  • The figures are unverified: 12,000 years old, pillars at 5.5 m, over twenty enclosures, 2,000 years of use, at least a dozen Taş Tepeler sites.
  • The el-Khiam, Helwan and Aswad point types are named as heard and the spellings have not been checked.
  • The Explorers Club photographs are uncredited, and the room shown is not identified beyond her description.
