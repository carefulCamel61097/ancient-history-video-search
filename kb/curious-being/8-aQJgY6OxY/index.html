Could the Giza Pyramids and Sphinx Be Much Older Than Believed?

Tina · 21 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-23

datingconvergencewhywater-erosion-and-weatheringpyramid-constructionpaleoclimate

This is the sequel to her argument that the Sphinx enclosure was a stormwater catch basin, and it asks one question: if the Giza monuments were built in a wetter climate than today's, which wetter climate?

The starting position is recapped rather than re-argued 00:00–01:42. Deep vertical water erosion on the Sphinx enclosure walls, the Sphinx body, the Sphinx Temple and the tomb of Khentkawes; Giza in desert climate for the last 6,000 years; therefore a humid phase before dynastic Egypt. She also carries over the claim that the pyramid casings and core stones are artificial concrete-like limestone containing nanoparticles, attributed to multiple research teams.

Her scenario, also from the earlier video 01:42–02:46: the basin and pyramids made in a relatively humid era; climate turning dry and windy; wind damage to the lower portions of structures repaired by the builders with granite and basalt casings; that restoration left unfinished when the civilisation collapsed; the site rediscovered and reoccupied by Egyptians much later.

To place that in time she needs a period that goes from humid to hyper-arid near Giza — and first, a reading of how much rain the erosion actually required.

The badlands inversion

This is the original argument in the video, and it cuts against the standard alternative position 03:24–05:12.

The assumption everyone makes is that grooves that deep mean frequent, heavy rain. She points instead at badlands — dry terrain where soft sedimentary rock and clay-rich soil have been extensively eroded — and puts photographs of them beside Giza in two side-by-side panels: the Sphinx enclosure wall against a badland cone, and the tomb of Khentkawes against a badland face.

The argument the video turns on, and the evidence for it is visual: whether Giza's erosion looks like the erosion of semi-arid ground.

Her point about where gullies form: gully erosion in rock is most prevalent in semi-arid areas, not in truly humid or hyper-arid ones, and the reason is vegetation and sediment.

  • In humid areas, more evenly distributed rainfall supports vegetation, which mitigates erosion.
  • In very arid deserts the sunken basin fills with sediment, so the walls are not exposed to erode at all.
  • In between — bare rock, minimal plant cover, and sporadic intense storms — runoff moves fast over naked slopes and cuts rills and gullies.

So her conclusion is that the enclosure was cut in a semi-arid climate with intense seasonal rainfall, which is also the climate that would make a large drainage basin worth building.

Why the Green Sahara is the wrong answer

The obvious candidate window is the last African Humid Period, the "Green Sahara", 15,000 to 5,500 years ago, when the Sahara had grasslands, lakes and rivers. She rejects it on geography 07:33–08:07: the monsoon precipitation that produced it did not reach the Giza region. Giza sits by the Nile delta on the edge of the eastern Mediterranean climate zone.

Why the obvious wet window is ruled out: the rain that greened the Sahara came from the south and stopped short of Giza.

That leads to her methodological point, which she then applies consistently 08:07–09:03: local climate can diverge sharply from the regional trend. Her example is the Younger Dryas, a rapid cooling around 13,000 years ago that was dry in many regions but appears to have been cold and wet across much of the Mediterranean, possibly under ice-sheet influence — a regional pattern overruling the global signal. So a Giza date cannot be read off large-scale climate curves; it has to come from data near the Nile delta.

The cores

Sediment core MS27PT is the closest available to the Nile delta, and she takes it as the best proxy for Giza's paleoclimate 09:03–10:36. Citing a 2023 paper working from it, covering the period since the last glacial 18,000 years ago:

  • Heinrich Stadial 1, long regarded as uniformly cold and dry across North America, Eurasia and North Africa, resolves in the delta into two phases — dry between 17,500 and 16,000 years ago, then wet between 16,000 and 14,500.
  • Speleothem data from Soreq Cave, Israel shows a progressive increase in precipitation over the same interval.
  • Then roughly 3,000 relatively dry years, followed by a much wetter climate from 11,500 to 6,000 years ago.

Rather than rest on one paper she compares five 10:36–12:32: three on Nile delta and eastern Mediterranean sedimentation from drilled cores including MS27PT, two comparing Soreq speleothem oxygen isotopes against eastern Mediterranean cores, and one using rock varnish and Dead Sea lake levels. Her summary is that they broadly agree, with dating deviating a little, and she notes one outlier — a 2020 paper showing an extra brief wet phase — which she sets aside.

She names none of the five in the narration, but the chart prints a DOI beside every one of them, so each record on it can be taken back to its source. The rows are labelled 2023, 2020, 2019, 2014 and "2013, dead sea"; the last is the shortest, running out at about 18 ka with an earlier stretch marked only with a question mark.

The synthesis the date rests on - and the only place in this video where its sources can be looked up.

Three humid windows, then. And she makes a point that matters for her own argument: the two earlier ones were cold, so less humid than the Holocene one — which makes them a better fit for the semi-arid conditions her badlands reasoning requires, not a worse one.

Eliminating the most recent window

The 11,000–6,000 window is ruled out on an argument from absence 13:07–14:35. Predynastic Egyptians arrived in the Nile valley about 6,000 years ago. If the pyramid civilisation was still declining then, the two would have overlapped for centuries — and there would be folklore, legend, historical record or archaeology attesting to it. There is none. She takes that as ruling the window out, and as an argument that the builders must be far enough back for the evidence to have decayed, eroded and been buried.

Both earlier windows survive that test, since in either case the civilisation ended more than 5,000 years before the predynastic Egyptians arrived.

The bottleneck

To choose between the two, she introduces human genetics 15:35–19:16. Her reasoning is that a civilisation capable of this work must have been large and long-developing, so its end should leave a mark in the genome.

Citing a paper from the 1000 Genomes Project, she states that in the entire existence of anatomically modern humans there was only one worldwide population decrease severe enough to qualify as the end of an advanced civilisation: between 20,000 and 15,000 years ago, when all populations outside Africa were severely reduced and African populations also dropped, less sharply.

The genetics her date depends on, in the paper's own words - including the figures the narration passes over.

The passage she highlights carries figures she does not read out. The paper describes modelling effective population size by a pairwise sequentially Markovian coalescent method, and gives the bottleneck as Ne below 1,500 for European, Asian and American populations and Ne above 4,250 for African ones over the same window — followed, in the next sentence, by extremely rapid population growth outside Africa.

That bottleneck follows the humid phase inside the Last Glacial Maximum and coincides with the 4,000-year Northern Hemisphere drought covering HS1's first phase. She argues the Northern Hemisphere's importance from present-day figures — 68% of Earth's landmass, about 87% of today's population, and 88%, 45% and 91% of world corn, soybean and wheat production in the 2018–2022 crop years — and proposes that a millennia-long drought there would cost food and water, worsen living conditions, and spawn pandemics, territorial conflict, famine and war.

She adds a second genetic strand: between 19,000 and 14,000 years ago there is evidence of major population shifts in Europe and East Asia, with the people who lived there from 40–45,000 years ago through the glacial replaced by new groups who are the ancestors of present Europeans and East Asians. She argues the bottleneck and the replacement occurring consecutively around HS1 is not coincidence.

Where she lands

The pyramids built in the roughly two-thousand-year humid window inside the Last Glacial Maximum, 22,500 to 20,500 years ago, when average temperature was about 7 °C lower than now 19:16–20:35. The sunken Sphinx enclosure cut as a catch basin for a mining operation, in semi-arid conditions with seasonal storms; cold suppressing plant cover so the walls stayed bare and the runoff cut into them. Then hyper-aridity, the civilisation failing in the four-millennia drought and ending around 15,000 years ago, and Giza repopulated some 9,000 years later by ancient Egyptians, by which time the traces were buried and gone.

What you can check yourself

  • The badlands comparison is visual and available. Photographs of badland gully erosion and of the Sphinx enclosure walls can be put side by side by anyone.
  • Where gully erosion forms is standard geomorphology, and the reasoning about vegetation cover and sediment infill is checkable against that literature rather than against her.
  • The rainfall classifications and Giza's modern figure are published, ordinary numbers.
  • The Green Sahara's northern extent is mapped in the paleoclimate literature; whether the monsoon reached the Nile delta is a question with an existing answer.
  • The core is named. MS27PT is a real, repeatedly published record, and the two-phase reading of Heinrich Stadial 1 in the delta can be checked in the source.
  • Soreq Cave's speleothem record is one of the best-known paleoclimate archives in the region.
  • Every source behind the timeline is cited by DOI on the chart itself, so the synthesis is auditable in fact and not just in principle — and she marks the outlier she set aside rather than dropping it silently.
  • The genetics passage is shown as a screenshot of the paper, so its actual wording and its effective-population-size figures can be read directly off the frame.

What the video does not settle

  • The strongest part is the erosion argument, and it undermines the case it is used in. If gullies of this kind form in semi-arid conditions with sporadic storms, then the Sphinx enclosure no longer requires a markedly wet climate — and the erosion stops being strong evidence for great age at all. She uses it to select which early window, but the same reasoning weakens the premise that any pre-dynastic window is needed. That consequence is not addressed.
  • The papers are cited but never characterised. Each row of the timeline carries a DOI, which is more than most of this corpus offers — but no author, title or scope is given, and the video does not say which parts of each record it is reading, how the five were reconciled where they disagree, or on what basis the 2020 record was set aside as an outlier rather than treated as evidence.
  • The climate data constrains nothing about construction. Establishing that Giza was semi-arid 22,500 years ago does not connect that period to the monuments. The link is that the erosion needs such a climate, and it holds equally for all three windows on her own account.
  • The elimination of the recent window is an argument from absence that works against her too. If a civilisation ending 6,000 years ago would have left legends and archaeology, the burden is on why one ending 15,000 years ago left megalithic structures still standing but no other trace whatever. The reasoning selects for the least checkable option.
  • The bottleneck is a real finding put to an unsupported use, and her own screenshot shows why. The highlighted passage gives the bottleneck as effective population sizes below 1,500 outside Africa and above 4,250 within it, derived from coalescent modelling of genomes rather than from any count of people. Her argument requires the loss of a large, long-developed industrial population; the figures on screen are the modelled ancestral parameters for humanity as a whole over the last glacial. Neither number is mentioned, and nothing in the data distinguishes a climatic contraction of hunter-gatherers from a civilisation's collapse.
  • The modern agricultural statistics do not apply. Corn, soybean and wheat production shares for 2018–2022, and today's population distribution, describe an agricultural world. They are used to argue about the consequences of drought roughly 15,000 years before agriculture.
  • The 7 °C temperature drop cuts against the scenario. A civilisation is placed at Giza during the coldest interval of the last glacial, and the video does not address what that implies for its food base or for the mining operation the site is supposed to have served.
  • The casting claim is imported and load-bearing. "Multiple teams of researchers" and "nanoparticles" appear in the first minute as established, and no study is named in this video. The nanoparticles are the submicron silica spheres reported by Barsoum's group, cited in Pyramid Stones: The Scientific Evidence They're Hiding.

See also

  • Pyramid Stones: The Scientific Evidence They're Hiding — the "multiple teams of researchers" and the nanoparticles of the opening minute, named.
  • The Sphinx Temple's Secret — the immediately preceding video, whose catch-basin conclusion this one takes as its premise, and whose 1926 excavation photographs are the more checkable half of the pair.
  • Questioning the Luminescence Dating Result of the Giza Pyramid — three years earlier, arguing from published surface dating that Menkaure's casing dates to around 3450 BC. That result is not mentioned here, and no reading of it reaches 22,500 years ago.
  • Great Pyramids' Radiocarbon Dating — the companion argument that dynastic Egyptians found the pyramids rather than built them, made from mortar rather than climate.
  • Was an Advanced Civilization Wiped Out by a 12,800-Year-old Comet at Younger Dryas? — the Younger Dryas as an impact rather than a climate window, tested as the cause of the collapse and rejected.
  • Carolina Bays Mystery — where she gives the builders a timeline: begun around 40,000 years ago, at their height about 28,000, gone between 20,000 and 15,000. The 22,500-year figure argued for here falls inside that window, which is one of the few places two of her videos can be checked against each other for consistency.

What we have not checked

  • No paper has been located or read. Five are identified by DOI on the timeline chart — 10.5194/cp-2023-23, 10.1016/j.quascirev.2020.106306, 10.3390/quat2020016, 10.1007/s10113-014-0588-x and 10.1002/grl.50492 — as read off the frame; none has been opened, and no author or title is given for any of them. The genetics paper is shown only as an untitled page of text.
  • Whether the 2023 paper cited in the narration for MS27PT is the same as the 2023 row on the chart has not been confirmed.
  • The figures are as she states them and have not been traced: the phase boundaries at 22,500, 20,500, 16,000, 14,500, 11,000 and 6,000 years ago; HS1 at 18–14.7 ka; the LGM at 26–19 ka; Younger Dryas at ~13 ka; the Green Sahara at 15,000–5,500 BP; the 7 °C temperature drop; the 1 inch of annual rainfall at Giza.
  • The rainfall classification bands are given without a source.
  • The characterisation of the Younger Dryas as cold and wet in the Mediterranean is attributed to "multiple studies" and has not been checked.
  • The population replacement in Europe and East Asia between 19,000 and 14,000 years ago is stated without citation.
  • The 2018–2022 crop production shares and the 68% landmass and 87% population figures are as given and have not been verified.
  • The claim that the pyramid casings and core stones are artificial limestone containing nanoparticles, attributed to multiple research teams, is carried over from earlier videos. The underlying studies are identified in Pyramid Stones: The Scientific Evidence They're Hiding; none of them has been read, and the nanospheres are reported there as a finding in one sample.
  • The tomb of Khentkawes and its second sphinx are named in the recap and their erosion has not been examined here.
