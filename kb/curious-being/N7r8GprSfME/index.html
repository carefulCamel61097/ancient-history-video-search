Carolina Bays Mystery: What Created These Giant Prehistoric Craters?

The Carolina Bays are shallow oval depressions scattered along the American Atlantic coastal plain from New Jersey to northern Florida, best known and best developed in the Carolinas. At ground level they are almost impossible to see, and they were noticed only once people started flying over them. A 1952 report put the count above 500,000 00:00–00:40.

What the bays are, and why nobody saw them from the ground.

Their characteristics are the evidence, and she lists them carefully 00:36–02:27:

  • generally elliptical, with many long axes running north-west to south-east
  • from a few hundred metres to 12 km long — 650 ft to 7½ miles
  • the average long-axis trend varies by region, from N16°W in Georgia to N64°W in Virginia
  • shallow, often less than 3 m deep, with slightly raised sandy rims

And a correction of her own, dropped in passing and doing real work later:

By the way, their long axes do not meet at the same conjunction point, as others have stated.

Nothing on screen shows this. The passage runs over a satellite view of Woods Bay State Park in South Carolina and a low aerial photograph, neither of which carries an axis, a bearing or a north arrow.

What a bay looks like without LiDAR - the reason nobody noticed them.

The impact case, and why she rejects it

Since the 1930s the bays have been read as impact craters from a meteor shower or a fragmented comet, on the strength of the shared orientation and the clustering. The immediate problem is that no extraterrestrial material has been found in the soil 02:27–02:48.

The version still in circulation is Antonio Zamora's glacier-ice impact hypothesis: that the bays are secondary impacts, made by chunks of terrestrial ice thrown out when a meteor struck the Laurentide ice sheet 02:48–03:12.

Her objections are a sequence and each one stands on its own 03:12–06:40:

They are not all the same age. Bay sediments were sampled as early as the 1930s using pollen, which already showed at least one glacial cycle had passed. Radiocarbon followed, then optically stimulated luminescence, which measures time since quartz sand last saw daylight. Some bays are over 100,000 years old; some may be 10,000 or less. Most published OSL dates fall between 41,000 and 11,000 years ago. A single event does not produce a 30,000-year spread.

One bay's rims record it moving. A 2016 study published dates from the successive sand rims of Herndon Bay in North Carolina — the name is on screen even though the auto-transcript loses it — giving 36,700, 29,600 and 27,200 years ago, which she notes track the rapid temperature oscillations of the period. The rims nearer the present basin are the younger ones, so the bay migrated east to west over time. The published figure carries four numbered sample points along a ground-penetrating-radar transect; she labels three of them.

Impact craters do not migrate while keeping their impact depression shapes.

The dated rim sequence: the bay moved.

Impact craters are round. Despite almost every incoming object striking at an angle, only about 5% of the crater record on Mars, the Moon and Venus is elliptical. Elliptical craters need a very shallow angle of incidence — a 2013 paper puts the threshold at around 10–20°. And even then, a shallow-angle impact leaves a tail on one side, which the bays do not have.

What a shallow-angle impact actually produces.

The numbers are absurd. The Earth Impact Database as of 2017 lists 190 confirmed craters, large and small, on the whole planet. There are more than 500,000 Carolina Bays.

What are the odds that these many depressions were caused by meteors or glacier fragments, in particular shallow angles, and chopped nowhere else but the east coast of America? I think exceedingly unlikely.

What the geologists say, and why she agrees

Most geologists today read the bays as relics of thermokarst lakes — bodies of water formed when permafrost thaws, which enlarge, then dry out and leave a depression when the thawing continues. They occur in Arctic and subarctic lowlands: the northern Alaskan coastal plain, the Canadian Arctic, northern Eurasia and Siberia, and also in mountains such as the Himalayas and the Alps 06:43–07:50.

The landform she concludes the bays are relics of.

The shared features are the argument 07:50–09:15. Thermokarst lakes occur in great numbers, vary in size, sit on coastal plains, and are shallow — under 4 m, about the same as the bays. Lakes in one area tend to share a long-axis orientation and an elongated oval shape. In Alaska the elongation runs perpendicular to the prevailing wind. In water that shallow, wind and waves reshape the lake and its beach rim, wind stress can make the whole lake migrate, and repeated freeze–thaw cycles with steady wind build up multiple sandy rims.

Every one of those matches something observed in the bays.

The objection she takes seriously

The bays look like more perfect ellipses than thermokarst lakes do. She gives three answers 09:15–10:25, and the third is the best thing in the video.

First, many bays are not refined ovals either — those in Maryland, Delaware and New Jersey are more bell-shaped, and published figures often draw dashed ellipses over the photographs, which flatters them. Second, parts of Siberia contain near-perfect smooth oval lakes that rival the best bays.

Third:

Please keep in mind that we are comparing low-resolution satellite photos of the thermokarst lakes with the LiDAR images of Carolina Bays, which is not really a fair comparison.

LiDAR builds ground-elevation models with a vertical accuracy of 10 cm. Put both on aerial photography at the same scale, she says, and the regularities are comparable.

The methodological catch: the two things being compared are not photographed the same way.

She then argues against her own explanation as well 10:25–11:15. Scanning Arctic regions on Google and Bing Maps, she finds that even within one part of northern Siberia, nearby groups of shallow lakes do not always share shapes, ellipticities or long-axis angles — despite having the same prevailing wind. Her conclusion is that wind matters a great deal but is not the only thing: topography, drainage, river systems, geology, soil materials and antecedent conditions all contribute, and that variety is why the bays are mixed too.

Where conditions are right, near-flawless ovals form. So: the bays are plausibly thermokarst relics that went through freeze–thaw cycles for tens of thousands of years.

The turn

That conclusion has a consequence, and the rest of the video is about it 11:36–12:10. Thermokarst lakes only form in ice-rich permafrost. If the Carolina Bays are thermokarst relics, permafrost once reached Georgia and northern Florida.

She builds the case for a much colder North America 12:10–13:12. A map of simulated near-surface air temperatures during Marine Isotope Stage 3, roughly 60,000–30,000 years ago, is read as showing North America as the coldest continent of the period — colder than the Arctic and Greenland. Meanwhile most of northern Russia, Siberia, northern Alaska and the Yukon were never fully glaciated during the last glacial period, which is demonstrated by the quantity of Ice Age animal and plant remains found there.

The map's own title and caption, printed on it, say something narrower than the narration does. It reads ANN T2m (MIS3 − PI) and Simulated MIS3 minus PI annual mean near-surface air temperature, with a mean of −2.88 °C. It is an anomaly field: how much colder each region was than today, not how cold it was.

How cold North America was, and what the map actually plots.

Why did glaciers reach so far south in North America but eastern Siberia remained ice free?

The wind changed 13:12–14:00. Today North America has westerlies. Between about 36,000 and 14,000 years ago the prevailing wind in mid-latitude America was the exact reverse — easterly. She attributes the finding to Dartmouth researchers in 2007, working from wood samples, who read it as direct evidence that the polar easterlies — the cold dry winds that circle the poles — had expanded to latitudes as low as 40°N. What is on screen is not the study but a news article reporting it: Serious Climate Change: Winds Blew in Reverse During Last Ice Age, by Andrea Thompson, 23 January 2007.

The wind reversal, and where the claim comes from.

Two things then click into place for her 14:00–16:40. The migrating bay moved more than 600 m east to west in 10,000 years, which is the direction polar easterlies would push it, at latitude 35°N. And elongated thermokarst lakes lie perpendicular to the prevailing wind, so easterlies from the north-east would produce ovals with long axes running north-west to south-east — which is what most bays have.

Then a dune argument. Bay rims are typically wider on the east side and narrower on the west. Barchan dunes are arc-shaped with the wider, gentler slope facing the wind; parabolic dunes look similar but have the horns pointing the other way relative to the wind. Bay rims resemble barchans with the gentle side facing east — pointing again to an easterly wind, and to conditions of flat ground, sparse vegetation, limited sand and steady unidirectional wind.

The dune type the bay rims are said to resemble.

True polar wander

Modern polar easterlies sit between 60° and 90° latitude. If they reached 40°N in North America during the last glacial period, she argues, the North Pole was somewhere else 16:40–18:30.

True polar wander is the movement of the geographic poles relative to the mantle and crust, as the planet rearranges to align its maximum moment of inertia with its rotation axis. She notes that some scientists have connected it with ice ages: a pole shifted into a region gives that region much less direct sunlight, and the glacial Arctic Circle would have been larger than the interglacial one.

What true polar wander means.

If the Arctic Circle had expanded over the south-east of America, then everything lines up — the glaciation of North America, the mid-latitude polar easterlies, and permafrost landforms along the Atlantic coastal plain.

Her summary of the climate 18:30–19:11: North America was largely unlivable through most of the last glacial period, roughly 100,000 to 21,000 years ago — thick glaciers in the north, permafrost patches as far south as northern Florida, and an Arctic-like climate, cold, dry and windy, like today's northern Alaska and Siberia.

What all of it was for

The last section states the point the video was built toward 19:11–21:41. She holds that the precise megalithic sites — Giza, Sacsayhuamán, the Longyou caves, Yangshan Quarry — were made by a previous globe-spanning industrial culture, and gives its timeline: begun by Paleolithic humans around 40,000 years ago, at its height around 28,000 years ago, collapsed and gone between 20,000 and 15,000 years before present.

Here is the issue. The recognised precise megalithic sites are in North Africa, Europe, Middle East, Asia and South America, but none have ever been found in North America. Why?

The absence the whole video was built to explain.

Her answer is the climate she has just reconstructed. North America was inside the Arctic Circle, extremely cold and uninhabitable, with permafrost down to the southern Atlantic coastline. That is why no megalithic sites were built there — and the window, 40,000 to 15,000 years ago, matches her proposed rise and fall.

She closes with a flat statement about one counter-example, and a promise she kept 21:41–21:53:

Some argue that the Montana megaliths is a man-made site, which I disagree with. I may do a video on this topic if you guys are interested.

What you can check yourself

  • The bays are visible from the air and in public LiDAR. Anyone can look at the elevation data for the Carolina coastal plain and see them. This is the rare claim in the corpus where the primary evidence is free, online and unambiguous.
  • The measurements are specific and published: over 500,000 bays from a 1952 report, lengths of 650 ft to 7½ miles, depths under 3 m, and long-axis trends from N16°W in Georgia to N64°W in Virginia.
  • The "no conjunction point" claim is testable with the numbers she just gave. If the axis trend varies by nearly 50° across the range, the axes cannot converge on one origin, and that is the whole basis of the meteor-shower geometry.
  • The three rim dates are a published result — 36,700, 29,600 and 27,200 years ago from one bay's successive rims, from a 2016 study, and the direction of migration follows from which rim is youngest.
  • The OSL range, 41,000 to 11,000 years, is a published spread and is the single most damaging fact for a single-event explanation.
  • The crater statistics are checkable: about 5% of craters on Mars, the Moon and Venus are elliptical; the 2013 threshold-angle paper gives roughly 10–20°; the Earth Impact Database listed 190 confirmed craters in 2017.
  • The tail is a shape you can look for. Shallow-angle impact craters have one; the bays do not.
  • Thermokarst lakes exist now and are photographed constantly. Northern Alaska, the Canadian Arctic and Siberia are on every satellite map, and her like-for-like comparison can be repeated by anyone in a browser.
  • The dune types are standard geomorphology. Which way a barchan's horns point relative to the wind, and how that differs from a parabolic dune, is textbook material.
  • The temperature map says what it is on its own face. The title ANN T2m (MIS3 − PI) and the caption beneath it are legible in the video, and a reader can decide for themselves whether an anomaly field supports the sentence spoken over it.

What the video does not settle

  • The video has two halves and only the first is evidence-led. Everything up to the thermokarst conclusion is argued from dates, shapes, counts and comparisons. Everything after it is a chain of inferences in which each link is used as a premise for the next: bays are thermokarst, so permafrost reached Florida, so North America was Arctic, so the pole moved, so no one could build there, so the absence of megaliths is explained. No step is tested once the chain starts.
  • True polar wander is invoked without a rate. It is a real geophysical process; whether it can move the pole far enough within tens of thousands of years is the entire question, and no figure, paper or mechanism is offered. The proposal is made from the wind evidence backwards.
  • The temperature map is an anomaly field and is read as absolute temperature. Its printed title says MIS3 minus PI. A −20 °C anomaly over the Great Lakes means that region cooled more than anywhere else did; it does not mean it was colder than the Arctic, which is the claim made over it. The deepest blue also sits exactly where the Laurentide ice sheet stood, so the figure may be showing the effect she is treating as the cause.
  • No conventional explanation of the glaciation asymmetry is stated before it is replaced. That North America glaciated further south than Siberia is presented as unexplained. Whether glaciologists have an account of it — moisture supply, ice-sheet nucleation, elevation — is not mentioned, so the reader cannot judge whether a pole shift is needed.
  • The polar easterly inference runs the wrong way. Expanded polar cells are a documented feature of glacial climate models with the pole where it is. That the wind belt moved is not by itself evidence that the pole moved, and the alternative is never weighed.
  • The migrating bay is one bay. The 600 m of east-to-west movement over 10,000 years is a single case, and it carries the wind-direction argument, the dune argument and the pole argument.
  • Absence of evidence is converted into evidence. "No megalithic sites in North America" is explained by a hypothesis constructed to explain it, and there is no observation that could count against it: if a site is found, the climate reconstruction changes; if none is, the reconstruction is confirmed.
  • The megalithic timeline appears from nowhere here. Begun 40,000 years ago, peak 28,000, collapse 20,000–15,000 — three specific figures, presented as her proposal, with the supporting argument entirely delegated to other videos.
  • The absence itself is asserted rather than examined. Whether North America has monumental stone construction, and what the criteria are for a site counting as "precise megalithic", is never stated. The one North American candidate she knows of is dismissed in a sentence and postponed.
  • The permafrost conclusion rests on a modern analogy. Thermokarst lakes form in ice-rich permafrost today. That a landform resembling them existed at 33°N in the Pleistocene is her premise for the climate reconstruction, and whether comparable basins can form by other means in other conditions is not asked.
  • Not one source is named, and the one shown is a news article. A 1952 report, a 2016 study, a 2013 paper, the Earth Impact Database — all by year only, with no author, title or journal in twenty-three minutes. The 2007 wind result, which the entire pole-shift argument depends on, appears on screen as a popular science news page by a staff writer, and the underlying study is named nowhere.
  • The best figures in the video are uncredited. The crater-angle simulation, the Herndon Bay rim rendering and the MIS3 map are all published scientific figures, and none of them carries a source on screen.
  • The Siberian comparison is her own eyeballing. She says so — scanning Google and Bing Maps — and the observation about neighbouring lake groups differing under the same wind is presented as a personal finding, not a measurement.
  • The 2007 wind result is described, not shown in its own terms. What the wood samples were, how a wind direction is recovered from them, and over what area, is not explained.
