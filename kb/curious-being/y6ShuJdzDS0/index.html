Prehistoric Mining: What was Mined on the Giza Plateau

Tina · 20 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-23

whyscalehowconvergencepyramid-functionpyramid-constructionquarries

Two questions carried over from the shaft videos 00:00–01:07: if the Giza shafts were a mine, what was being mined, and did it produce enough waste to build the pyramids?

The scale to be accounted for: the Great Pyramid at an estimated 2.3 million blocks and 6 million tons, and the three great pyramids together over 13 million tons and 4 million blocks.

The plateau is not its original shape

The strongest section, and it rests on published survey rather than on analogy 01:34–03:21.

Today's Giza plateau is man-made ground — the top layer of bedrock has been cut away. Citing a 2020 paper in Heritage Science, she reports:

  • The west side of the Great Pyramid stands about 110 m above the Nile.
  • The plateau was geologically continuous with Mokattam Hill across the river, which stands 200 m above the Nile — so Giza's original top may have been close to 90 m higher than today's.
  • Working instead from the summed mass of the pyramids, the paper's researchers propose the area was at least 50 m higher.

The figure she puts on screen is the paper's own cross-section of the valley, and its numbers are given above sea level rather than above the river: Pyramid Plateau +90, the Nile at about +20, Mokattam Hill +140 rising to +200. Those are not the numbers in the narration, and the entry records both rather than choosing between them.

And the pyramids are not built entirely of stacked blocks. Original rock hills survive inside two of them: about 11.5% of Khafre's volume and about 23% of Khufu's are the hill they stand on. She takes that as direct proof the plateau was higher.

That the pyramids are not purely stacked: natural rock continues up into them from the plateau they stand on.

The question that follows

Which produces the question the whole video turns on 03:21–04:36.

Cutting 50 m off a rock plateau is an enormous undertaking. If the goal was to build pyramids, why do it that way at all? Why not carve the pyramids out of the bedrock in place and add blocks on top to finish the shape — less labour, and a structure with a solid rock lower body would be sounder than one of stacked blocks. Instead the material was removed and then piled back up.

Her answer: the only comparable modern activity is mining. In mining, overburden is the material lying above an ore body, removed in open-pit or surface working and typically not contaminated. Her proposal is that the plateau's top layer was overburden, stripped to reach the ore, and that some Giza structures were built from it. Mines commonly have both surface and underground portions.

So the sequence she proposes 05:53–06:25: start on the hilltop and work down, crush and extract ore on site, pile waste as you go, and build storage piles from what is left — which are the pyramids. She adds that the surviving rock hills would then be doing a job: anchors and natural foundations for structures that otherwise have none.

The 0.014%

The argument she has been building across three videos, stated in one figure 08:25–09:36.

The Great Pyramid is the only one with connected chambers above and below ground. The others are almost entirely solid, sitting on top of bedrock chambers. And even in the Great Pyramid, the openings come to about 0.014% of its total volume — a figure she attributes on screen to an 1818 report in the Quarterly Review, which put the passages and chambers at one 7,400th of the whole.

From a construction standpoint, she argues, no one uses that much material to enclose that little usable space — unless packing in as much material as possible was the point. The one structure type she can think of that fits: mine waste storage.

The figure that reframes every argument about what the Great Pyramid is for: the chambers are a rounding error in the building.

She refers back to six similarities she drew in an earlier video between pyramids and dry-stack waste rock and tailings mounds, listing four here: close in shape and size; mostly solid with no foundations; a less permeable, buttress-like exterior layer; and located near complex underground systems. And she notes that if the blocks are a form of concrete, as she says multiple papers on material analysis have shown, then the blocks themselves could be cemented mine waste.

The ore

Her case that it was iron 10:21–15:03, assembled from four things:

1. Inside the Osiris Shaft — dark veins on the second level, and a yellow-orange rock tunnel on the third, which she reads as iron oxidation. 2. Near Khafre's funerary temple and causeway — areas of dark red and orange colouration. Russian researchers who noted these first thought the colour might be the remains of rusted iron or steel equipment. The stained areas extend into an underground space carrying the same machine marks seen in the Osiris Shaft and in the Great Pyramid's underground chambers; that space was covered over with blocks and a temple built above it. Her suggestion is that the temple was a utility building for the mining system. A long narrow shaft near Khafre shows the same colour, as do other pits. 3. The Russian samples — collected in front of the Khafre pyramid, from surfaces she notes also carry machine marks, and analysed. Most were found likely to be hematite and other ferruginous rocks such as limonite, both important iron ores. 4. X-ray diffraction in the 2020 paper — applied to the backing limestone blocks and casing stones of all three great pyramids. The core stones of Khufu and Menkaure were found to contain minor amounts of iron oxides; Khafre's core stones, rare amounts.

The colouration near Khafre's causeway that the ore argument is built on.

Why iron would be worth mining, on her account 13:46–15:03: it is the most-mined metal in the world, more of it produced than all other metals combined, and the backbone of modern manufacturing. A civilisation more advanced than ours would have used it. She allows the alternative that the operation targeted some other metal and iron was a by-product.

Acid drainage, and what the casings are for

The part of the theory that gives the casing stones a job 16:02–18:58.

Iron ore tailings are among the most common solid wastes in the world. Mining iron degrades landscapes and contaminates surface and ground water through runoff and leaching from waste rock and tailings. Iron uncovered by mining reacts with air and releases sulphuric acid into water, causing acid drainage that can last centuries.

Her figures: the UN has called acid drainage the second biggest problem facing the world after global warming; in the US, about 22,000 km of streams and 180,000 acres of freshwater reservoirs are affected; a 1997 paper put worldwide treatment liabilities at over $10 billion, with the US mining industry spending over $1 million a day.

Preventing it means stopping it at the start, and acid drainage needs three things: oxygen, water and reactive rock. So the waste must be covered with an impermeable layer.

That, she argues, is what the casing stones are: extremely precise, tightly fitted, waterproofing the interior blocks against surface runoff and seepage. The pyramids sit near the Nile, the main freshwater source for a vast area, with documented annual flooding — so controlling runoff from waste would have been a priority. Her conclusion is that the pyramids are a cemented dry-stack tailings storage solution, designed to protect the river and the settlements near it, and that dry-stack tailings is a method we have only recently begun to explore ourselves.

What you can check yourself

  • The rock cores inside Khufu and Khafre are published measurements — 23% and 11.5% of volume — and they settle by themselves that the plateau was higher and that the pyramids are not purely stacked.
  • The 2020 Heritage Science paper is identified by journal and year, and its elevation figures and XRD results can be read against the source — including whether the plateau stands 110 m above the Nile, as the narration says, or 90 m above sea level with the river at 20, as the figure she displays says.
  • Mokattam Hill's elevation is a surveyed number, and the geological continuity with Giza is standard regional geology.
  • The 0.014% figure is arithmetic on published dimensions and can be recomputed — and the source she credits, an 1818 Quarterly Review report giving one 7,400th, is old enough to be worth checking against a modern survey rather than repeated.
  • That the other big pyramids are solid is a matter of public record.
  • The red and orange staining near Khafre's causeway and inside the Osiris Shaft is visible and photographable, and identifying hematite or limonite is routine geology.
  • Acid mine drainage chemistry is textbook, and so is the requirement for oxygen, water and reactive rock.
  • The Nile's flood record is documented history.

What the video does not settle

  • The narration and the displayed figure do not agree on the elevations. The cross-section on screen puts the plateau at +90 m above sea level and the Nile at about +20, a difference of 70 m, against a narrated 110 m above the Nile; and Mokattam's +200 would put the original surface about 110 m above today's, not the 90 m she states. The paper's text may carry other figures, but the video does not reconcile them.
  • Trace iron oxide in Egyptian limestone is unremarkable. Iron oxides are what colour limestone yellow and brown, and "minor" or "rare" amounts in core blocks is what one would expect of ordinary local stone. The XRD result is presented as evidence of an iron operation without any comparison against unmined limestone from the same formation, which is the measurement that would make it mean something.
  • Ore is an economic category, not a mineral one. Hematite and limonite are important iron ores where they occur in workable concentration. Staining on a limestone plateau is not the same thing, and no grade, tonnage or extent is given for any of it.
  • The acid drainage chemistry does not fit the ore she proposes. Acid mine drainage is generated by sulphide minerals — pyrite above all — oxidising to sulphuric acid. Hematite and limonite are already oxides and are not acid-generating. Her own sentence has iron reacting with air to release sulphuric acid, which requires sulphur that nothing in the video places at Giza. The casings-as- acid-barrier argument depends on that step.
  • The core argument is circular as presented. The pyramids are waste storage because they are solid; the solidity is explained by their being waste storage. The 0.014% observation is a real problem for functional theories, but it does not by itself select storage over the conventional reading, in which a monument's mass is its point.
  • The overburden question has a conventional answer that is not addressed. That Giza was extensively quarried for building stone is the standard account, and it explains a lowered plateau and a rebuilt mass without any ore. Her "why not carve them in place" is a genuine question; it is asked of the alternative hypothesis and not of her own.
  • The Russian analysis is not identified. No team, institution, publication or date is given for the sampling near Khafre, and it is one of the two loads the ore argument carries.
  • The concrete claim is imported again. "Multiple scientific papers have shown various material analyses" that the blocks could be concrete — no study is named in this video, and the cemented-tailings reading depends on it entirely. The papers are cited in Pyramid Stones: The Scientific Evidence They're Hiding.
  • The UN ranking is not a real citation. "Second biggest problem facing the world after global warming" is a widely repeated line with no source given.
  • The tailings-mound similarities are shape arguments. Solid, no foundations, less permeable exterior, near underground workings — those are consistent with a pyramid and with a great many other large stone structures, and no feature is identified that would count against the reading.

What we have not checked

  • The 2020 Heritage Science paper has not been located or read. It is identified only by journal and year; no author or title is given. The 110 m and 200 m elevations, the 90 m and 50 m reconstructions, the 11.5% and 23% rock-core volumes and the XRD results are all as she reports them.
  • The Russian researchers who sampled near the Khafre pyramid are not named, and no report has been located. This is a second unidentified Russian analysis in this corpus, distinct from the Sacsayhuamán one.
  • The claim that multiple papers show the pyramid blocks to be a form of concrete is asserted with no study named in this video. The papers are identified in Pyramid Stones: The Scientific Evidence They're Hiding; none has been read.
  • Figures are as stated and have not been traced: 2.3 million blocks and 6 million tons for Khufu; over 13 million tons and 4 million blocks for the three; 0.014% interior volume; 22,000 km of streams and 180,000 acres; over $10 billion and $1 million a day from a 1997 paper.
  • The UN characterisation of acid drainage as the second biggest global problem is unsourced.
  • The six similarities between pyramids and tailings mounds come from a prior video not in this selection; four are repeated here and have not been reviewed in their original form.
  • Whether the underground space near Khafre's funerary temple carries the same machine marks as the Osiris Shaft is asserted and has not been independently confirmed.
