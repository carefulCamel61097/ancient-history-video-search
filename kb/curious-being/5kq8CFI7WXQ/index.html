Mount Nokogiri Quarry: Ancient Machines or Hand Tools?

Tina · 21 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-23

howmainstreamdeflatedunfinishedmachining-and-tool-marksquarriesrock-cut-sites

Mount Nokogiri — Saw Mountain — is a volcanic tuff mountain, barely 329 m high, whose straight cliff faces are entirely man-made 00:20–00:31. The name comes from a jagged cliff at the summit she calls the Hell Peek Point, which projects out like a traditional Japanese saw. It was worked from the Edo period until the 1980s, and the quantity of rock removed is large enough that the mountain's present shape is the quarry. Every worked surface, from the summit down, carries the same mark: evenly spaced parallel slanted striations in horizontal courses 01:44–01:55.

Every worked surface of the mountain carries the same courses of parallel slanted striations, from the summit to the foot of the cliffs.

The video is unusual in structure and says so at the outset 02:12–02:33. Rather than argue a case, she states that after researching it she thinks both possibilities are live, and sets out to play devil's advocate on each in turn.

The case that something is wrong here

The documented Edo quarries look nothing like it 06:08–07:30. Hundreds of quarries on the Izu Peninsula and the western coast of Sagami province supplied stone for the rebuilding of Edo Castle, ordered by Tokugawa Ieyasu in 1603 and continued under his successors across the 250 years of the shogunate. They are smaller, mostly flat or on gentle slopes, and they were worked by feather and wedge: a line of keyholes smashed into the rock, wooden wedges driven in and wetted so they swelled and split the block, sometimes with a groove cut first to control the fracture. The surfaces this leaves are flat splits, and the sites are littered with half-split blocks and keyhole rows. She notes that in 2021 a buried section of the old Edo Castle wall was found carrying exactly those wedge marks — and adds the caveat herself, that the castle has been repaired and rebuilt so often that not everything standing in Tokyo is original Edo work.

The documented Edo-period quarries were worked by feather and wedge, which leaves a line of keyholes and a flat split - nothing like the striated courses at Nokogiri.

Those quarries are signed, and Nokogiri is not 04:00–04:54. Each daimyō assigned a quarrying quota — set by the size of their domain, and imposed partly to drain the clans financially and so make rebellion harder — marked their stone with a clan symbol, kokuin, plus mason's marks, so that shogunate officials could tally output and no other clan could claim the stone. She notes the incentive was real: one quarry recorded injuries frequent enough that the stream running through it ran red. These marks are found across Edo quarries and sometimes on the castle walls themselves. At Nokogiri there are no daimyō symbols and no mason's signatures.

Quarries that supplied Edo Castle signed their stone - clan symbols and mason's marks, cut for quota accounting and against theft. Nokogiri carries none.

And there is no record of it [05:33–06:00, 08:38–09:24]. Nokogiri is closer to Tokyo than most of the supplying quarries and its stone was considered desirable, yet it appears in no documentation as a source for Edo Castle, across two and a half centuries. Work at that scale should have impressed anyone who saw it.

The excavation-marks-versus-dressing-marks distinction 10:42–11:56. This is the most idea in the video and it is a general one. Dressing marks are decorative surface finishes cut into stonework for appearance. Excavation marks are the residue of getting stone out of the bedrock. A worker separating stone uses the fastest method available and has no reason to make it tidy, because roughing and dressing come later, on the block, elsewhere. Her point is that the Nokogiri marks are on the abandoned bedrock and are as uniform as dressing — and there is no reason to spend labour dressing a face you are walking away from, unless producing marks that uniform cost nothing.

She adds a check on this 11:54–12:27: walls and buildings built from Nokogiri stone carry chisel marks on only some blocks, and those marks are not of the same consistency — so the uniformity is not simply a by-product of block extraction showing up on the finished stone.

The curved strokes 17:07–17:52. On one wall she finds large, densely spaced curved marks of matching curvature. Using the horizontal courses as a ruler — a worker elsewhere in the same photograph stands shorter than six rows — she scales a drawn human silhouette up to the strokes and measures them at roughly the height of a standing person, which she argues is well outside a controlled pickaxe swing. She suggests a machine's cutter struck the wall in passing.

Curved strokes she measures at roughly the height of a standing person, which she argues is outside the range of a controlled pickaxe swing.

Alongside this she puts Nokogiri beside modern rotary drum cutters, which cut stone in horizontal courses with uniform lines between them 09:35–10:02, and notes that even those machines do not always produce marks as fine. Her proposal, put as a question, is that the mountain was first worked by machines of a prehistoric civilisation and later rediscovered and reused as an ordinary quarry 10:29–10:41.

The case that it is simply hand work

She then argues the other way — though not without taking some of it back, which is the part the entry has to be exact about.

Trenching is a standard method 12:41–13:39. Beyond wedge splitting, masons cut separation trenches on four sides of a square base with pickaxes, then wedge the block free at the bottom. Greek and Roman quarries show the resulting grid of block scars, with pickaxe marks left on the trench walls. Experienced masons keep a swing consistent — she shows old-time German masons dressing a millstone in regular parallel strokes — and because blocks come out top down, layer by layer, the horizontal courses follow automatically.

Her own counter-example: experienced masons swinging by hand produce regular parallel marks, which is the mundane explanation for the uniformity.

The photographic evidence is against the machine reading 14:26–15:49. Two inscriptions from previous quarry companies survive on one wall at Nokogiri, in an area called the Stone Stage. The lower is from the Suzuki Shiroemon family, who worked the site until 1985 using modern powered saws; the upper is probably later than 1950, because it is absent from a photograph she dates — hedging it herself as "this alleged 1950s photo" — to that decade. That photograph shows the same wall being worked with no machines at all: masons digging trenches with pickaxes and splitting blocks from the bedrock with wedges. Decades of that work produced the cliff, with saw marks from the later mechanised operation at the bottom. She matches the archival photograph to the modern wall by the setback and by the curved markings.

The same wall, before machines: masons trenching with pickaxes and splitting with wedges, which is the work she says produced the cliff.

She states the conclusion this supports plainly: generations of masons with hand tools can remove unthinkable quantities of rock and reshape a mountain, and there is nothing mysterious about the cliffs.

Then she qualifies it, twice 18:33–18:58. Of her own trenching footage: from a distance the marks look consistent, but a closer look shows them "not as refined as the Nokogiri markings". Of the German millstone masons: more uniform, "though a clearer shot reveals that it's still not up to the level of the ones observed at Nokogiri". Both of her hand-work demonstrations are offered and then downgraded, which is why the section does not settle the question even on her own terms.

Two further reservations. She could not find clear photographs zoomed into the earlier marks on the Stone Stage wall to compare them with the rest of the mountain 15:49–16:01; and searching in Japanese she found no video anywhere demonstrating this slanted cutting technique, in a country that documents its crafts thoroughly and has no shortage of splitting videos 19:11–20:10.

Two comparisons and one control, all in her favour as she presents them

  • Pickaxe marks elsewhere 17:59–18:20. She shows quarry pickaxe marks from Egypt and from Norway, and says neither is as regular or parallel as Nokogiri.
  • The other Japanese quarries 13:39–14:26. Nokogiri is not alone. She shows further quarries with desirable stone, said to be Edo-era, with layered faces and sharp walls — also absent from the records and also carrying no daimyō symbols. Some have marks identical to Nokogiri's; some are less orderly.
  • The temple on the same mountain 18:58–19:11. Nokogiri also has an ancient temple with small carved buddhas, and she points out that the tool marks there are less consistent and less parallel than the quarry marks a short distance away.

What she concluded later

The question is left open here. She closes by offering a third possibility — that some parts were quarried by a lost civilisation and that later Japanese masons, inspired by the markings they found, continued in a way that produced similar ones — and asks the viewer to decide 20:11–20:49.

It did not stay open. In A Method to Decipher Ancient Machine Marks from Hand Tools, published eighteen months after this one, she names Mount Nokogiri directly among the Japanese quarries whose marks sweep direction in mid-course, between courses and at inner corners, flaring outward from the corner lines in a herringbone pattern — the signature she uses there for hand work. She concludes that the Japanese tuff quarries, Nokogiri included, were hand excavated with modern pickaxes on soft rock, and uses them as contrast cases against Longyou.

The argument that changed the answer is the one absent from this video: not the uniformity of the marks, but whether their angle survives an inner corner.

What you can check yourself

  • Whether Edo-period quarries carry daimyō and mason's marks while Nokogiri does not is a survey of inscriptions, and both bodies of evidence are in the open.
  • Whether Nokogiri appears in Edo Castle construction records is an archival question with a yes or no answer — as is the same question for the other unrecorded quarries she shows.
  • The 1950s photograph shows an identifiable wall, and she matches it to the modern one on screen by the setback and the curved marks. Anyone can check the match.
  • The two quarry-company inscriptions are dated and one belongs to the Suzuki Shiroemon firm, which operated until 1985.
  • The curved strokes appear in the archival photograph itself, so whether they predate the mechanised operation is a question the picture answers.
  • Tuff is soft — a porous rock formed from compacted volcanic ash. That is stated in the video and is standard geology.
  • The temple carvings on the same mountain are a control anyone visiting can photograph.
  • Whether the striation angle holds through an inner corner is a photograph, and it is the test that later decided the case.

What the video does not settle

  • It does not weigh the two sides. Both are argued and no criterion is offered for choosing between them, so the 1950s photograph of the same wall being cut by hand sits unresolved beside the machine reading.
  • Her own hand-work evidence is withdrawn as soon as it is offered. The trenching footage and the German millstone masons are both introduced as showing that hand tools make regular marks, and both are then said to fall short of Nokogiri. No measurement supports either judgement; it is made by eye, on video of different rock at different distances.
  • The curved strokes are visible in the archival photograph she dates to the 1950s — she uses them to identify the wall. What that means for the powered saws that arrived later, and for the decades of hand quarrying the same photograph shows in progress, is not followed through.
  • The measurement is a drawn overlay. A red silhouette, scaled from a worker standing elsewhere in the frame, is moved across a photograph of a wall that recedes from the camera. Nothing corrects for perspective, and no absolute dimension for the strokes is ever given.
  • No other account of the documentary gap is canvassed. Whether the site was worked privately, outside the quota system, or for local rather than shogunal building is not raised. She finds the same silence across a whole group of quarries and does not return to it.
  • The hardness argument that carries her other sites does not apply here. Tuff is soft, and she says so; the difficulty of the work therefore rests on scale and consistency alone.
  • The dressing/excavation distinction assumes uniform marks cost extra effort. Whether a technique might produce regular marks as a by-product is not considered.
  • The absence of demonstration videos is treated as meaningful without asking whether an extraction technique abandoned by the 1980s would be demonstrated at all, when what Japanese craft documentation preserves is splitting, which she found plenty of.
  • Her own close-up shows strokes running in more than one direction. In the frame used to illustrate the marks as uniform and accurate, a broad fan of curved strokes meets near-vertical strokes along a diagonal on a single face. The claim of consistency is made over footage that does not obviously show it, and the direction question is never raised here at all.
  • Nothing is measured. Stroke spacing, angular deviation and depth consistency are all judged by eye, at both sites and in every comparison.

What we have not checked

  • The proper nouns are now from the channel's own captions, not auto-generated ones, so Nokogiri, Edo, Tokugawa Ieyasu, daimyō, kokuin, Izu, Sagami, feather and wedge and Suzuki Shiroemon are as she wrote them. The romanisation of the family name is hers.
  • "Hell Peek Point" is her own English for the summit viewpoint, not a mangled transcript. It is almost certainly Jigoku-nozoki, literally a peep into hell — but that identification is inferred here, not stated in the video.
  • The mountain is on the Bōsō Peninsula in Chiba — stated here from general knowledge, not from the video, which never names the location beyond "Japan".
  • The dating of the photograph to the 1950s is hers and is hedged in the video itself ("this alleged 1950s photo").
  • The giant Buddha at Nokogiri is dated in the video to 1966, carved with prayers for world peace — mentioned by her in passing and not part of any argument, but worth knowing before anyone reads the mountain's carvings as ancient.
