What Caused the Fall of a Previous Advanced Civilization?

Tina · 22 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-09-28

This is where her collapse date comes from. Everything else in the corpus that says "20,000 to 15,000 years ago" traces back here.

The bottleneck

00:00–03:25 Between 20,000 and 15,000 years ago every human population outside Africa went through a severe reduction, and African populations dropped too, less sharply. She puts up a chart of effective population size across the last 500,000 years, published in Nature in 2015, with the dip marked.

The figures she gives: European, Asian and American effective population sizes fell below 1,500; Africa held at 4,250; the overall figure about 8,750.

Against that she sets the minimum viable population — the smallest number at which a population can persist without being killed off by chance. The study she cites puts survival for 100 years at 95% certainty at 4,169 individuals, and notes the figure rises steeply for longer spans.

This was the closest time we as a species facing potential extinction.

The published curve her collapse date comes from, and the axes it is drawn on.

The figure repays reading. It is sourced on the slide to Nature (2015) 526:68–74, and two things are printed on it that the narration does not say. Its vertical axis is "Population size (scaled in units of 4μNₑ × 10⁴)" — effective population size, a genetic parameter, not a count of people. And its horizontal axis is scaled time, with a header noting it assumes a range of mutation rates and 20 to 30 years per generation; its four labelled ticks are ranges — 333–600 kya, 67–120 kya, 33–60 kya and 7–12 kya, which is the closest tick to the present. The yellow "Population Bottleneck" band is hers, drawn from about the 67–120 kya tick to short of the 7–12 kya one. The date 20,000 to 15,000 years ago is not marked on the chart.

The figures for the individual populations — below 1,500, Africa at 4,250, 8,750 overall — are spoken over an artist's reconstruction painting of a Palaeolithic camp. No table accompanies them.

And the Younger Dryas is crossed out. The event usually named as the catastrophe — global cooling, biomass burning, a possible impact 12,800 years ago — produced no significant population decline. She had already argued that in an earlier video. So if the famous cataclysm did not do it, what did?

Ruling things out

03:25–06:07 The decline lasted thousands of years, so a single sudden event is the wrong shape of cause — "a slow and prolonged march to possible extinction".

She notes that the Late Quaternary Extinction, which removed hundreds of large mammals between 50,000 and 5,000 years ago, has three main candidate causes — climate and habitat change, the Younger Dryas impact, and human overkill — and that there were no megafaunal extinctions at the coldest point, the Last Glacial Maximum of 26,000 to 19,000 years ago. Cold alone, in other words, was not the problem.

Of the three, the impact is ruled out on timing and overkill is dismissed for humans, which leaves climate.

HS1

06:22–13:12 Heinrich Stadial 1: a massive discharge of icebergs into the North Atlantic between 18,000 and 15,000 years ago, after the Last Glacial Maximum had ended — one of the most extreme climate episodes of the last glacial cycle, and simultaneous with the population decline.

This is the most heavily sourced stretch in the video. She reads out a run of paper titles and quoted abstracts, and they are legible on screen:

  • Stager, Ryves, Chase and Pausata, "Catastrophic Drought in the Afro-Asian Monsoon Region During Heinrich Event 1", Science, 11 March 2011, 331(6022):1299–1302 — shown with its DOI and abstract. HS1 "coincided with one of the most extreme and widespread megadroughts of the past 50,000 years or more in the Afro-Asian monsoon region, with potentially serious consequences for Paleolithic cultures"
  • "Enhanced aridity and atmospheric high-pressure stability over the western Mediterranean during the North Atlantic cold events of the past 50 k.y."
  • "Millennial to orbital-scale variations of drought intensity in the Eastern Mediterranean"
  • "Sahel megadrought during Heinrich Stadial 1"
  • "Elevated dust depositions in West Asia linked to ocean–atmosphere shifts during North Atlantic cold events"
  • "Catastrophic drought in East Asian monsoon region during Heinrich event 1", with arid conditions documented as far south as Borneo

The climate half of the argument, and the one place in it that is fully cited.

Her point about where those droughts fall is the good one: West and central Africa, the Mediterranean basin, West Asia, East Asia and South Asia were the habitable refugia during the Last Glacial Maximum. The people who had survived the cold by concentrating there then met three thousand years of drought.

The southern hemisphere got the opposite problem 10:16–11:21. The same iceberg discharge drove an abrupt increase in southern monsoon rainfall over the South Pacific, eastern South America and Australia, with an active El Niño–Southern Oscillation — which means storms, floods and landslides rather than drought.

Why Africa fared better is her own suggestion 11:34–11:46: it is the only continent with large areas in both hemispheres, so its people could move within it.

The dust check 12:32–13:12. A Vostok ice core chart covering 420,000 years shows temperature tracking CO₂, and dust concentration rising when temperature falls — cold periods were dry and dusty. In the whole record, she says, HS1 carries the highest dust concentration, which is what a millennia-long northern drought should look like.

Independent confirmation of a long northern drought: dust in an Antarctic ice core.

One caution about that chart, visible on it: the band she shades runs from roughly 25,000 to 15,000 years ago, so it covers the Last Glacial Maximum as well as HS1, and the dust maximum sits at the older end of it. Her argument is that the crisis came after the cold peak rather than during it, and the dust peak is at the cold peak.

Her explanation for why this glacial ending and not the others: anatomically modern humans had been through multiple glacial cycles, but never a drought like this one. And she adds a second factor of her own — that losing food and water would have triggered territorial conflict and cycles of war.

On whether it could happen again 14:00–15:15: Heinrich stadials recur at the ends of glacial periods, so another is coming eventually. We could not stop it, but she wonders whether ice sheets could be monitored for critical mass and icebergs steered to where their melting would do less — "this is just my layman's idea."

The turn

15:19–21:15 Then the argument the video was built for.

The worldwide megalithic sites — Giza, Baalbek, Machu Picchu, Sacsayhuamán, Puma Punku, Yangshan, Changyu, Longyou — are, on her reading, the remains of a previous globe-spanning civilisation. Its end should show up as a devastating drop in human population. The Younger Dryas does not provide one. HS1 is the only candidate in the entire span of anatomically modern humans.

Her supporting chain: Homo sapiens had already existed for over 280,000 years; by at least 40,000 years ago people had the same brain shape and equal or larger brain size; and it took us about 12,000 years to get from stone tools to landing rockets on Mars — so there was ample time for an earlier run at it.

What she pictures: droughts and floods, resource shortage, geopolitical conflict, wars "with highly developed weaponry" in which "nuclear weapons or similarly destructive devices were unleashed", followed by collapse. Survivors scattered, specialised knowledge became worthless, buildings failed without maintenance.

Metals rust, plastic breaks down and time itself is the final destroyer. Only these rock-hewn caves and stone structures remain to tell their passing glory.

What you can check yourself

  • The Nature 2015 population curve is published and the bottleneck it shows is not in dispute; what it means is.
  • Every drought paper she quotes is titled on screen, most of them fully — six titles a reader can look up in an afternoon.
  • HS1's dates and mechanism — iceberg discharge into the North Atlantic, 18,000 to 15,000 years ago — are standard paleoclimatology.
  • The Vostok ice core record is public data. Temperature, CO₂ and dust for 420,000 years can be plotted by anyone, and whether HS1 carries the highest dust peak is a question the data answers.
  • The Last Glacial Maximum's dates, 26,000 to 19,000 years ago, and the absence of megafaunal extinction at its peak, are both published.
  • The minimum viable population figure of 4,169 comes from a specific study and can be read, including what it is a minimum of.
  • The southern hemisphere's wet response to Heinrich events is modelled and published, and the ENSO behaviour with it.
  • The Late Quaternary Extinction's three candidate causes are the live options in that literature, stated fairly.

What the video does not settle

  • The date is not on the chart. Her own figure's time axis is scaled and labelled in ranges, the nearest of which to the present is 7–12 kya. Where "20,000 to 15,000 years ago" comes from is not shown, and it is the date the rest of the corpus repeats.
  • An effective population size and a head count are set against each other. The axis on her own slide reads Nₑ. She puts a figure of 1,500 from it beside a minimum viable population of 4,169, which is a count of individuals. That the two are different quantities is not addressed.
  • The curve tracks the lineages that survived to have descendants today. On her own account the megalithic builders were a different population, and whether their collapse would register in it is not discussed.
  • The war is invented. Resource conflict is offered as speculation; "nuclear weapons or similarly destructive devices were unleashed" is scene-setting with nothing behind it. Nothing in the video distinguishes her scenario from drought alone.
  • "The only candidate in the whole span" assumes the record would show it. Whether a regional or small civilisation's end would appear in a global genetic curve is not asked.
  • The curve's resolution is not discussed. What the method can and cannot resolve in the recent past is never raised, and the date rests on it.
  • Her own date for anatomically modern humans has moved again — 300,000 years here, and about 190,000 in the 12,000-to-6,000 video.
  • Africa's shallower bottleneck is explained by a guess she flags as one: that people could migrate between hemispheres within the continent. No test is offered.
  • The iceberg-steering proposal is labelled a layman's idea by her, and nothing is cited for it.
