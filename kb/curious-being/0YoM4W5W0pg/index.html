Great Sphinx Water Erosion Mystery: A Buried Secret Revealed

The water erosion hypothesis in one line 00:06–00:26: John Anthony West and geologist Robert Schoch argued that the heavy vertical channelling on the Sphinx enclosure walls could only have been produced by rainfall and runoff over a long period, not by sand and wind. Giza receives about 25 mm / 1 inch of rain a year, so Schoch placed the oldest parts of the Sphinx at around 12,000 years ago against a conventional date of about 4,500 00:58–01:18.

She sets out where other geologists stand 01:30–02:03. Colin Reader accepts the rainfall reading but proposes a far more conservative shift — a few centuries earlier, not thousands of years. Jørn Christiansen argues the rainwater erosion predates the carving of the Sphinx itself, and that because the enclosure walls are cut at right angles to a height of up to 10 m they are manifestly man-made. Whoever cut them, and whenever, they were cut.

The video then does two things, and the second is the more interesting: it argues the erosion needs a longer exposure than the conventional date allows, and it argues that the comparison normally used to prove the Sphinx is older than the pyramids does not work.

The Green Sahara did not reach Giza

The usual place to put the wetter period is the last African Humid Period, the Green Sahara of about 11,000 to 5,000 years ago 02:34–02:56. She argues it does not help.

The source is on screen, and it is a full citation: **Daniel Palchan and Adi Torfstein, "A drop in Sahara dust fluxes records the northern limits of the African Humid Period", Nature Communications (2019), DOI 10.1038/s41467-019-11701-z** 03:51–03:57. The paper's own abstract, also legible, says the latitudinal extent of the northward expansion of the tropical rain belt "remains poorly constrained."

Her reading of it 03:57–04:30: the wetted zone lay below latitude 22° N. The pyramids stand above 29° N, outside it. So even at the height of the Green Sahara, Giza was not in the rain.

How far north the African Humid Period reached, and where Giza sits relative to it.

She adds the qualification rather than skipping it 04:43–05:00: the region was still wetter, but by a mechanism that does not produce runoff on a plateau — recharged rivers flowing north out of the southern monsoon zone into Lower Egypt.

Then the longer view 05:12–05:38. A chart of green and desert Sahara periods across the last 230,000 years, sourced on screen to the barium-to-aluminium ratio at Ocean Drilling Program site 968 in the eastern Mediterranean, leads her to conclude that for the last 78,000 years or longer the Giza plateau received very limited rainfall, and that what it did receive probably arrived as infrequent intense storms — the pattern the area still has.

The long record of green and desert Sahara phases.

This cuts against her own side. Removing the Green Sahara removes the easiest explanation for where the water came from, and she says so plainly rather than working around it.

How long was it out of the sand?

This is the argument the video is built on, and it is a counting exercise rather than a geological one.

The burial is documented in pictures 06:25–06:57. A 16th-century engraving, dated 1572 on screen, shows the Sphinx buried to its chest. Later illustrations and 19th-century photographs show the same thing: lower body and enclosure walls under sand. When it was dug out in the early 1900s the enclosure walls already carried serious erosion, so the damage was not done by recent storms. The same photographs show ancient restoration on the lower body, which means damage older than the restoration 07:04–07:12.

For most of recorded history only the head and upper body stood above the sand.

The erosion was already there when the enclosure came out of the sand.

Buried stone does not weather 07:14–07:22. So the question becomes: when, and for how long, was the enclosure open?

Her timeline 07:29–11:10:

Two of those entries carry an argument beyond chronology. Amenhotep II's temple overlaps the northwest corner of the Sphinx Temple and sits at a higher level 07:54–08:12, which she reads as meaning the builders did not know the Sphinx Temple was there — it was already buried.

The New Kingdom builders did not know the Sphinx Temple was there.

The Roman stairway was built on top of the Sphinx Temple as well 10:05–10:18, so the Romans did not know either. The stairway was dismantled in the 1930s.

The Roman stair was built over the buried Sphinx Temple.

The count that follows 11:12–11:56: for most of the last 4,000 years only the head and upper body stood above the sand, the rest protected from both wind and water. On the conventional date the Sphinx has been exposed for at most about 500 years in total, in a climate delivering 25 mm of rain a year. Her conclusion is that this is not enough to cut the channels on the walls, so the weathering must predate the conventional timeline.

She adds a separate objection 12:11–12:41: if Khufu and Khafre raised the pyramids 4,500 years ago, why does the next documented work at the site, a millennium later, consist of small mudbrick structures? On that reading the technology did not advance, it collapsed.

Where she stops the argument she has been making

The video's best passage is the one that takes apart its own side's standard comparison 12:56–16:15. The comparison is this: the walls of the Khafre quarry, cut into the same plateau at about the same time, are not as badly eroded as the Sphinx enclosure walls — which is usually offered as proof that the Sphinx enclosure is older.

She gives two reasons the comparison fails.

Position. The enclosure floor is 20–22 m above sea level; the bases of the three pyramids are all over 62 m — more than 40 m, or thirteen storeys, higher 13:25–13:45. The plateau slopes east toward the Nile, with the north and west sides above the Sphinx, so runoff drains into the enclosure. The Khafre quarry walls sit near the top of the plateau and never took that concentrated flow 14:20–14:30.

Stone. The three layers, as she names them 14:35–15:45:

layerwherecharacter
Member IIwestern and southern enclosure walls; the Sphinx bodylighter, softer, most visibly water-eroded
Member I, the lower building stonebase of the southern walldarker, harder — and severely eroded, with what she reads as chemical weathering from the wetting and drying of runoff
Member IIIthe Sphinx headdarker, harder, and remarkably well preserved despite being the most exposed part of the statue

The head is the demonstration: the most exposed surface on the monument is the least damaged, because of what it is made of. And the Khafre quarry walls are cut in the darker, harder limestone throughout.

The Sphinx is cut through three limestone layers of different hardness.

Her conclusion is stated as a limit on inference 16:07–16:15: since the two sets of walls are neither of the same stone nor exposed to the same conditions, "it might not be proper to conclude that the heavier erosion of the Sphinx enclosure walls means they were excavated earlier than the pyramid walls."

What is left, and where she takes it

Having removed the Green Sahara and the quarry comparison, what remains is the exposure problem 16:16–17:19. The walls and the lower body were damaged — the ancient restoration proves it — so the enclosure was open long enough for heavy runoff erosion. Without maintenance it silts up in a few decades; that is what the modern tourist park exists to prevent. So on her reading there must have been an operation keeping the enclosure clear for a long time. And because the western enclosure walls carry sheet-wash marks, the upper plateau feeding them had to be substantially open too.

From there 17:30–17:46 she suggests the ancient operation may have intended to direct rainfall into the enclosure — a sunken catch basin at the low end of the site, in the manner of modern stormwater management. The catch-basin reading is developed much further in the two later videos: the Sphinx Temple entry and the erosion-sequence entry.

The closing question is about economics rather than geology 17:53–18:30. The modern park is paid for by ticket revenue. What paid for an ancient workforce to keep a plateau clear of sand for centuries? An operation of that duration has to make financial sense. Her answer is her standing hypothesis: Giza as a prehistoric mine and mine-waste storage site, which would need both open working areas and drainage 18:31–19:00.

The same method, used to argue the other way: How Old Are These MEGALITHS? A Study of Erosion in Ancient Egyptian Architecture builds a case for much longer timelines out of published limestone erosion rates, and out of a comparison this video does not address — megalithic structures at Giza eroding badly while small-block Fourth Dynasty work at the same elevation, in the same limestone and never protected by granite casing, keeps its flat faces and square corners.

What you can check yourself

  • The paper. Palchan and Torfstein (2019) is open access and the DOI is on screen. Its abstract is shown, and it says the northern limit "remains poorly constrained."
  • The latitudes. Giza is at about 29° N. What northern limit the paper's own figure draws is visible in the figure itself.
  • The elevations. Enclosure floor at 20–22 m above sea level, pyramid bases above 62 m — both are surveyed figures.
  • The pictures. The 1572 engraving and the 19th-century photographs of a sand-buried Sphinx are published and reproduced widely. What they show of the enclosure walls is a matter of looking.
  • The silences. Herodotus, Diodorus Siculus and Strabo are all in print. Whether they describe the Sphinx is a search anyone can run.
  • The Members. The three-layer division of the Sphinx's bedrock is standard geology with a standard nomenclature, and which member the head is cut from is published.
  • The Roman stairway's position. That it was built over the Sphinx Temple, and dismantled in the 1930s, is in the excavation record.

What the video does not settle

  • Her own figure offers four northern limits, not one. The map she shows carries four dashed contours labelled (a), (b), (c) and (d), at progressively higher latitudes. The narration takes one value — "below 22° N" — which corresponds to roughly where the magenta (c) line runs, and which is where she has added a black arrow. The green (d) line is drawn further north and reaches about the latitude of her own "Pyramids" marker. What distinguishes the four is not on screen: no legend, caption or explanation for them appears, and the narration does not mention that the figure gives a range. The argument may well survive — the lines may not all be monsoon limits — but the viewer is shown a spread and told a single number.
  • The 230,000-year chart does not show 78,000 dry years. It shows a flat, low stretch from about 78,000 to about 12,000 years ago, and then one of the largest Green Sahara peaks on the whole record in the last ~12,000 years. Her sentence works only because of the latitude argument made a minute earlier, which excludes Giza from that peak. The chart on its own does not support the claim as spoken.
  • The exposure count is an argument, not a measurement. No erosion rate is offered — no figure for how much limestone a given rainfall removes in a given time — so "500 years is not enough" is asserted rather than calculated. The same gap applies to any longer window she prefers.
  • Her own alternative has no date. Having argued the conventional window is too short, she does not propose a replacement — in this video the wetter period is a requirement with no time attached to it.
  • The sheet-wash claim is not shown. Marks on the western walls are described, not displayed with an indication of what distinguishes sheet wash from channel flow.
  • The Amenhotep II temple is drawn as a dashed outline on the plan, in the convention used for a reconstructed or inferred footprint rather than excavated walls. The overlap argument rests on where that dashed square is placed.
  • The mudbrick argument has an obvious answer that is not addressed — that mudbrick is what ordinary New Kingdom building used, and its scale says nothing about what the state could do in stone.
  • Nothing here connects an open plateau to mining. The economic question is real; the answer is imported from other videos.
