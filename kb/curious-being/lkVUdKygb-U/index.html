The Osireion Mystery: Water, Construction, and Its True Purpose?!

Tina · 23 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-23

whyhowdatingcross-continentalrock-cut-sitesmachining-and-tool-markspolygonal-masonrywater-erosion-and-weathering

The Osireion at Abydos is a subterranean megalithic structure known for enormous blocks and standing water. The granite pillars average around 50 tons; some suggest the largest blocks run to 100 tons or more 00:00–00:25.

The numbers that make it strange:

  • The water channel is about 2 m wide and 15 m / 49 ft deep — a stone-lined channel running five storeys underground.
  • There is no bedrock underneath. Ground penetrating radar and seismic scans from deep wells in the area found no rock formation around it. A megalithic structure holding this much water is built on sand.
  • James Westerman's team could not dewater it. Pumping at 500 gallons a minute failed. Water gushes in from below.

And this is not a modern water-table problem: Strabo described it 2,000 years ago as already holding water — "also a fountain which lies at a great depth, so that one descends to it down vaulted galleries made of monoliths of surprising size and workmanship" 01:18–01:43. The vaulted roof he saw is gone, she says, taken by stone robbers.

The 1914 opinion nobody kept

The Temple of Seti stands beside it, 13th century BC, and the mainstream position is that the Osireion dates to the same reign. Against that she puts Edouard Naville, the Swiss archaeologist who was among the first to excavate it 02:10–02:58. In his 1914 report he wrote that the Osireion "has all the characteristics of a very old construction" and is "one of the finest specimens of the gigantic architecture of the first dynasties", closely resembling the Valley Temple of Khafre at Giza — the "same style of architecture and masonry, and the same absence of ornament of any kind", though on larger proportions.

The water is not ordinary water

Multiple teams have sampled and analysed the water over several decades, and the published results agree that it is unlike anything else nearby — not the closest wells, not any other tested source 03:11–04:09. A 2022 paper found it to be a mixture of three:

1. Old Nile water, from before the Aswan High Dam was built in 1970 2. Recent Nile water, from after the dam 3. Paleowater from deeper aquifers

The same results indicated a probable paleochannel to the northeast.

Paleowater — fossil water — is groundwater that recharged before the Holocene. She gives the scale: actively circulating groundwater is generally under 10,000 years old, paleowater runs from tens to hundreds of thousands of years and can be millions, and it is non-renewable because the conditions that recharged it are gone.

Her question, which she puts as a question: if the Osireion reaches a paleowater aquifer, was it built in that era — or is the connection an accident?

What the stonework shows

The structure uses three stones — granite, sandstone and limestone — and she argues it displays nearly every feature that recurs at megalithic sites worldwide 05:15–06:19: mega-scale blocks, paper-thin joints, knobs and inverted knobs, polygonal blocks, bent L-shaped corner blocks. The polygonal fitting and the bent corners are the same as at Khafre's Valley Temple and at sites in Peru. One feature she says is unique to the Osireion: a long angled drilled hole in the wall of the middle pool on the central island.

The mortise and tenon joints 06:44–09:55. The granite pillars, roughly 2.4 m square by over 3.5 m high, are tied to the stone beams above them by mortise and tenon joints — and she finds the same joints elsewhere on site, on the entrance traverse hall and the outer east wall.

Her argument for why this matters is materials engineering. Mortise and tenon is a woodworking joint, rarely used in masonry, because stone has high compressive but low tensile strength — it is brittle, and right-angled edges and corners chip easily. A knock while positioning a block ruins the joint. Ancient stone construction normally uses dovetails, metal clamps, or mortar. Lifting a 50-ton granite beam and lowering it so a mortise meets a tenon exactly would be hard with modern equipment.

The joint she argues is beyond bronze-age masonry - a woodworking connection cut into fifty-ton granite.

The screeding marks

Her evidence that the blocks were cast rather than carved 09:55–12:42. On one Osireion wall the upper portion is flat and straight while the blocks below are puffy and bulging, and between the two finishes there are screeding marks — the pattern left when a soft material is struck flat and the process stops. Elsewhere, niche walls are screeded and flattened; one is flattened almost entirely except at the bottom.

She points to the same thing on the Menkaure pyramid's granite casings — blocks flat on one side and bulging on the other, frozen mid-process. And she draws the conclusion that matters most to her: the Osireion screeding is in sandstone and the Menkaure in granite, so whoever did it could cast more than one kind of stone.

The wall she calls the best evidence that the blocks were cast rather than carved.

Casting, she argues, would account at once for the oversized blocks, the sharp 90° edges, the tight seams, the bulging faces, the polygonal fitting, the shallow knobs, the stamp and scrape marks, and how the mortise and tenon joints were made at all.

Nobody can get in

The layout argument, and it is the most concrete thing in the video 13:14–15:37.

The structure is called a cenotaph of Seti I. A cenotaph is a memorial for people to visit. But enter through the hallway and the water channel is immediately in the way — 15 m deep, over 2 m wide, wrapping right round and isolating the central hall, and blocking access to every niche and to the end chamber. Tourists cross today on long wooden boards. Without them nobody gets past the front traverse hall.

There is a 123 m / over 400 ft corridor leading to the building, at the end of which a visitor would be stopped by water. It cannot have been for worship, since nobody can get in to pay respects; its low elevation and constant flooding rule out a tomb; and the end chamber and all the niches were found empty at first excavation.

One detail she cannot fit: two sets of human-scale stairs going down into the channel, on an island the channel makes unreachable.

Why the building cannot be what it is called: there is no way in, and the only crossing is modern.

The reservoir

Her answer, and Naville's 15:37–17:32. Bahay Issawi, a former Director of the Egyptian Geological Survey, wrote in 2009 that the Osireion is the only gushing spring on the entire western side of the Nile Valley: "Nowhere in this long stretch, approximately 1000 km, is the topography lower than the aquifer except in the Osireion."

So the site is exceptional, and the location was chosen. A subterranean structure with a 15 m shaft sunk through sand into paleowater and old Nile water was built to collect water: a well and cistern combination. Naville said as much — "neither a temple nor a tomb, but a reservoir, a great hydraulic work" — and added that its builders "must have known very well the laws of the rise and fall of the river, and also of the subterranean sheet of water… the so-called underground Nile."

The reading explains several things at once, on her account: no circulation path is needed because people only entered to maintain it; the drilled hole becomes an overflow or pipe; and mortise and tenon rather than clamps or mortar makes sense in permanent damp, because metal would rust and mortar would swell, crack and lose its bond.

On the cartouches

Seti I's cartouches are carved on parts of the Osireion, which with the neighbouring temple is why it is dated to him 19:19–20:41. Her reply: cartouches prove only that the structure existed in Seti's time. Ramesses II is known to have inscribed his name on pre-existing monuments, and claiming a predecessor's work was ordinary practice.

She contrasts the two buildings directly. The Temple of Seti is limestone and sandstone, ornate and classically Egyptian; the Osireion is granite, austere and unornamented. Seti's columns are taller at about 7 m but are stacked drums; the Osireion's pillars are shorter, monolithic, much thicker and around 50 tons each.

A last argument on age: a well dug by dynastic Egyptians would not have needed to go this deep, since a shallower one would have reached groundwater. Going deep enough to tap paleowater suggests somebody else.

What you can check yourself

  • Naville's 1914 report is published, and both his quoted judgements — that the structure is very old and that it is a reservoir rather than a tomb — can be read in his own words.
  • Strabo's description is a surviving text, and it settles that the structure was already water-filled 2,000 years ago.
  • Issawi's 2009 statement about the Osireion being the only place in 1,000 km where the ground lies below the aquifer is a claim by a named former head of a national geological survey.
  • The water chemistry is published. Whether Osireion water is a three-way mixture including paleowater is a laboratory result, not an interpretation.
  • The layout is the strongest checkable thing here. That a 15 m channel wraps round the central hall and blocks every niche, and that modern boards are what let visitors cross, is visible to anyone who goes and in any plan of the site.
  • The mortise and tenon joints are on the pillars and photographable.
  • The stacked drums of Seti's columns against the monolithic Osireion pillars is a comparison a visitor can make in twenty minutes.
  • Whether the end chamber and niches were empty at first excavation is in the excavation record.

What the video does not settle

  • Paleowater dates the water, not the well. This is the central problem. Groundwater that recharged 50,000 years ago can be reached by a shaft sunk last week; the age of an aquifer places no constraint on when something was dug into it. She raises the objection herself — "was the connection to paleowater just an accident or a design oversight?" — and then proceeds as though the first reading had won.
  • The "a shallower well would have done" argument assumes a static water table. The depth needed to reach water depends on where the water was, and the video's own wider argument is that the climate and hydrology of Egypt have changed repeatedly.
  • Naville is used as an authority on two different questions. His reservoir reading is an architectural judgement about a building he excavated. His First Dynasty dating was a judgement made in 1914, before most of what is now known about Abydos, and the video does not say what happened to it afterwards or why the field moved.
  • The resemblance to Khafre's Valley Temple is doing dating work it cannot do. Similar masonry is evidence of similar practice; it becomes evidence of a shared date only if one already accepts that the Valley Temple is far older than the accepted account, which is argued elsewhere and assumed here.
  • The layout argument is strong and its conclusion is narrower than stated. No circulation path is good evidence that the structure was not built for visitors. A reservoir is one thing that fits; so is a structure meant to be entered rarely, or one whose flooding was not part of the design. Ruling out a cenotaph does not select between those.
  • The stairs are left unexplained. Two flights of human-scale steps descending into the channel sit awkwardly with a building nobody was meant to enter, and the video notes them without resolving them.
  • The casting claim is imported and load-bearing. Screeding marks are offered as "the best evidence" that the blocks are not natural rock. No analysis of Osireion stone is cited, and no study underlying the artificial-stone research is named anywhere in this corpus.
  • The screeding is not legible as tooling in the frame she shows it in. The wall she points to does have flat, tightly jointed courses above and softer, bulging, rounded-edged blocks below — that much is plain. The vertical streaking she reads as screeding runs down the face in a way that is equally consistent with water staining and surface erosion, and nothing in the image distinguishes them.
  • Whether the structure really sits on no bedrock is a striking claim that would follow from named survey data, and no survey, team or report is identified.

What we have not checked

  • Edouard Naville's 1914 report has not been located or read. All three quotations are as she presents them.
  • The 2022 water paper is not named and has not been read; the three-source mixture, the paleochannel and the pre- and post-dam Nile water are as reported.
  • Bahay Issawi's 2009 statement is quoted from screen; the publication is not identified.
  • James Westerman's dewatering attempt and the 500 gallons per minute figure are as given and have not been traced.
  • The claim that GPR and seismic scans found no bedrock beneath the structure is unsourced.
  • Figures are as stated and have not been traced: 50-ton pillars, 2.4 m square by 3.5 m high, the 15 m and 2 m channel, the 123 m corridor, the 16 niches, 6 ft niche openings, Seti's 7 m columns.
  • Strabo's passage is quoted in translation from screen and has not been checked.
  • The claim that the Osireion has no bedrock and no ornament in common with any dynastic work is her characterisation and has not been tested against the archaeological literature.
  • The screeding marks are asserted to indicate cast stone; no analysis of Osireion material has been located, and the supporting research is unnamed here as everywhere else in this corpus.
