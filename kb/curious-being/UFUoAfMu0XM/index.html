Sanxingdui's Tree of Life: A Symbol Found Across Ancient Cultures?

Tina · 13 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-09-28

cross-continentalconvergencemainstreamwhysanxingduishared-symbolsbronze-age-metal-trademigration-and-diffusion

The method is announced, which is unusual enough to be the reason to read this one 04:33–04:45:

"I'll try to be like a detective as we walk through my thought process and reasoning. During my investigation, improbable candidates will be eliminated, so eventually we can filter down to the most reasonable answer."

The question is where Sanxingdui came from. Her framing 00:05–00:45: a Bronze Age culture in the Sichuan basin whose bronzes predate the Chinese Bronze Age by 200 years, with artefacts unlike anything previously found in China — and, she adds, DNA results on human remains that have not been released despite samples being collected decades ago.

Her prior, carried in from the metallurgy video 00:51–01:54: China entered the Bronze Age about 1,400 years after Mesopotamia and Central Asia, and most Chinese archaeologists agree that the earliest bronze technology was not invented in China but imported by nomadic steppe cultures. Chariots in Sumer and horse riding made the Eurasian steppe a working route from about 5,000 years ago.

The masks, before the elimination starts

Three observations she draws off the masks themselves 01:58–03:26:

  • The human masks have no pupils; the animal masks do. She does not read that as closed eyes, because excavators found paint on eyebrows, eyes and lips on some masks — so the features may once have been painted in.
  • Every human mask has pierced ears, and she states there is no record of ear-piercing among ancient Chinese men. Beside them she puts a later Chinese bronze of a foreign merchant with a horse, dated 25–220 AD, who has a bearded chin, a pierced ear and a brimless cap she compares to the Sanxingdui headgear.
  • The projection on the chin she reads as a beard, making most of the masks male — with one possible exception.

The masks are bearded, pierced and pupil-less.

The context she sets around them 03:33–04:22: the artefacts look ceremonial, but the people of the central plain were not known for deity worship or for sculpture commemorating kings. Sanxingdui ran 2100–1200 BC in a region that was not part of the Chinese empire until about 200 BC, and written records place pastoral steppe people west of ancient China. So she treats it as a stand-alone culture with its own religion.

The elimination

Mesoamerica, ruled out on dates 04:50–05:48. Maya and Aztec ritual masks look superficially similar, but metallurgy in pre-Columbian Mesoamerica appears in West Mexico by roughly 600–800 AD — about 2,000 years after Sanxingdui. She notes she finds other Maya–Chinese similarities interesting and defers them.

The candidate list 06:03–06:29: six regions entered the Bronze Age before China — Mesopotamia, Central Asia, Egypt, Greece and Rome, the Indus Valley, the Andes.

The filter is gold foil. Some Sanxingdui masks are covered with gold foil, so she asks which Bronze Age cultures did the same 06:35–07:26:

Mycenae, Greecethe Mask of Agamemnon, about 1550 BC
Scythian, Central Asian steppea gold foil mask, 700–300 BC

Which Bronze Age cultures made gold foil masks, and when.

Her composite map is worth reading against her own argument. Of the four cultures pinned on it, Sanxingdui at 2100–1200 BC has the earliest start date; Mycenae at 1550 BC falls inside its range, and Scythia and Persia are entirely later. So the map does not show a tradition Sanxingdui could have inherited foil from — which is the problem she solves later by stepping back to the Andronovo, though no Andronovo foil mask appears on it.

Egypt, ruled out on technique 07:26–07:53. Egyptian gold masks are thick gold with gem inlay, not foil — a different craft, so not a direct relation.

The Andes, ruled out on a second reading 07:53–08:31. This is the most careful moment in the video. A summary timeline shows the Andes entering the Bronze Age early — but reading the detail under it, Andean metalworkers first used gold and copper rather than bronze, and evidence of fully developed smelting only appears with the Moche, 200 BC – 600 AD. So the Andean Bronze Age postdates Sanxingdui, and the timeline she started from was misleading at a glance.

Three regions left: Greece and the Mediterranean, the Middle East, Central Asia.

The trees

The title subject, and the part where she is looking rather than sorting 08:45–09:20.

The bronze trees are 4 m tall. Archaeologists read them as damaged, with parts missing from the top. Her own observation is about their shape: most trees have branches going up or out, and these curl downward, with leaves only at the tips. What tells her they are trees at all is that there are birds on the branches.

The bronze trees do not branch the way trees branch.

The comparison she draws between the bronze trees and the Mesopotamian sacred tree.

That form reminds her of the Sumerian tree of life 09:20–10:06. She shows a Sumerian relief of Sargon standing before a tree of life and lists the resemblances: branches spreading out, upper branches curling down, some leaves up and some down. On an Assyrian version she zooms in on nodes that, magnified, look like birds with eyes and beaks. The Sumerian tree, she says, is the earliest such symbol, and many cultures have their own.

Then the object that does the actual work 10:06–10:37. A felt tapestry dated 241 BC, recovered from a Pazyryk tomb in the Altai Mountains, Siberia, carries a repeated scene of the seated goddess Tabiti and a horse rider — and the goddess holds a sacred item resembling the Sanxingdui bronze trees.

A steppe object that does share the bronze trees' form.

Stepping back from the Scythians

The best reasoning in the video, because she applies her own date rule to her own favoured answer 10:37–11:53.

Pazyryk is Scythian: nomads across the Eurasian steppe, master metallurgists who used gold foil for masks and for decorating artefacts, exactly as at Sanxingdui. But Scythian culture ran 900 BC – 200 AD, and Sanxingdui 2100–1200 BC. The Scythians are too late to be the source.

So she steps back to their predecessor: the Andronovo culture, a group of related Bronze Age steppe cultures of 2200–900 BC, occupying the Altai Mountains and the surrounding parts of Siberia — close to ancient China. The Andronovo in turn inherit from Afanasievo and Yamnaya, 3300–2500 BC.

And a small physical detail to close the loop 11:53–12:09: Bronze Age steppe burials in that region have produced metal earrings on male remains.

What she has assembled

Her convergence, stated as a list 12:09–12:38: a steppe culture close to ancient China, with advanced bronze before the Chinese Bronze Age, a tradition of gold foil masks, a similar tree of life symbol, and men who wore ear piercings.

She closes by pointing forward to the red-haired Tarim Basin mummies as possibly related, which is the next entry.

A conflict with her own later video

Worth setting out rather than smoothing over, because it is a change of position and not an error.

This video is from April 2021 and uses the Sumerian and Assyrian "tree of life" as a symbol that travelled — zooming into an Assyrian relief to find bird-like nodes in it.

In March 2023, in the handbags and pinecones entry, she argues that the Neo-Assyrian sacred tree is a date palm — a single unbranched trunk with a crown of fan-shaped fronds — and that the "pinecone" held beside it is a male date flower cluster used for hand pollination, an agricultural operation Mesopotamians were performing by 3300 BC. That reading leaves much less room for the tree as a free-floating symbol of shared descent.

Both entries are kept as their presenter made them, two years apart, and the disagreement is left where it is.

What you can check yourself

  • The masks. Pierced ears, the chin projection and the absence of pupils are all visible in museum photographs of the Sanxingdui bronzes, and the Sanxingdui Museum publishes them.
  • The dated comparisons. Every mask in her gold foil table is a famous, catalogued object with a published date — Agamemnon, the Oxus treasure, Tutankhamun. The table can be rebuilt from museum records.
  • The Andes correction. That a summary timeline and the detailed text beneath it give different impressions is checkable in a minute, and is the kind of thing worth checking generally.
  • The Pazyryk tapestry. It is published, dated and in the Hermitage; whether the object in Tabiti's hand resembles the Sanxingdui trees is a matter of looking at both.
  • The bronze trees' branch direction. Downward-curling branches with leaves at the tips is a claim any photograph settles.
  • Andronovo dates and extent. 2200–900 BC across the Altai region is standard archaeology.

What the video does not settle

  • The relief she puts beside the tree does not show the feature she names. The whole observation is that the Sanxingdui branches curl downward where a normal tree's go up. On her own side-by-side at 09:26, the panel labelled Sumerian Tree of Life shows a single straight trunk with short branches rising upward and outward in tiers, each ending in a small fan. The downward curl is not there. The object that does share it is the Pazyryk one — and that is the one dated a thousand years too late.
  • The tree comparison breaks her own rule. She rejects the Scythians as too late and steps back to the Andronovo — correctly. But the object she compares with the bronze trees is the Pazyryk tapestry of 241 BC, roughly a thousand years after Sanxingdui ends, and no Andronovo tree of life is shown at all. The step-back is applied to the masks and not to the tree.
  • The relief is captioned Sumerian and described as Sargon. What is on screen is a bearded figure in a horned headdress with one hand raised and something held in the lowered hand, beside a tiered palmette tree — the iconography her own 2023 video treats as Neo-Assyrian, roughly 1,500 years after Sargon of Akkad. Nothing on the frame carries a museum label or date.
  • "Predate the Chinese Bronze Age by 200 years" carries the whole premise and has no source.
  • The ear-piercing claim is a negative one — that no record exists of the custom among ancient Chinese men — and negatives are the hardest to support. None is offered.
  • The comparison figurine is 1,200 years too late. The bearded, pierced, capped merchant is dated 25–220 AD, so it shows that such people were in China eventually, not that they were there in 2100 BC.
  • The withheld DNA is asserted and not sourced, here as in the metallurgy entry and the Tarim entry, where the same claim is examined at more length.
  • Her own map undercuts the foil argument. Three of the four cultures pinned on it are later than Sanxingdui's start, and two are later than its end. The map shows that gold foil masks existed in several places; it does not show a source.
  • Gold foil is a weak filter. Beating gold into sheet is one of the earliest metal techniques anywhere, and covering an object with it is a solution many cultures would reach independently. The video treats shared use of foil as evidence of relation without asking how easily it is reinvented.
  • The opening date does not match the body. "5,000–3,000 years ago" at 00:05 is about 3000–1000 BC; the figure used throughout the rest is 2100–1200 BC.
  • Nothing here is a mechanism. Even granting every resemblance, the video proposes a source region, not a route, a date of contact, or anything found at Sanxingdui that was made on the steppe.
