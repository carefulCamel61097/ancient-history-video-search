World's Largest Monolith: Yangshan Monument

Tina · 14 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-23

scaleunfinishedhowcross-continentalrock-cut-sitesquarriesmassive-stones-movedunfinished-workknobs

Yangshan, outside Nanjing in southern China, holds three man-made stone blocks that were never moved. The accepted account is that they were cut in 1405, in the Ming dynasty, for a monument an emperor wanted raised to his father, and abandoned when it became clear they could not be transported. Her position is that people working with hand tools in the early 15th century could not have cut them at all, and that the blocks are the work of a prehistoric culture with tools long since lost 00:00–00:38.

The three blocks

She uses the names the site uses 01:18–03:16.

The base is the largest: 30 m long, 13 m wide, 16 m thick, and over 16,000 tonnes — which she puts at a hundred and eighty blue whales, or something near 2,700 African elephants. Her argument that it is worked rather than natural is the sharp edges, the gap between the block and the bedrock behind it, and the smoothness of the block against the parent rock. Three rectangular openings are cut into its foot, each about the height of a person.

The largest of the three blocks, and the evidence she reads as proof it is worked rather than natural.

The body is about 50 m long and 8,800 tonnes — standing on end it would be a sixteen-storey building, taller than the Statue of Liberty. Whoever cut it removed most of the material underneath, leaving a row of blocks she describes as stool-shaped, with room for an adult to stand upright in the gaps between them 02:35–03:16. Her questions about them are the ones she says remain unanswered: why carve them out at all, why an imperial architect would agree to it if it did not serve the finished monument, and how the workers knew the mass overhead would not come down.

The feature she says nobody can explain - and what it looks like from underneath.

The head is the odd one: a rounded mass with rectangular holes cut through it, curved cuts at one end, and protruding knobs. Knobs of some kind, she says, turn up in Peru and Egypt too 03:16–03:42.

The third block, with the protrusions she connects to Peru and Egypt.

Together, on her own diagram, the three come to 31,167 tonnes — 152 times the Statue of Liberty and 91 times Levitated Mass, the 340-tonne boulder at the Los Angeles County Museum of Art, whose 100-mile move took eleven days, ten million dollars and a purpose-built 196-wheeled transporter 04:08–04:58.

The arithmetic of the scale argument, in her own figures.

What it would take today

The official account, which she quotes from Wikipedia, is that after most of the cutting was done the architects realised that moving and installing stones this size was not physically possible 04:59–05:23. Her response is that this makes the imperial architects incompetent at assessing their own project before starting it, and she does not accept that.

Her case that the job is beyond us now 05:30–06:35: the most powerful gantry crane, fixed to the ground, lifts 20,000 tonnes, and the largest mobile crane 1,200 — so even if a gantry could be mounted on the mountain, nothing could carry a block away. The largest stone block cutter with a diamond blade cuts to a depth of 3.5 m; the thinnest Yangshan block is over four. Then, over footage of the cut beneath the body: "Look at the tool marks here. Don't they look like machine marks?"

The frame the machine-mark question is asked over.

What was actually erected

The monument that does stand at the emperor's tomb is under 9 m tall and weighs about 20 tonnes 06:53–07:16. She grants that it is the largest in the Nanjing area, and sets it beside the Yangshan blocks to show there is no comparison at all.

What the same builders actually put up, beside what they left behind.

On Chinese stone-working generally she does not argue from her own judgement but quotes Liang Sicheng, the architect and architectural historian who surveyed ancient Chinese buildings with his wife in the early 20th century, and whose History of Chinese Architecture concluded that Chinese builders worked mainly in wood, never developed a sufficient grasp of stone mechanics, and used stone as though it were timber, which does not hold up in freestanding structures 07:16–08:32.

She adds a structural point about form: in 15th-century China a monument of this kind was made of two stones, the head and body cut as one piece and the base separate. On that reading the three-block reconstruction is wrong about the object it claims to reconstruct 09:14–09:35.

The documentary case

This is the strongest section, and it is archival rather than geological 09:35–11:35.

The project is not mentioned once in the chronicles of the Ming dynasty, which she describes as written by imperial scholars in great detail — and an emperor's direct order, she argues, would have been recorded. The reference Wikipedia rests on is a poem written in the 18th century, three hundred years after the fact.

The one contemporary source modern historians have found is a scholar's diary of 1405, displayed on a wall at the site. Two things about it matter to her: it describes one block, not three; and it says the great stone was found at Yangshan quarry rather than quarried there. The same writer, she says, described other stones at Yangshan as natural formations.

The single contemporary source, as it is displayed at the site.

Then the site plan. On an aerial view of the park, the quarry lies immediately beside the base — but the body and the head are elsewhere, and she says there are no signs of quarrying near them 11:09–11:35.

Where she takes it

If the Ming could not have cut the blocks, the question is who did 11:35–12:47. She returns to the knobs, which she reports at Yangshan, in Peru and in Egypt, and asks whether the sites are connected.

The cross-continental claim, with all three panels visible at once.

From there: Plato's Timaeus on the many deluges the world has forgotten; recent evidence, unnamed, that Atlantis was a real civilisation destroyed by a flood perhaps 12,000 years ago; and the suggestion that the Yangshan blocks may be Atlantean, or the work of some other prehistoric culture that left megalithic sites worldwide.

She closes by saying this is her first video, and trails a second one on the Yangshan tool marks and their resemblance to marks at Baalbek, Petra and in India 12:52–13:29.

What you can check yourself

  • The site is a public park and the blocks are photographable. The features the argument turns on — the sharp edges, the gap behind the base, the openings, the row of supports under the body, the knobs on the head — are all visible in ordinary visitor photographs.
  • Her weight figures are on screen and add up. 16,250 for the base, 8,799 for the body, 6,118 for the head, 31,167 in total. The arithmetic is hers and it is correct.
  • The erected monument can be compared directly. It stands at the emperor's tomb complex, and whether it is a stele on a separate carved base — two stones, not three — is a question about an object anyone can walk up to. Her own comparison frame shows it standing on a carved tortoise plinth.
  • The Levitated Mass numbers are public. 340 tonnes, eleven days, a hundred miles, a 196-wheeled transporter, and the museum published the cost.
  • Crane capacities are manufacturers' published specifications, and so is the cutting depth of a diamond block saw.
  • Liang Sicheng's book exists and is translated. What he argued about stone and timber, and what he was arguing about, can be read rather than taken second-hand.
  • The 1405 text is on a wall at the site. The panel is titled 游陽山記, "A Record of a Journey to Yangshan", and attributed to 胡廣 of the Ming. Its opening column carries the date 永樂三年秋 — autumn of the third year of Yongle, 1405 — and, as far as the frame can be read, a phrase naming the emperor and the raising of a stele at Xiaoling, the tomb complex. It is quoted in the video but never translated, and anyone reading Chinese can check what it says against what she says it says.

What the video does not settle

  • Her own frames answer the question she calls unanswered. She asks why anyone would carve out the stool-shaped blocks under the body. In the footage, they are a row of narrow supports left standing in a long undercut, with the mass resting on them — which is what an unfinished extraction looks like when the block has been trenched around, undercut, and not yet dropped. The video does not state that reading, or say why it should be rejected.
  • "No signs of quarrying" is said over a frame that shows the cut. The aerial of the body has the block sitting in a rectangular pit with sheer worked faces on three sides of it. Whether she means there is no separate spoil quarry nearby is not clear, and the image does not support the words as spoken.
  • The crane comparison uses the assembled weight. 31,167 tonnes is the finished monument; the blocks would have been moved one at a time, and the heaviest is 16,250 against the 20,000-tonne gantry figure she gives herself. The transport problem is real without the arithmetic being stacked.
  • The saw comparison sets different operations against each other. A single-pass diamond blade cutting through a slab is not the same job as trenching around a block from above and undercutting it from the sides, which is how these blocks were being freed, and which is why they are still attached.
  • Liang Sicheng was writing about building with stone, not extracting it. Whether a tradition raises freestanding stone structures and whether it can quarry a large block are separate competences, and the video moves from the first to the second without an argument.
  • The absence from the chronicles is asserted and not demonstrated. No edition, no search, no citation. The claim is checkable in principle and is not checked here.
  • The 18th-century poem is dismissed without being quoted, so a reader cannot judge what it does or does not claim.
  • The "found, not quarried" reading is a translation argument conducted without a translation. The text is shown as an image, never transcribed or rendered into English, and the difference she is resting on is a difference between two Chinese words that the audience is not shown.
  • The knobs comparison puts three different things side by side. The Egyptian panel shows shallow crescent-shaped bosses on the face of a dressed wall block; the Peruvian shows tapered conical nubs projecting from fine coursed masonry; the Yangshan feature is a pair of large irregular horn-like masses on the flank of an unfinished monolith. They differ in shape, in size and in what they are attached to.
  • The tool-mark claim is made over a wide shot. No individual mark is resolvable in the frame where she asks whether they look like machine marks. Four years later, on footage of the same cut-out, she describes the marks there as sharp chisel or pickaxe work with crude edges — see below.
  • Atlantis arrives without evidence. Plato is quoted on deluges; the "new evidence" for Atlantis as a real civilisation is not named, dated or sourced, and nothing offered connects it to Nanjing.
  • The strong observation does not need the conclusion. Nobody has moved sixteen thousand tonnes of stone, before or since, and an abandoned attempt at three of them is a real object worth explaining. That holds whoever cut them and whenever.

What we have not checked

  • The Chinese text has not been translated or transcribed in full. The title 游陽山記, the attribution to 胡廣, the date 永樂三年秋 and a phrase naming the emperor and a stele at Xiaoling are read off a single frame of a wall panel photographed at an angle. That reading is medium confidence and should be confirmed against a printed edition before it is repeated anywhere. It matters, because it bears directly on her claim about what the source does and does not connect.
  • Nothing in the documentary case has been verified. Whether the chronicles of the Ming are silent on the project, what the 18th-century poem says, and whether the 1405 text uses a word meaning "found" rather than "quarried" are all as she gives them.
  • The tonnages, dimensions and crane and saw capacities are as stated and have not been traced: 16,250, 8,799 and 6,118 tonnes; 30 × 13 × 16 m; roughly 50 m; the 20,000-tonne gantry and 1,200-tonne mobile crane; the 3.5 m blade depth; and the Levitated Mass figures.
  • Liang Sicheng's conclusion is quoted at second hand. The passage is characterised, not shown, and The History of Chinese Architecture has not been consulted.
  • The claim that 15th-century monuments were made of two stones rather than three is hers and has not been checked against surviving examples.
  • The emperor is never named in the video and is not named here. The tomb complex is identifiable as Ming Xiaoling from the text panel and from her footage of the erected monument; that identification is inferred, not heard.
  • The Egyptian and Peruvian panels in the knobs comparison are not located. Neither is captioned on screen, and the Peruvian wall is not identified here beyond being fine coursed masonry.
