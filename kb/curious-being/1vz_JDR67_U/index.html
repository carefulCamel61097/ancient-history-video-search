Sumerian Handbags, Pinecones & Tree of Life: The Hidden Code of Ancient Gods

Tina · 19 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-23

deflatedmainstreamwhycross-continentalshared-symbolshandbags

The winged genies of Mesopotamia hold a handbag-like object in one hand and a pine cone in the other, often flanking a stylised sacred tree. The bags have been called the handbags of the Anunnaki, the handbags of the gods. The theories she lists at the top 00:00–01:38: that the bag represents the cosmos, its semicircular strap the dome of the sky and its square base the earth; that the tree is cannabis and the bags hold hallucinogens; that if the Anunnaki were aliens who taught humans technology, as Zecharia Sitchin proposed, the bags and cones are their tools; that the cone is the pineal gland where the soul sits. And the historical reading: that the bags are buckets, and the bucket-and-cone pair belongs to Mesopotamian ritual.

She proposes to say what the objects are, what was in the bags, and what the tree of life was.

The image the whole debate is about.

It is the wrong figure

Her first move is a correction that removes the premise 01:40–03:22. The bags and cones are not carried by the Anunnaki. The Anunnaki were seven major heavenly deities of immense power and they do not carry bags or cones — so if they were aliens, their power did not come from the bag.

The winged genies that carry them are Apkallu: sage or expert in Akkadian, associated with wisdom, depicted as fish-man hybrids, bird-headed or winged human figures. They are the Seven Sages, whose job was to bring knowledge and learning to humans as teachers and advisers. Semi-divine, and not even counted as minor gods.

Two more things she notes about them, and the second is the sharper. Apkallu also hold sprigs, sceptres with various heads, a string of beads, and a baby goat. And the bag and cone are not always paired — sometimes a genie carries only a bag, the other hand making a gesture. So: if the handbag is the cosmos, what does the baby goat mean, and why is the great symbol of the cosmos attached to a minor figure rather than to the gods?

Then the chronology

The region ran for about five thousand years through Sumerians, Akkadians, Assyrians and Babylonians, sharing and repurposing each other's ideas. Sumer was established some 6,500 years ago. The earliest known record of the Apkallu is in the Epic of Gilgamesh, written 4,100–3,200 years ago. The great winged-genie carvings are Neo-Assyrian, 9th century BC — about 2,900 years ago. So at least 300 and possibly 1,200 years separate the first documentation of the Apkallu from the carvings everyone argues about 03:22–04:55.

That gap is her method: read each period in its own context, because the idea of the sages changed.

The Neo-Assyrian context

In the late reliefs, the Apkallu appear in three situations 04:55–05:29: at the corners and gates of rooms alongside the protective lamassu; flanking a sacred tree with a bag, holding a cone towards the tree's flowers; and holding the cone close to the king or another person.

Records of the period tie them to apotropaic ritual — the sage Adapa's name was invoked in exorcism. And the objects have Akkadian names: the bag is a banduddu, which means bucket; the cone is a mullilu, which means purifier 05:29–05:50.

What the people who carved the reliefs called the object.

The standard reading follows from that: the cone was dipped in the bucket and used to sprinkle liquid over the king, his court and his palace to purify them, which is why the figures stand at doorways and in corners where evil was thought to gather, and why plaques of them were kept in houses 05:50–06:27.

But the earlier images show something else

She accepts the purification reading for those scenes and then asks about the ones with the tree 06:27–07:44.

In one relief two Apkallu appear to be tending a young tree with no ritual items at all. And there is a long Mesopotamian tradition of figures facing a central plant: the temptation seal of 2200–2100 BC with two seated figures either side of a tree; a seal impression of the 15th–13th century BC with two beings flanking one; and an unusual seal of the 12th–11th century BC in which a bird-headed Apkallu stands on one branch while grabbing and twisting another — which does not look like protecting a tree.

The earlier image she reads against the ritual interpretation, with its date.

That seal, she notes, is more than two hundred years older than the protective Apkallu in the Assyrian palace, and may preserve the earlier Sumerian idea better.

Which is borne out by the text 07:44–08:22: in the Epic of Gilgamesh the Seven Sages built the walls of Uruk — surveyed the foundations, examined the brickwork. Practical, down-to-earth wisdom, fitting the original notion of sages as the teachers of humankind. So the genies tending trees might be doing something useful, and what depends on what kind of tree it is.

The tree

The Neo-Assyrian sacred tree has many versions, mostly stylised into flowering nodes and crisscrossing vines, but the centre never changes: a single unbranched trunk with a dramatic crown of fan-shaped fronds 08:22–09:02. That, she says, is a palm — and specifically a date palm.

What stays the same across every version of the sacred tree - and what does not.

Her supporting evidence is that other reliefs of the same period show date palms constantly, with their hanging fruit: in the king's garden, over soldiers and horses, in the groves where offerings are brought. And the much earlier seals show them too — the temptation seal's tree is a date palm with fruit on it, and the Apkallu grabbing a branch is grabbing a frond. The unbranched spiky trunk, the erect crown and the dangling fruit are the signature 09:02–09:58.

The tree on the earliest seals set against the real thing.

Why the date palm mattered

The middle of the video is agricultural economics, and it is the part that makes the conclusion land 09:58–13:32.

The date palm has been the most prominent native species of Mesopotamia for millennia; Herodotus in the 5th century BC wrote that palms grew in great numbers over the whole flat country. The region is the domestication centre of the earliest crops, including the oldest cultivated fruit; there is evidence of people eating wild dates in the Near East 8,000 years ago.

Date palms are dioecious — each tree is male or female. The males bear no fruit but produce the pollen that the wind carries to the females. The Sumerians worked this out, realised that one male produces enough pollen for 50 to 100 females, replaced most of the males in their groves with females, and began hand pollination by at least 3300 BC. The same operation is still done the same way across the Middle East and North Africa; some growers keep no male trees at all and buy male flowers at market at pollination time. Artificial pollination, she argues, was the turning point in Mesopotamian agriculture, because it economised on water, land and labour at once.

The tree earns its place economically. It fruits from year five and lives to 150; a mature tree yields up to 140 kg per season; the fruit is high in sugar, resists spoilage and dried dates keep for years, which mattered enormously before refrigeration; boiled and strained it makes honey — the biblical land of milk and honey means date honey, not bees' — and it can be harvested through the dry season. And every part is used: leaves and timber for baskets, sandals, houses and temples, fibre and thread for mattresses and rope.

She quotes the Assyriologist A. Leo Oppenheim: "No other fruit tree has received similar attention… Only in groves of date palms did the garden play an important economic role in Mesopotamia. The date palm was the only essential fruit tree in the region."

The answer

Back to the genie at the tree 13:40–15:33.

The ones holding a cone to the tree's flowers look like they are pollinating a female flower. Pines, including Turkish pines, are not native to that hot arid plain, so the so-called pine cones are in all likelihood not pine cones. Examined closely, the cones held by the Apkallu resemble the male flower of the date palm, which is also cone-shaped.

What the cone is, tested against the thing it is said to be.

Modern date farms still employ experienced workers to apply pollen by hand. Sometimes they shake bundles of male flowers over the female ones; more often they collect the pollen into a cloth, a bucket or a bag, carry it up the tree, and dab an applier into it.

That a bucket of pollen is a real object a real worker carries.

So the bags the Apkallu carry near trees probably held pollen, and the bag-and-cone-at-the-flower scene is a depiction of artificial pollination. The embellished outer parts of the stylised tree may represent the protective covers put over young offshoots — wired nets, hessian wrappings, tents of date leaves against sun, wind, cold and animals. And the seal of the Apkallu twisting a palm leaf may be a picture of trimming, which is routine.

Her synthesis is that both readings are right for their own period 15:33–16:45. In Sumerian times the Apkallu were teachers who supervised city walls and possibly invented artificial pollination. By the 9th century BC their role had shifted to the ritual and apotropaic, and the bucket then held water or some other liquid. "As a bag or a bucket, it is designed to carry all kinds of things. Pollen or water; it really depends on the specific jobs these sages were supposed to do."

The coda

The sprinkling survived 17:01–18:42. The Torah describes taking hyssop, dipping it in water and sprinkling it as part of a Jewish rite. The Christian aspergillum does the same in liturgy. So do Buddhist and Hindu ceremonies — near-identical rituals with local plants and instruments. Holy water is agreed to predate Christianity, and Christianity began in Jerusalem, on the Fertile Crescent and connected to Mesopotamia. She proposes, without claiming it, that the sprinkling rite may be Mesopotamian in origin, and asks whether the flower sprigs the Apkallu carry are the ancestors of the hyssop.

The gesture, still being made.

What you can check yourself

  • The Akkadian words are on screen. Banduddu, bucket, and mullilu, purifier. Those are dictionary entries and anyone can look them up. They are also the strongest single piece of evidence in the whole handbag debate, because they are what the people who carved the reliefs called the objects.
  • The other things the Apkallu carry are in the same reliefs. Sprigs, sceptres, beads, a goat. Any reading of the bag as a cosmic symbol has to say what those are.
  • The seals are dated and labelled on screen. The Middle Assyrian cylinder seal she rests the earlier reading on is captioned South Mesopotamia, 12th–11th century BC.
  • The cone comparison is a photograph against a relief and can be judged directly. An opened male date-palm spathe is a boat-shaped sheath packed with rows of tiny cream flowers; the carved object is an ovoid covered in rows of small rounded bosses. They are considerably more alike than either is to a pine cone.
  • Hand pollination is filmed and photographed constantly because it is a working practice, not a reconstruction. Her frames show a bucket of pollen and a cloth bundle puffing pollen over a flower spike.
  • The date palm figures are agricultural literature: dioecy, one male to 50–100 females, first fruit at five years, up to 140 kg a season, a 150-year life.
  • Oppenheim is a real Assyriologist and the quotation is specific enough to find.
  • Herodotus on the palms of Babylonia is in Book I of the Histories.

What the video does not settle

  • Her own best evidence points at the ritual reading, and she splits it by scene. Mullilu means purifier. That is what the object was called, and a name attaches to the object rather than to the situation it is used in. The video keeps the purification reading for the doorway scenes and the pollination reading for the tree scenes without saying why one Akkadian noun should cover two different implements.
  • The pollination reading is presented as her own and may not be. She says at the start that she will give "my theories", and credits the ritual reading to historians. A date-palm fertilisation reading of the bucket and cone has been argued in Assyriology before, and whether this is a rediscovery or an original proposal is not addressed here and matters for how much weight it carries.
  • The sacred tree is stylised past the point where the identification is safe. Her own two drawings show a banded, columnar trunk under a palmette crown, framed in a lattice of rosettes. Neither carries dates. The reading rests on the trunk being unbranched and the crown fanning, which is true of the drawings and also true of a palmette, which is an ornament.
  • The naturalistic date palms are a different class of image from the stylised tree. That Assyrian reliefs show real date palms in gardens and groves establishes that they knew what a date palm looked like. It does not establish that the stylised tree is one.
  • The cone comparison is made at one scale only. The relief detail is a close-up; the male flower is a close-up. Neither is shown in the whole scene, so how large the carved object is relative to the figure holding it — which is what would settle whether it is a spathe or something else — is never shown.
  • The 3300 BC date for hand pollination is asserted with no source, and it is the earliest and most load-bearing date in the argument.
  • The chronology argument cuts both ways. If 300 to 1,200 years separate the Sumerian sages from the Assyrian carvings, and meanings changed across that gap, then reading the Assyrian carvings back through Gilgamesh is exposed to the same objection she raises against everyone else.
  • The seal of the Apkallu "twisting a branch" is interpreted twice. It is first offered as evidence that the action is not protective, and later as possibly trimming. Neither reading is argued from anything in the image beyond the posture.
  • The Oppenheim quotation has no book named, and it is read over a stock photograph of a date grove rather than over the page.
  • The coda is speculation and is flagged as such, but the argument in it is only geographical proximity. Sprinkling water is a near-universal ritual gesture, and shared descent from Mesopotamia is one explanation among several that the video does not weigh.
  • Nothing in the video is located. No relief is identified by palace, room or museum, and the cuneiform carved across several of them — which would name the king and the building — is never mentioned.
