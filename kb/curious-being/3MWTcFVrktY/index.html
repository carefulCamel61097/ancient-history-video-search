The Forgotten Purpose of the Pyramids' Black and White Casings

Tina · 24 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-23

deflateddatingmainstreamtwo-industriespyramid-constructionwater-erosion-and-weatheringquarries

Two kinds of casing survive on the Egyptian pyramids: white limestone — on top of Khafre, in patches at the base of Khufu, and most extensively on the Bent Pyramid at Dahshur — and dark granite, at the base of Khafre and over the lower portion of Menkaure 00:00–01:05. Khafre still carries both.

Her two questions: why use two materials, and did the pyramids ever look the way everyone pictures them?

What the historians actually wrote

This is the strongest section and it is documentary work 01:12–06:02.

The earliest surviving descriptions of the Giza pyramids come from Herodotus and Strabo, over 2,000 years ago. Both record the dark bottom casings on Khafre. Neither mentions white casings. Her point: an entire smooth white pyramid throwing back desert sun would be worth writing about, and no Greek scholar wrote about it.

Herodotus on the Great Pyramid: "the whole is of stone polished and most exactly fitted… This pyramid was made like a stairway with tiers, or steps." She reads the two halves against each other — polished and exactly fitted sounds like intact casing, but stairway with tiers means the smooth surface was already gone in the 5th century BC. The polished stones are most likely the bottom casings still visible today.

Diodorus Siculus, 1st century BC, called the structure undecayed and reported the dimensions and stones of the pyramid's top layer. Her observation: that knowledge requires climbing it, and climbing Khufu is only possible once the smooth casing is mostly gone and the tiered core stones are exposed to grip and stand on.

What the historians were describing, and what they were not: smooth casing at the bottom, bare steps above it.

Then the claim that the casings survived until 1650, attributed to the astronomer John Greaves. She read his Pyramidographia (1646), and reports that Greaves does not say this — he neither records the great pyramids as fully cased nor cites any earlier source that does. On Khafre he writes that its sides "rise not with degrees (aka steps) like [Khufu], but are all of them plain and smooth… seeming very entire, free from any deformed ruptures or breaches", which reads as if Khafre's casing were largely intact.

And then she does the thing that makes the section worth keeping: she checks it against a picture. An engraving from **George Sandys, A Relation of a Journey begun An: Dom: 1610, made a generation before Greaves went to Egypt, shows the Giza pyramids looking much as they do now — the nearer one drawn in stepped courses, the further one smooth-sided, and the Sphinx buried to the neck. Her conclusion is about method as much as about pyramids: when verbal descriptions are not specific, they mislead.**

The picture that checks the texts - what a traveller drew before the century in which the casings are supposed to have survived.

The lighting problem

She presses the same point against her own kind of evidence 07:31–09:02.

There is an alleged quotation of Thales, who visited Egypt, describing the pyramids as "white as teeth, shining in the sun, with golden apex". She treats it as artistic expression — a pyramid under glaring desert sun looking white and gold-tipped.

Her demonstration case is Greaves again. He wrote that Menkaure "seems to be of clear and white stone, somewhat choicer and brighter than that in either of the two other pyramids" — and, because Herodotus, Diodorus and Strabo all described Menkaure as coloured with black stone, Greaves doubted whether any of them had actually been there.

The Greeks were right. Menkaure is darker, because of the red granite casings on its lower portion. Greaves, standing in front of it, was the one misled — by the light. Her conclusion: eyewitness accounts of how the pyramids looked depend on the conditions the witness stood in.

The zigzag joints

Her own observation, and the pivot of the video 09:02–10:56.

At the base of Khafre, the granite casings carry wedge marks where later stone robbers tried to cut them away. Looking at them she noticed something else: the join between the granite casing and the limestone core behind it is not straight but zigzag. The granite was made to fit into an uneven back surface, so many of those casing blocks had to be custom cut to match whatever shape the core presented.

Every other pyramid stone is a rectangular block. So why are these irregular? She rejects the stone robbers as the cause — the surfaces lack wedge holes and saw marks, and the shapes are random and organic rather than worked.

The observation the video turns on: casing cut to fit a shape that was already there.

Her answer: the builders did not make them uneven. Weathering did. The core stones eroded into those shapes over a long period, and the granite was added afterwards, cut to fit the damage, to protect what was left. Which means the granite is not part of the original design but repair — and that a significant interval separates the original casing from the repair, because the erosion had to accumulate.

The same custom-fitted granite over irregular limestone appears at Menkaure, with the same ragged joint lines.

Which erosion, and where

The argument for what did the damage 11:33–17:22.

The granite is at the bottom of the pyramids, so the worst erosion was near ground level. She argues that points to wind: while wind carries dust higher, the abrasive work is done near the ground by sand grains bouncing along the surface. The classic result is the mushroom rock — narrow smooth base, broader top. The Bent Pyramid, which has the most intact white casing of any, is missing its casing precisely at the bottom.

The same pattern elsewhere on the plateau: the Menkaure mortuary temple pathway clad in black basalt over limestone eroded near ground level; Khafre's Valley Temple and the Sphinx Temple with granite casings over eroded limestone cores. She reads all of these as the work of one maintenance effort.

Then a discriminator, which is the most careful reasoning in the video 14:31–15:56. Two erosion processes are visible at Giza: the deep vertical water erosion on the Sphinx enclosure walls, and horizontal wind erosion on the Sphinx body and the temple cores. In a semi-arid climate, water attacks limestone chiefly by chemical weathering — slightly acidic rainwater dissolving calcium carbonate — which starts at the top of a formation where rain first lands, and leaves rough, pitted, honeycombed surfaces. Plenty of blocks at Giza look like that, and their worst damage is on their upper surfaces.

But the Khafre ground-level limestone next to the granite casings is not pitted, and shows no grooves. So it was not weathered by rain. It was weathered dry.

The discriminator her dating argument depends on: where on a block the worst damage is.

Where the argument runs into itself

She then adds an altitude argument 16:03–17:22. Wind speed rises with height above ground, so the two temples by the Sphinx at about 20 m above sea level should have seen far less wind than the pyramids on the plateau — yet they show severe wind erosion, which means the pyramids endured more. Among the three, Menkaure stands highest at 73 m and has the most granite casing; Khufu stands lowest at 60 m and has none. She adds that the three satellite pyramids on Khufu's east side may have sheltered it.

Her reconstruction

Where it all lands 17:22–23:27.

She takes it as established by scientific studies that the original limestone casings and some core stones are artificial limestone concrete, which dynastic and predynastic Egyptians could not produce — so the pyramids predate them.

Her sequence: the pyramids built with smooth synthetic white limestone casings during a relatively humid period, shedding rainwater; the Sphinx basin dug at the same time as a stormwater catch basin with the two temples as drainage treatment. Then the climate turns cold, dry and windy. Wind strips the lower casings, the exposed cores erode, temperature and moisture swings crack the stone. A maintenance crew clads the worst damage in granite — Khafre's base, Menkaure's lower portion, and both temples by the Sphinx.

So the pyramids have lived through a full climate cycle from humid to arid, and were maintained across it. Her estimate: over 12,000 years old. The white casings were likely gone before the Egyptians arrived, which fits her separate argument that the gypsum mortar dated by radiocarbon is their patchwork rather than original construction.

She also notes something she finds telling: if the builders were as sophisticated as she believes, they would have run the equivalent of wind tunnel tests and used durable casing at the bottom from the start. They did not — which she reads as evidence that the wind was not a problem when the pyramids were designed, and arrived later.

What you can check yourself

  • The primary sources are named and quotable. Herodotus, Diodorus, Strabo and Greaves are all in print, and whether any of them describes a fully cased pyramid is a question anyone can settle by reading them.
  • The Herodotus passage does the most work and is three lines long. "Made like a stairway with tiers, or steps" either means the casings were gone in the 5th century BC or it does not.
  • The Sandys engraving is a published book plate, named on screen, and can be found and compared with a photograph taken today.
  • Greaves' error about Menkaure is checkable in his own text against the pyramid's surviving granite, and it is the cleanest demonstration in the corpus that eyewitness colour descriptions are unreliable.
  • The zigzag joints are on the pyramids, in daylight, and photographable by anyone who goes.
  • Whether those irregular surfaces carry wedge holes and saw marks — which would indicate robbers rather than weathering — is a close-up photograph away.
  • Pitted, honeycombed limestone against smooth undercut limestone are visually distinct and the distinction is standard weathering geology.
  • The elevations of the three pyramids and the two temples are surveyed figures.
  • The Bent Pyramid's missing bottom casing is visible in any photograph of it.

What the video does not settle

  • The two wind arguments pull against each other. The mushroom-rock argument says abrasion is worst near the ground because sand saltates close to the surface. The altitude argument says higher is windier, therefore more eroded. Those are different mechanisms, and the second does not follow from the first — faster wind carrying less sand does not abrade more.
  • The altitude evidence contradicts the altitude rule. The Sphinx temples at about 20 m are said to show severe wind erosion while Khufu at 60 m has no granite casing at all, which is the opposite of what the rule predicts. Thirteen metres between Menkaure and Khufu is also a trivial difference on one plateau, and the satellite-pyramid shelter offered for Khufu is introduced only once it is needed.
  • Granite over eroded limestone has an ordinary reading too. Cladding damaged stone is what repair looks like in any period, including a dynastic one. That the granite is later than the core is well supported by the zigzag joints; that it is later by millennia is not.
  • The absence of pitting is a good discriminator used at one place only. It is applied to the Khafre ground-level blocks and not to the Bent Pyramid, the Menkaure temple pathway or the two Sphinx temples, all of which are folded into the same story.
  • The "they would have anticipated it" argument assumes what is in dispute. Reasoning that sophisticated builders would have designed for wind, so the wind must have come later, requires the builders' sophistication as a premise — and its conclusion is precisely the climate change the dating rests on.
  • The eyewitness caution is not applied evenly. She establishes carefully that verbal descriptions mislead and that Greaves was fooled by the light, then builds the first half of her case on what Herodotus, Diodorus and Strabo did and did not write.
  • A 1615 engraving is weak evidence about surface texture. The Sandys plate is a stylised landscape with riders in the foreground, and its pyramids are rendered in conventional hatching. That one reads as stepped and another as smooth is consistent with her claim, but an engraver's shorthand is not a survey, and the video treats the picture as settling what the text left open.
  • Absence of mention is not absence of casing. That no Greek source describes a white pyramid is suggestive, but ancient authors omit a great deal, and the Herodotus passage is doing nearly all the positive work.
  • The concrete claim is imported and load-bearing. "Several studies have proven" that the Khufu and Bent Pyramid casings are reconstituted limestone distinct from natural Tura stone — no study is named, and the step from there to "therefore not dynastic Egyptians" is the hinge of the whole dating argument.

What we have not checked

  • **John Greaves' Pyramidographia (1646) has not been read here.** Her account of what it does and does not say, and the two passages quoted from it, are as she presents them — and the claim that it does not support the intact-casing story is the section's most important assertion.
  • The Herodotus, Diodorus and Strabo passages are quoted in translation from screen and have not been checked against their sources; nor has the claim that Strabo records the dark casings.
  • The Thales quotation is flagged as alleged by the presenter and remains unsourced.
  • The 1610 engraving is attributed on screen to George Sandys, A Relation of a Journey begun An: Dom: 1610, but the plate has not been traced to a copy of the book, and no engraver or edition is given.
  • Ibn Abd Alhorkm, the 9th-century Arabian scholar quoted on whether the pyramids predate the flood, is named as the auto-captions render him and the spelling has not been confirmed.
  • The claim that several studies proved the Khufu and Bent Pyramid casings are reconstituted limestone distinct from Tura stone is asserted with no study named, here or anywhere in this corpus.
  • The elevations — Menkaure at 73 m, Khufu at 60 m, the temples at about 20 m above sea level — are as stated and have not been traced.
  • Whether the irregular surfaces at Khafre's base genuinely lack wedge holes and saw marks has not been verified beyond her statement and the frames shown.
