Israel's Land of 1,000 Caves: evidence of a prehistoric advanced civilization

Tina · 17 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-23

howwhycross-continentalmainstreamrock-cut-sitesquarriesmachining-and-tool-marks

Beit Guvrin-Maresha is a UNESCO site and a 1,250-acre national park in the Judean lowlands, with over 2,500 years of history and a name that translates as the city under a city. The southern part is Tel Maresha, the mound of the biblical city of the First Temple period, and all the man-made caves are there — over 800 bell caves, chambers and tunnels, many linked by underground networks connecting groups of forty or fifty. Maresha was destroyed by the Parthians in 40 BC, after which Beit Guvrin succeeded it and became the Roman town of Eleutheropolis, whose amphitheatre survives 00:00–01:35.

The caves are cut into a thick, homogeneous layer of soft chalk. The accepted account is that they were quarries, later reused for olive presses, columbaria, stables, cisterns, baths, tombs, places of worship and refuges 01:35–02:11.

She takes two kinds of cave: the bell caves and the columbaria.

The bell caves

Over 60 ft — 18 m — high, airy, cool in summer. The main complex has seventy connected bell-shaped caves, taken to be quarries for mortar and plaster stone and for lime. Arabic inscriptions on the walls, she reports, indicate roughly two thousand years of activity. Some have collapsed; that many have not she finds impressive 02:11–02:55.

What a bell cave is, and how big.

The accepted quarrying method, which she sets out 02:55–03:26: open a small hole in the hard grey limestone at ground level; on reaching the soft chalk beneath, enlarge it; carry on until each bell eventually cuts into the ones beside it; raise the rectangular blocks out with ropes.

How the site itself says the caves were made - and what that answers.

Her verdict on it is that the caves could well be quarries but the excavation theory does not sound convincing to her, and her questions follow 03:26–04:25. Open-pit quarrying is easier and safer, so why go underground when other quarries nearby did not? Why the bell shape? A bell is close to a structurally sound parabolic dome, which needs calculation and planning — how did the workers know it would not come down on them? And what were they using for light?

She sets the bell caves against open pits and lime pits of about the same period, and reads the difference as technique: the bell caves are strikingly more advanced.

The comparison offered to show the bell caves are more advanced than ordinary quarrying.

The tool marks

The caves differ from one another in every other respect, she says, but all carry the same large, uniform parallel tool marks — the same ones she has pointed out at Baalbek, Petra, Yangshan and Longyou 04:25–05:41. Her position, argued in an earlier video and asserted here, is that ridge and groove marks of this size and regularity resemble modern machine marks and cannot be made by hand.

Her own phrasing of it is worth quoting because of what it concedes: manual labour cannot achieve this consistency "even on stone that is soft enough to dig with fingernails". We could not draw such marks that consistently, she says, let alone carve them at this scale.

The marks she says hand tools cannot make, at a size where a stroke can be seen.

The columbaria

The niched caves are dated to the third century BC. A Roman columbarium held urns of cremated remains — but no ash has been found at Maresha, and cremation was not the local burial practice, so the niches were for something else 05:41–06:18.

The accepted answer is doves and pigeons; columba is Latin for dove. The park has 85 columbaria and pigeons still live in them. She notes the so-called Polish cave, a cistern later reused as a columbarium, where Polish soldiers cut an inscription in 1943. Doves were kept everywhere in the ancient world for meat, eggs and droppings for fertiliser 06:18–07:09.

How large a columbarium is, and how many niches are in one.

Maresha is not alone. She lists Luzit, Beit Lehi and Adullam-France Park nearby, all with the same parallel tool marks, all with rows of niches, all connected by tunnels into what amount to underground cities. At Luzit some niches sit higher up the wall, which she suggests may mean certain bell caves were cut with niches from the start. The central roof opening and the scale remind her of the Pantheon, which she suggests may have been inspired by caves like these 07:09–08:20.

The niched caves turn out to be widespread — Jordan, Turkey, Italy, France. She found a paper online giving evidence for pigeon breeding in Jordanian columbaria, and points to Pigeon Valley in Cappadocia, named for the dovecotes cut into its cliffs. The niche shapes differ slightly; the composition, she says, is much the same 08:20–09:10.

The niches close to, where the regularity and the cutting can be judged.

Her qualification is the pivot of the video 09:10–09:44: none of that proves the columbaria were originally designed for pigeons, nor that they or the bell caves are 2,500 years old. If the caves show machine marks and were cut by an earlier civilisation, then the pigeon farms may belong to that civilisation too.

Pigeons

The rest is about domestication 09:44–15:02. The rock dove was the first bird domesticated and nests naturally on cliffs and in caves, which is why the birds return to a columbarium they could fly out of. Mesopotamian tablets and Egyptian hieroglyphs record pigeon-keeping more than 5,000 years ago, and research she does not name suggests domestication as early as 10,000 years ago on the Tigris–Euphrates plains.

She is careful about the distinction: domestication is not taming. A tamed wild animal stays genetically wild and its offspring are not born tame; domestic animals have been selectively bred and genetically adapted over generations, as dogs are from wolves. Domestication is tied to the shift from hunting and gathering to farming, and scholars treat it as a process lasting millennia.

Her example is well chosen and cuts against the easy version. In 1959 a Soviet biologist began breeding silver foxes and claimed domestication within a decade; a recent review found that overstated — the foxes had been selectively bred for tame and subservient behaviour, not domesticated. There is, she says, no consensus on how animals were domesticated, and the current theories hold only until the next dig.

Then the fork 13:34–15:02. If pigeons were domesticated 10,000 years ago, either our ancestors ran consistent selective breeding generation after generation — and the earliest known writing is 4,700 years old, 5,300 years after the practice began, so the method must have passed by speech alone, which she calls incredible — or domesticated animals were genetically altered by an earlier advanced civilisation. She flags the second as unusual and far-fetched, but technologically possible, and consistent with the same civilisation cutting the caves.

Her three-point summary 15:30–16:32: the tool marks resemble machine marks and require advanced technology; the bell caves show sophisticated engineering; and columbaria for pigeons mean pigeons were already domesticated when the caves were cut. If the caves are prehistoric, so perhaps is the domestication, and perhaps it was done by manipulation.

What you can check yourself

  • The site is a national park and everything the argument turns on is open to visitors: the bell cave domes and their roof openings, the tool marks on the walls, the niches, the connecting tunnels.
  • The site's own explanation is on a panel she photographs. It is a drawn section headed BELL CAVES showing four bells in the ground: a hard hatched layer at the surface, a narrow neck cut through it, the bell widening downwards into stippled soft chalk, and at the right a tripod and rope over the shaft with workers at the bottom. The panel can be read directly.
  • The chalk is soft, and she says so. Whether it can be worked with hand tools is settled by handling it, and her own words are that it is soft enough to dig with fingernails.
  • The Arabic inscriptions are on the walls of the bell caves and are photographable. What script and what period they belong to is decidable by anyone who can read them, and it bears directly on when the bell caves were being worked.
  • The pigeons are still there. The park has 85 columbaria and the birds still use them.
  • The Polish inscription of 1943 is dated on the stone.
  • The Farm Fox review is real published work and can be read against the original claim.
  • The comparison sites are all visitable — Luzit, Beit Lehi, Adullam, and Pigeon Valley in Cappadocia.

What the video does not settle

  • The panel she shows answers the questions she then asks. Why underground: the good material is the soft chalk, and it lies beneath a hard limestone cap, so an open pit would mean stripping the cap first. Why the bell shape: it is what you get by cutting a narrow shaft through the cap and widening as you go down. How did they know it would not collapse: on the drawing they are not building a dome at all — they work downwards, always standing on rock not yet removed, never under a span they have made. The video puts the diagram on screen, narrates the method from it, and then says the theory is unconvincing.
  • The hardness argument disappears in her own sentence. Chalk soft enough to dig with fingernails is not evidence for machinery. At that point the claim quietly changes from hand tools cannot cut this to hand tools cannot be this consistent — a different claim, about regularity of stroke, which a worker with a fixed stance and a fixed blade produces as a matter of course.
  • The limepit comparison is not comparing like with like. Her right-hand panel is a small circular structure with a built rubble wall — a kiln, where stone is burned to make lime — set against a large rock-cut void where stone is extracted. Those are different installations at different stages of the same industry, and the difference between them is not a difference of technique.
  • Her own dating evidence points later, not earlier. Arabic inscriptions on the bell cave walls are, by the nature of the script, no earlier than the seventh century AD. She uses them for two thousand years of activity and then proposes that the caves may be prehistoric, without addressing what her only cited inscriptions actually date.
  • Two different classes of cave are treated as one. On the accepted account the columbaria belong to Hellenistic Maresha in the third century BC and the bell caves are much later quarrying. The video moves between them as "the caves at Maresha" and carries a conclusion about one across to the other.
  • The absence of ash argues against the funerary reading, not against the pigeon reading, and she accepts the pigeon reading — so the strongest negative evidence in the section is not doing the work the section implies.
  • The oral-transmission objection is not an objection. Selective breeding does not require anyone to know they are doing it, or to pass on a method. Keeping the calmest birds and eating the rest produces the effect over generations without instruction, writing or intention. The video treats a deliberate multi-millennial programme as the only alternative to genetic engineering.
  • The Farm Fox example cuts the other way. That a deliberate, funded, decade-long breeding programme did not achieve domestication is evidence that domestication is slow and unplanned — not that it required technology.
  • The final chain is conditional at every link. Columbaria imply pigeons; pigeons imply domestication; the caves may be older than stated; therefore the domestication may be the earlier civilisation's; therefore it may have been genetic manipulation. None of the steps is supported and each depends on the one before.
  • The paper she found is not cited. She says she found a paper providing evidence for pigeon breeding in Jordanian columbaria, and neither names nor shows it — which is the one piece of primary literature the video mentions.
  • The 10,000-year date is "research suggests" with no source, and it is the number the whole final third rests on.
  • The Pantheon suggestion is raised and dropped, with nothing offered for it.
  • The Soviet biologist is not named, nor is the review that reassessed the experiment.

What we have not checked

  • No excavation report or survey has been located or read. The 800 caves, the 70-cave bell complex, the 18 m height, the 85 columbaria, the 1,250 acres and the groups of 40–50 linked caves are as she gives them.
  • The Arabic inscriptions are mentioned and not shown. Their content, date and position have not been checked, and the "roughly 2,000 years of activity" they are said to indicate is unverified.
  • The dating of the columbaria to the third century BC is unsourced, and so is the statement that cremation was not the local practice.
  • The paper on Jordanian columbaria is not named or displayed and has not been located.
  • The 10,000-year pigeon domestication date has no citation, and neither do the Mesopotamian tablet and Egyptian hieroglyph references.
  • The Farm Fox experiment and the review reassessing it are described without names. Neither the 1959 biologist nor the review is identified in the video, and neither has been traced here.
  • The right-hand panel of the limepit comparison is not captioned or located. It is described here from the image alone; that it is a kiln rather than a quarry is read off the photograph, not stated in the video, and should be confirmed before it is repeated.
  • Which cave each interior frame shows is not stated. The bell cave, tool-mark and columbarium images are not identified individually, and the Luzit and Cappadocia photographs are not credited.
  • The transcript's "Balbeck" is corrected to Baalbek, "Capadoccia" to Cappadocia, and "colomba" to the Latin columba.
