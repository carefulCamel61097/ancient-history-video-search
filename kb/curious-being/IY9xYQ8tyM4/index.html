The object

Artifact K7QW-TIE-1, recovered in 2021 from the bottom of sacrificial pit no. 7 at Sanxingdui. It was so corroded that its shape was unrecognisable — a shapeless mass of rust, logged among thousands of other finds 00:00–00:50.

The excavators made what she calls a bold call: rather than clean it in the field, they cut the object and its surrounding soil into blocks and moved the whole thing to a laboratory, preserving the spatial context that surface excavation would have destroyed 00:56–01:23.

It is about 20 cm long and 5–8 cm wide, with a corroded profile suggesting an axe-like form — possibly a tool or a weapon.

The results were published in 2026 in the peer-reviewed journal Archaeological Research in Asia, by Professor Li Haichao of Sichuan University and lead corresponding author Yang Honglin of the Sichuan Provincial Institute of Cultural Relics and Archaeology 01:23–02:09.

The correction

Virtually every major English-language report on this discovery published a critical figure incorrectly.

Multiple prominent archaeological news sites gave the nickel content as approximately 7.41%, with iron above 90%. She reports the actual SEM-EDS area scan results, published by the authors' own institution — the Center for Archaeological Science at Sichuan University — as:

Why the number decides what the object is

The difference is not cosmetic, and she explains why properly 03:16–04:27. Meteoritic iron is a natural alloy carrying anywhere from 5% to 65% nickel. Terrestrial iron from surface crustal rock has been oxidised into ores and typically contains little or no native metallic nickel.

Iron meteorites are classified by nickel content:

classnickelfrequency
hexahedritesunder 6%rarer
octahedrites6–13%most common
ataxites16% or moreunder 8% of all known iron meteorites

At 19.84%, the artefact is almost certainly an ataxite — and on the 7.41% figure it would have been an ordinary octahedrite. The correction changes the object's category.

The classification the corrected number moves the object into.

The ancient Shu people did not simply find a piece of sky metal. They found an extraordinarily rare piece of sky metal.

The proof it was never smelted

The second analytical result, and the one that settles meteoritic against terrestrial 04:44–07:05. Under metallographic microscopy the team observed equiaxed ferrite grains — crystals of pure iron, uniform in size, entirely undeformed, with the elements distributed evenly at micron scale. The microstructure is single-phase ferrite throughout.

Two things follow. First, the grains are undeformed, so the object was not cold-worked after it was found; hammering or forging cold would have crushed, flattened or elongated those boundaries. Second, the grain structure itself is diagnostic of origin: iron cooling on Earth cools in hours or days and produces irregular, stressed crystals, while iron buried inside a large thermally insulating parent asteroid cools imperceptibly slowly over geological time, allowing uniform undeformed crystals to form.

No Bronze Age furnace could produce this structure… It's proof written in the metal's own crystalline memory.

Three continents, no contact

The comparative section, and it is the best-formed version of a cross-continental argument anywhere in this corpus 07:32–10:22:

  • Tutankhamun's dagger. The pharaoh died around 1323 BC, roughly contemporary with Sanxingdui. X-ray fluorescence analysis in 2016 confirmed the blade was forged from meteoritic iron, at 11% nickel and 0.6% cobalt, matching known iron meteorites.
  • The Hittites used a dedicated cuneiform term for meteoric iron — black iron of the sky. Their earliest iron objects predate widespread smelting and consistently show meteoritic composition.
  • The Treasure of Villena, Bronze Age Spain. Two meteoritic iron artefacts — a bracelet and a hollow decorative sphere, made between 1400 and 1200 BC. Mass spectrometry showed extraterrestrial iron at roughly 5.5% nickel, against under 1% in terrestrial ores. The metal was worked into ornament rather than into anything functional.

The contemporary Egyptian parallel.

The Spanish parallel, and how the iron sits among the gold.

Different civilizations, three continents, no contact with each other. They separately recognized meteorites and reached the same conclusion: metal that fell from the sky was sacred.

The contrast that carries the argument

The sharpest observation in the video, and it is about China rather than about the world 11:20–13:17. Across the Central Plains, the heartland of Shang civilisation, meteoritic iron turns up in a consistent and practical role: as small inserts forming the cutting edges of bimetallic composite weapons, combined with bronze to make a harder blade. Sky metal valued for hardness and put to work.

The Sanxingdui object follows none of that. It is monometallic — pure meteoritic iron, no bronze, no composite construction — and it was placed in a sacrificial pit alongside bronze sacred trees and elephant ivory, the most ceremonially charged objects the culture had.

The Central Plains people incorporated sky metal into weapons. The Shu people placed it among offerings. That is not a difference of degree. It's a difference of worldview.

The four mysteries

The closing section returns to familiar ground 13:18–15:53. The Sanxingdui culture appears in the archaeological record with sophisticated bronze casting already fully developed — no primitive early stages, no visible developmental sequence, no gradual improvement traceable through the excavated layers. Its lost-wax casting and composite sculpture assembly challenge established accounts of Bronze Age capability in south-west China. Its treasures were deliberately broken, burnt and interred rather than stored or hidden. And it vanished as abruptly as it appeared, with no conquest narrative, no gradual decline and no clear successor.

A civilization sophisticated enough to forge many complex bronze objects, identify ataxite meteorites and treat them as sacred does not simply forget how to exist. Something ended them. We still do not know what.

She ends by noting what is still open: the specific meteorite group has not been identified, and the research team has confirmed that as its next investigation 15:54–17:03.

What you can check yourself

  • The paper is named, dated, and in a peer-reviewed journal — Archaeological Research in Asia, 2026, Li Haichao and Yang Honglin. Anyone can find it and read the figures for themselves.
  • The correction is the whole point and it is checkable in both directions. Compare the English-language coverage giving ~7.41% nickel against the Sichuan University institutional publication giving 19.84%, and see which matches the paper.
  • The meteorite classification is standard geochemistry — hexahedrite, octahedrite, ataxite, by nickel content, with ataxites under 8% of known iron meteorites.
  • The Tutankhamun dagger analysis is published and widely reported: 2016, XRF, about 11% nickel and 0.6% cobalt.
  • The Villena artefacts are in a museum and their analysis is published.
  • The Hittite term is in the cuneiform corpus and can be looked up.
  • The Central Plains bimetallic weapons are excavated, catalogued objects — bronze blades with meteoritic iron edges — and the contrast with a monometallic object is a matter of looking at both.
  • The object's dimensions are given: about 20 cm by 5–8 cm.

What the video does not settle

  • She contradicts her own classification once. Having argued at length that 19.84% nickel makes it an ataxite, she later refers to "the ability to identify and work on an octahedrite". One of the two is a slip; nothing in the video reconciles them.
  • The erroneous coverage is shown; the corrected figure is not. She puts the offending article on screen with the 7.41% highlighted — unusual care for this corpus. But no screenshot of the Sichuan University publication carrying 77.8% and 19.84% appears anywhere, so the half of the correction that matters is spoken only. The outlet she is correcting is also never named.
  • "Virtually every major English-language report" is one screenshot. One example is shown and the claim is about a field of coverage.
  • Whether the two figures describe the same thing is not addressed. An SEM-EDS area scan and a bulk composition are different measurements, and a corroded object can give very different readings depending on where the beam lands. A 7.41% and a 19.84% result are not necessarily two reports of one number, and the possibility is not raised.
  • "They recognized it as something apart" is inference, not evidence. What is established is that a rare meteoritic object was placed in a ritual deposit. That the Shu identified it as sky metal, rather than as an unusually hard or unusually strange material, does not follow — and the video treats the recognition as the profound part.
  • The convergence has an ordinary explanation the video does not weigh. Meteoritic iron is visibly and physically distinctive — it is metal lying on the ground in a world with almost no metallic iron, it is unusually hard, it does not corrode like ore-smelted iron, and in many cases people saw it fall. Independent cultures treating it as special is what you would expect from the material alone, without any shared idea about the sky.
  • "No developmental sequence" is the same claim made in her 2021 videos and it is still unsupported. No excavation report, stratigraphic sequence or survey of the surrounding region is cited, and the possibility that earlier phases exist and are unexcavated or unpublished is not raised.
  • "Something ended them" restates a mystery as a conclusion. No collapse evidence is offered.
  • The worldview contrast rests on one object. A single monometallic find against a Central Plains pattern is a real and interesting difference, but one object is not a practice, and no other Sanxingdui meteoritic iron is known.
  • Nothing here supports the migration thesis of her 2021 Sanxingdui videos, and she does not claim it does. Worth stating plainly: the suppressed-DNA argument does not appear, and the steppe origin is not mentioned.
