Are the Montana Megaliths Man-Made?

Tina · 17 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-24

deflatedmainstreamhowwhynatural-versus-artificialpolygonal-masonrygeomorphology

The Montana megaliths are a rock complex of towering domes, high walls of tightly fitted polygonal granite blocks, and other formations. Some read it as a man-made megalithic site, which would matter a great deal: it would be the first such site in North America, alongside Giza, Sacsayhuamán, the Longyou caves and Yangshan Quarry 00:00–01:17.

The site itself, at the scale that makes the man-made reading convincing.

Her question is whether it is built at all — and the same question about three other sites treated the same way: the Gornaya Shoria megaliths in Siberia, the Saskatchewan megaliths in Canada, and the Kaimanawa Wall in New Zealand 01:17–01:56. All four names are printed on screen, which is the only reason they are recoverable — the auto-transcript renders them "Gia shua", "sucession one" and "Kwa wall".

Two things are promised: side-by-side comparisons, and a test — because, as she says outright, "photos or appearances might not be sufficient to make the judgment" 01:56–02:20.

The case for building them, put fairly first

She makes the affirmative argument before dismantling it 02:20–03:47. The Montana scale is imposing: big domes, high walls, blocks rounded and bulging and stacked, whose morphology and size resemble the polygonal walls of Sacsayhuamán. Gornaya Shoria has enormous rectangular blocks in different shapes and sizes, some with tight precise joints between them — and joints like that are prevalent in ancient artificial walls in Peru, Egypt and Eurasia.

Both sites are granite, which is harder than iron and than some non-specialised steel tools. So cutting blocks that size and fitting them with accurate seams must prove ancient high technology.

Right? Hold on.

Widen the frame

The first move is simply to show more of each site 03:47–04:27. Most of both the Montana megaliths and Gornaya Shoria look geologically ordinary. A few sections mimic man-made polygonal walls to some degree, but not quite. And the famous rectangular blocks of Gornaya Shoria, viewed from another angle, lose the man-made feeling entirely.

The Siberian site, and what it looks like once the frame widens.

Then a set of natural formations, introduced with a disarming admission — "for non-geologists like me it can be hard to believe some stone formations are natural, since their patterns are just too regular and consistent" 04:27–05:18.

Columnar jointing, which produces mostly hexagonal columns. And the tessellated pavement: a flat rock surface subdivided by systematic joints into polygons, most often rough rectangles, so uniform they look like a tiled floor.

A natural surface that looks laid rather than cracked.

That reminded her of the Canadian site, which has flat rectangular blocks lying on the ground like tiles and notably resembles a tessellated pavement. She takes it as plausibly natural on that resemblance alone 05:18–05:44.

The Canadian site, and the natural landform it resembles.

What joints are

The geological middle of the video, and she gives it properly 05:44–07:17. Rock masses contain fractures, joints and faults; joints are among the most universal geological structures and occur in almost every rock exposure. The most prominent ones occur in the best-consolidated, most competent rocks — sandstone, limestone, quartzite and granite.

A joint is a natural break with no visible movement parallel to the fracture surface. Joints arise from brittle fracture under tensile stress: layers stretched, pore fluid pressure rising, or shrinkage as a rock body cools or dries while its outer boundaries stay fixed. When tensile strength is exceeded, the rock breaks in a plane parallel to the maximum principal stress and perpendicular to the minimum.

To put it simply, joints can create rows or layers of rectangular or polygonal blocks.

The slide she puts up while saying it carries more than she says. It divides igneous jointing into three named kinds — sheet, columnar and mural — and the textbook figure beside the word shows a granite mass cut by three perpendicular joint sets into a stack of rectangular blocks. Mural means wall-like. Geology has had a name for the phenomenon under discussion the whole time, and the narration passes over it without reading the heading aloud.

That granite has a named jointing habit which produces wall-like blocks.

The comparisons follow 07:17–08:26. Nature makes gate-like formations — she names Devil's Lake and Balanced Rock in Big Bend. Nature makes very fine precise seams, hairline cracks between uniform rectangular blocks, and polygonal walls. Nature cracks, fractures and erodes every kind of hard rock without difficulty.

The two comparisons the visual case rests on.

The two comparisons are well made and it is worth saying so: Montana against Capo Pecora in Sardinia, and Gornaya Shoria against the Granite Dells in Arizona. Same rock type on both sides, the same order of scale, a person in every frame, and the feature at issue visible in all four. Very little else in this corpus meets that standard.

Her conclusion from the photographs is carefully limited: the two proposed sites do not have clear-cut differences from natural rock, while they do differ from what she regards as genuine high-tech construction such as the polygonal walls of Peru.

The two-prong test

Photographs are not enough, and not every megalithic site has tool marks. So 08:26–09:03:

For standing structures that have no tool marks, without being a geologist, how can we tell whether the sites are man-made versus natural? This can be baffling.

One: does it serve a justifiable function?

Building megalithically costs survey, planning, design, logistics, labour, equipment, materials and transport. People do not spend that on something with no important purpose. So a man-made megalithic site must have a function that justifies the cost 09:03–09:56.

Her worked examples: Sacsayhuamán as retaining walls, Baalbek as foundations, the Western Stone in Jerusalem's Western Wall as a structural wall.

What a megalithic site doing a job looks like, on her account.

Applied to the disputed sites 09:56–11:29: Montana and Gornaya Shoria are both in remote mountains, unreachable by vehicle and only accessible on foot. There are no other megalithic sites and no roads nearby. On Google Earth, Gornaya Shoria looks like a cliff wall. Why build a relatively short wall in a Siberian mountain range, or a small patch of pavement halfway up a Canadian mountain, and why haul equipment, tools, power, food and water into deep mountains to construct random non-connecting walls and domes?

It doesn't make sense. These sites could not pass the cost-benefit analysis.

Two: is it structurally sound?

The better half, and the more original 11:29–14:33. She goes to modern masonry for the standard.

Running bond: units in each course are centred over the head joints of the course below, so the joints stagger. The interlock distributes load. It is a structural bond.

Stack bond: units line up directly with those below, giving continuous head and bed joints and a grid. It is decorative, weaker, and non-structural. And she flags the important qualifier herself — this comparison assumes mortar. Without mortar, stack-bonded loose blocks would simply collapse.

The masonry standard the structural test is drawn from.

At Montana and Gornaya Shoria the blocks are not always staggered. They sometimes share uninterrupted continuous joints, cross-shaped joints, and even grids.

How a wall she accepts as built handles its joints.

There's no way a sophisticated culture which was capable of making grand megalithic walls with precisely fitted stone blocks would dress and align their blocks in continuous vertical joints and crosses or in a grid pattern. They should have known to stagger the blocks.

And the contrast that makes it a test rather than an impression: at sites she does accept, blocks are placed so that no vertical joint runs through multiple courses — while natural joint sets in rock masses are routinely continuous, producing exactly the grid pattern that modern masons call stack bond.

In a nutshell, man-made dry-stack structural masonry walls should never have grid-like joints, whereas natural rocks can and often do show long continuous vertical joints and grid patterns.

The verdict, and one more site

Neither disputed site passes either prong: no identifiable function, and inferior structural integrity. She adds that the Kaimanawa Wall in New Zealand has cross joints too, and is therefore likely natural as well 14:33–15:25.

The fourth site, disposed of in six seconds.

Then the loop back to the previous video 15:25–15:50. If Montana and the Canadian site are natural, then high-tech megalithic sites really are absent from North America — and she has just spent a video explaining why. The link is on screen.

Her closing recommendation is the test itself: when unsure whether a megalithic structure with gigantic precisely jointed blocks is man-made, ask whether it serves a purpose that justifies its construction cost, and whether it is structurally sound. Both must be satisfied.

If not, then they are most probably created by Mother Nature.

What you can check yourself

  • The comparison is the argument and it is all photographic. Anyone can put images of the Montana formations beside columnar jointing, tessellated pavement and jointed granite outcrops and judge whether they separate.
  • Rock jointing is standard geology and her account of it — brittle fracture under tensile stress, planes parallel to maximum principal stress — is textbook and checkable.
  • The tessellated pavement at Eaglehawk Neck in Tasmania is a real, photographed, visited place. So is columnar jointing at dozens of sites.
  • Running bond and stack bond are defined terms in masonry practice, and the claim that stack bond is non-structural is standard building knowledge rather than her invention.
  • The joint pattern is visible in photographs. Whether a vertical joint runs through several courses is something you can see, at Montana, at Gornaya Shoria and at any wall you accept as built.
  • The remoteness claims are checkable on a map. Both sites can be located and their access routes examined.
  • Her own test can be turned on her own sites, which is the most useful thing about it.

What the video does not settle

  • Prong one is never applied to the sites she believes in, and it would damage them. The whole point of the why argument across her corpus is that nobody has a good explanation for these sites — that is the entry's own framing at Huashan, at Longyou, at the Osireion. "We cannot determine its function, so rethink whether it is artificial" is a rule that, run consistently, reclassifies half of what she is trying to protect. The video applies it in one direction only.
  • A cost-benefit test assumes builders reason like us. Monuments raised for religious, funerary or status reasons routinely fail modern cost-benefit analysis, and that is not evidence they were not built. No allowance is made for a purpose we cannot recognise.
  • Roadlessness now is not roadlessness then. "Cannot be reached by automobiles" is offered as though it were an argument about antiquity. Many accepted sites are remote today; some were on routes that no longer exist.
  • "No other megalithic sites nearby" is circular where the question is whether any North American megalithic sites exist at all.
  • Prong two cannot distinguish natural from badly built or later disturbed. A collapsed, re-stacked or partially natural site would fail it too. The test as stated has one output for three quite different situations.
  • The mortar qualifier cuts deeper than she takes it. She notes that stack bond without mortar would collapse — and the walls are standing. If they are natural that is unremarkable; but the observation is doing double duty as both premise and conclusion and is never separated out.
  • "Distinctions from the legitimate ancient high-tech megalithic constructions" is the question, not the answer. Which sites count as legitimate is what is being decided, and Peru is used as the fixed reference throughout without that being argued.
  • No geologist is consulted, and she says she is not one. The honesty is real and it does not substitute for an assessment. The entire video is a geological question answered from photographs and general reading.
  • The Montana site is never located, named precisely, dated or surveyed. No coordinates, no landowner, no measurement, no published description. It is argued about entirely through images whose provenance is not given.
  • Tool marks are dropped. The criterion she uses in almost every other video — machining marks — is set aside here on the grounds that not all sites have them, and no attempt is made to look for them at Montana.
  • The granite hardness point is raised and abandoned. Harder than iron and some steel is used to build up the impressiveness of the sites and never returns once they have been reclassified.
  • The Canadian site is reclassified on a resemblance alone, which is the reasoning the video opens by rejecting as insufficient.
  • The Kaimanawa verdict takes six seconds. A site with its own literature and a New Zealand government assessment is disposed of on one visual feature.
  • Nothing is dated. Whether any of the four disputed sites has ever been sampled, excavated or radiometrically assessed is not mentioned.
