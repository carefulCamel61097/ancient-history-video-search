I Spent Months Investigating the Barabar Caves. Here's What I Found

Tina · 29 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-23

deflatedmainstreamhowunfinishedrock-cut-sitesmachining-and-tool-marksunfinished-work

Seven caves cut into twin granite hills in Bihar, over 2,300 years old, with mirror-polished walls, tight barrel vaults and a clap that takes nearly a minute to fade. Two accounts compete: a lost civilisation with technology beyond ours, or one of the great engineering achievements of the ancient world. Her opening statement is that she spent months on engineering papers, laser scans and archaeological reports rather than on videos, and wanted to know which the evidence supports 00:00–02:18.

How precise are they, actually?

French teams — she names them as BAM and AGP — laser-scanned the caves and published metrology. Her position is that the reports settle the precision question if anyone reads them 02:18–02:53.

That the metrology exists and was taken on site, rather than being asserted.

The numbers she gives 02:53–04:08:

  • Gopika: about 50.5% of measured surface deviations fall within a 5 mm range; the radii of the curved ceilings at the two ends are offset by 9 cm; the floor base widths differ by 6 cm; the side walls lean at 87°, not 90.
  • Vadathika: barrel-vault radius varies between 2.02 and 2.25 m along its length; long side walls at roughly 87°, short end walls at 89°.
  • Vapiyaka: vault radii differ by 2 cm, floor width by 1 cm.
  • Karan Chaupar: the junction of wall and floor is bumpy and far from perfect.
  • Sudama: the two corners are slanted at varying angles, and the walls pitch more than a degree off perpendicular.

The precision claim, settled by the survey's own drawings.

Her conclusion is careful in both directions 04:08–05:47. The caves remain extraordinary — achieving this consistency in solid granite is impressive on any account. But laser-accurate, millimetre-perfect and flawlessly geometric are not what the data show. For scale she gives modern tunnel-boring alignment at a few millimetres and precast concrete tunnel segments at ±0.1 mm. And the chambers are small, 42–84 m², which is inherently easier to control dimensionally than a large space. "The precision here is masterful. It is not, however, miraculous."

Where the polish comes from

The finish is the part that gets people, and she agrees it is rare and difficult 05:47–06:05.

Her argument is that Barabar is not unique in showing it. The same Mauryan polish appears on the Ashokan pillars, including the Lion Capital at Sarnath that is India's national emblem, and on the Didarganj Yakshi in the Bihar Museum — a life-sized sandstone figure still gleaming after 2,300 years. The Bihar Museum's own note, she reports, is that the Mauryan polish appears to have been an imported technique, because Achaemenid Persian masonry shows the same characteristics 06:05–06:50.

She grants the alternative side its strongest point: before the Mauryan period, monumental Indian architecture was almost entirely timber, thatch and brick, and there is no gradual evolution towards sophisticated stone carving in the Indian record. It appears suddenly, fully formed, in Ashoka's reign — and she says she first took that as support for the advanced-civilisation reading 06:50–07:23.

Then the resolution she proposes 07:23–10:00. In 330 BCE Alexander destroyed the Persian empire, burned Persepolis and dismantled Achaemenid royal administration, putting the ancient world's most skilled scribes, craftsmen and stonemasons out of work overnight — the people who had polished the animal capitals at Persepolis to a mirror. Directly east, the Mauryan empire was rising and wanted monuments. Her supporting evidence for contact: Aramaic record-keeping behind Ashoka's edict programme; the Kharosthi script deriving from Aramaic; Ashoka's pillars echoing Persepolis closely enough that historians describe them as Indian in spirit and Achaemenid in technique; and the Kandahar bilingual inscription of about 260 BCE, in Greek and Aramaic, showing Persian and Greek communities under Mauryan administration seventy years after Alexander.

The cave nobody talks about

About fifteen miles from Barabar, cut into a single isolated granite boulder, is the Sitamarhi Cave 10:00–11:29. It is smaller than the two smallest Barabar caves — some 20 cm shorter, with a ceiling only 2 m high — and the design is different. But the ceiling profile is the same barrel vault, with the same finish and the same quality of work. It carries no royal inscription and is attributed not to the Ajivikas but to Jainism, in the Mauryan period.

The control case - the same vault and the same finish, fifteen miles away and on nobody's list.

She then runs the dilemma deliberately, and this is the best-constructed argument in the video 11:29–13:23. If a lost civilisation with technology beyond ours built Barabar, what is Sitamarhi? Did the same civilisation carve a small low-ceilinged chamber inside a lone boulder fifteen miles away — and why there, isolated, when there was room left on both Barabar and Nagarjuni hills? Why so much smaller? What purpose would it serve? And if Sitamarhi is instead a later Mauryan imitation, that concedes the point: it means ancient Indian craftsmen could carve a precisely vaulted, polished granite cave, which means Barabar stops being impossible.

"Either the advanced civilisation built a tiny purposeless chamber in an isolated boulder fifteen miles from their main site, or ancient Indians could in fact do this work after all."

The unfinished cave

Of the seven, Lomas Rishi stands apart: the most elaborate exterior on the hill, a carved arched façade imitating timber architecture with a frieze of elephants. Inside, everything changes 13:31–14:33. The ceiling is raw, covered in heavy percussion marks from iron chisels and the signs of bulk granite removal. The floor was never brought to final level, and there is a shallow sunken channel near the dividing wall where workers were still chipping down to grade. And yet in places the walls are polished.

The order the work was done in, preserved because it stopped.

The alternative reading of that mixture is that the rough work is later Mauryan intrusion into a finished, polished cave. Her counter is about sequence 14:33–15:46: no engineering culture, advanced or otherwise, polishes walls before the ceiling is cut and the floor is at level, or finishes and polishes its only doorway before the interior excavation is done and the spoil still has to come out through it. You excavate, you refine, you polish last — because percussive work on a ceiling directly above a polished wall would chip and scar it.

So on her reading the mixture is not two civilisations but one construction site frozen mid-progress, stopped by the geological crack still visible in the ceiling and the rockslide that followed — which would also explain why this cave alone among the finished ones was never dedicated and never inscribed 15:46–16:07.

She cites the historian John C. Huntington for the tool-mark progression: coarsest marks in the vault where bulk granite came out, increasingly fine regular chisel work approaching the wall surfaces, the finest detailing near the polished zones. A textbook staged sequence 16:07–16:31.

What the unfinished surface was made with.

Her stated method, for the whole video, is one question: what would we expect from an advanced civilisation on par with ours? Would they polish before finishing excavation? Carve a rough ceiling after mirror-polishing walls? Cut a small isolated polished cave fifteen miles away, for what 16:31–17:06?

Who inscribed what

On Barabar Hill, Sudama, Karan Chaupar and Viswamitra carry Ashoka's inscriptions; Lomas Rishi has none. On Nagarjuni Hill all three caves are inscribed — not by Ashoka but by his grandson Dasharatha, about thirty years later 17:14–17:47.

The alternative argument is that Ashoka's dedications use a word meaning gave, implying he found the caves rather than commissioned them. Her objection is geographic 17:47–18:50: if he was repurposing pre-existing ancient caves on Barabar Hill, why did he miss three virtually identical ones on Nagarjuni Hill 150 m away? Someone hunting for mysterious chambers to repurpose does not stop at one hill. The simpler reading is that the Nagarjuni caves did not exist yet, and were commissioned separately by Dasharatha three decades later — two kings, two phases, one technique.

On the claim that the inscriptions look crude beside the mirror finish 18:55–20:43: the Viswamitra cave is the least visited and therefore the best preserved, and its inscriptions are sharp, deep and comparable in quality to those on the polished pillars. The pillar inscriptions were out of reach for most of history; the cave inscriptions are at hand height and have been touched for two thousand years. The crude carvings that circulate in photographs include documented Gupta graffiti — an inscription of 320–550 CE calling Karan Chaupar the cave of beggars, and a fifth-to-sixth-century Sanskrit addition in Gopika's passageway. Those are later writing, not the original dedications. Of one photograph of a crude P-shaped symbol she says she cannot place it among the six Ashokan inscriptions at all.

All six dedications in one place, and the crude carving she says is not one of them.

The acoustics

A single sung note takes almost a minute to die away in a space the size of a studio apartment 20:43–21:09.

The claim usually made is that four of the caves share a fundamental resonance of 34.4 Hz. Her reading of the paper is that this is not what it says 21:09–22:45. The researchers analysed hundreds of resonant modes and found no single natural frequency common to all the caves. They then looked for frequencies shared by several, and four caves show a resonance near 34.4 Hz — but arising from different standing-wave patterns in each: a first-order longitudinal mode in one, a second-order longitudinal mode in another, a first-order wave mode in a third. The report does not conclude intentional tuning; it records 34.4 Hz as one frequency present in four caves among many modes unique to each.

The second document is the reverberation study 22:45–24:14. Reverberation time plotted against frequency across five caves, and the graph starts at 50 Hz, so it says nothing about 34 Hz at all. Between 50 and 100 Hz several caves approach 70 seconds; Notre-Dame, she says, sits at 8–10 seconds at low frequencies. But each cave peaks at a different frequency — Lomas Rishi at 63 Hz, Gopika around 100, Sudama's dome at both 50 and 80 — so if these were chambers tuned to one frequency by an advanced civilisation, either the goal was missed or it was never the goal.

The acoustic study itself - what it is, and what it says in its own words.

What she does with it instead: the peaks land between 50 and 120 Hz, which is the range of deep male chanting and of sustained drone tones. A monk chanting there would hear his voice booming for forty to sixty seconds. "Even so, amazing acoustics do not tell us who built the cave."

Could they have done it

Her closing section is about capability rather than about Barabar 24:43–26:40. The Shulba Sutras, roughly 800–200 BCE, set out how knotted ropes of specific lengths were used to lay out geometrical fire altars including right angles, circles and falcon forms, and contain an explicit statement of the Pythagorean theorem. Rulers calibrated to a sixteenth of an inch have been excavated at Lothal, dated 2400 BCE. Kautilya's Arthashastra codified measurement across the Mauryan empire — length units, surveying ropes, water levels for grade, plumb lines for verticality. The Ashokan pillars are monolithic polished shafts up to 15 m. The later Karla and Bhaja cave complexes scale the same rock-cut tradition up dramatically.

Her verdict 26:40–28:35: the precision surprised her because it was not perfect, and that is more interesting; the unfinished cave answered the biggest question almost by accident, through its tool marks. "These caves were not built by a lost civilisation or aliens. They were built by people. People with ropes and chisels and an almost incomprehensible level of patience and skill."

What you can check yourself

  • The acoustic report is on screen with its title and page count. It is displayed as a Scribd document headed BARABAR – RAPPORT-CHEVALIER-ACOUSTIQUE-JAYAN-FILM, 46 pages, with "BARABAR 2023" in the running foot. Two of its pages are legible in the frames, in French, and can be read directly.
  • The reverberation figures are printed in that report. Page 14 states that reverberation times can exceed a minute at Vadathika and the Sudama dome, that mid-frequency times run 13–36 seconds at 125 Hz and 5–10 seconds at 500 Hz, and that the caves fall into two groups at 800 Hz — Vapiyaka, Vadathika and Karan Chaupar around 3–4 seconds, Gopika and Sudama around 7–8. It also gives Notre-Dame's maximum measured reverberation as 12 seconds at 125 Hz, measured in 1987.
  • The metrology is shown as drawings, not as claims. Four cross-sections labelled T03 to T06 carry printed vault radii between R2.01 and R2.25 and printed wall angles between 87°29′ and 87°45′. The 87-degree figure can be read off the drawing.
  • The inscriptions are reproduced in a published plate she displays, listing all six by cave: Karna Chopâr, Sudâma and Viswamitra on Barabar Hill, Gopika, Vapiya and Vadathi on Nagarjuni.
  • The comparison objects are in public collections. The Lion Capital at Sarnath and the Didarganj Yakshi in the Bihar Museum can be looked at, and whether they carry the same finish is a matter of looking.
  • The Kandahar bilingual inscription is published, and whether it is in Greek and Aramaic is not in dispute.
  • The Shulba Sutras are translated, and whether they state the Pythagorean relation is checkable in the text.
  • Sitamarhi is a real place fifteen miles from Barabar, and its ceiling profile can be compared with Barabar's in her own two-panel frame.

What the video does not settle

  • How the polish was made is never described. The video establishes that the capability existed and traces where it came from, and never says what was actually done to granite to produce a mirror — what abrasive, what compound, how long. That is the claim the alternative side leans on hardest, and it is the one thing left unanswered.
  • The Persian craft-transfer is a hypothesis delivered as a conclusion. "Many historians believe" becomes "that's where the Mauryan polish comes from" within thirty seconds. No Persian mason is named in India, no transitional workshop is identified, and no technical comparison of Persepolis polish with Mauryan polish is cited — only the resemblance and the timing.
  • The report's own caveat is on screen and not read out. The page she displays carries the author's note that closing the cave entrances would distort the results, and that he does not know by how much. The reverberation figures are quoted without that limitation.
  • Her Notre-Dame number differs from the page she is showing. She says 8–10 seconds at low frequency; the report gives 12 seconds at 125 Hz as the maximum measured.
  • The 34 Hz paper is described but never identified. It is a different document from the acoustic report, and no author, title or date is given for it, so her account of what it does and does not conclude cannot be checked from the video.
  • "BAM" and "AGP" are never expanded. A "RETOUR SUR BAM" logo appears in the survey footage and a small mark on the drawings, but neither team is named in full, and no report title is given for the metrology.
  • The 50.5%-within-5 mm figure is quoted without its reference surface. Deviation from what — a fitted plane, a design intent, a best-fit cylinder — is not stated, and the number means different things depending on the answer.
  • The Sitamarhi dilemma rests on Sitamarhi's date. It is attributed to Jainism in the Mauryan period, by nobody named and on grounds not given. If the attribution is loose, the fork loses force.
  • The image that would settle Lomas Rishi is not shown. Her argument is that percussive ceiling work above a polished wall would have scarred it. Whether the polished surfaces there carry percussion damage is decidable by looking closely at the junction, and the frame offered is a wide view.
  • The rockslide is an inference. A crack is visible and is pointed at; no geological assessment, no dating of the fracture and no fallen material is shown, and "almost certainly ended the project" is doing real work in the argument.
  • Huntington is cited by name only. No title, year or publication for the tool-mark study.
  • The mathematics section proves surveying, not finishing. Knotted ropes, plumb lines and water levels lay a chamber out; they do not polish granite. The capability argument and the polish argument are answering different questions and are presented as one.
  • "No gradual evolution towards stone" is absence of evidence in a record where timber does not survive. She uses it as the premise for the Persian transfer; the other side uses the same premise for a lost civilisation. It is weak for both, and the video does not say so.
  • The inscription argument answers reuse, not authorship. That Ashoka would not have stopped searching at one hill is a good objection to the reuse reading. It is not by itself a demonstration that Mauryan masons cut the caves, and the video treats the simpler explanation as the established one.

What we have not checked

  • None of the sources has been consulted. The metrology reports, the 34 Hz paper, the Chevalier acoustic report and Huntington's work are known here only from her account and from the frames. The report's French pages are legible in two frames and are read from those frames only.
  • "BAM" and "AGP" are not expanded anywhere. "RETOUR SUR BAM" appears as a logo on the survey footage; a small mark appears on the CAD drawings. What the initials stand for is not established.
  • The cave names are taken from the screen, not the audio, which mangles them badly: the transcript's "Go big or go home cave" is Gopika, "Vadi Athika" is Vadathika, "Vepiyaka" is Vapiyaka, "Karenchopper" is Karan Chaupar. The fourth Barabar cave is printed as Viswamitra on the plate she shows and read aloud as something closer to "Vishva Jhopri"; both names are used for it in the literature and the spelling has not been settled here.
  • The photograph she disputes is captioned "BAM's Inscription photo" on screen, and the transcript renders her word as "Ben". The on-screen label is taken as correct here; if the audio is right and a third party is meant, that changes who she is disagreeing with, and it has not been resolved.
  • The Bihar Museum's note on imported technique is paraphrased, not quoted or shown.
  • The figures are as she gives them and have not been traced: 50.5% within 5 mm; the 9 cm, 6 cm, 2 cm and 1 cm offsets; 42–84 m²; ±0.1 mm for precast segments; Sitamarhi's 20 cm and 2 m; the 63, 100, 50 and 80 Hz peaks; 70 seconds at 50–100 Hz; and the 15 m pillar height.
  • Some footage carries a Bihar Tourism watermark, so not all interior video is her own or the survey team's.
  • The dating of the Gupta graffiti and the reading "cave of beggars" are as stated.
