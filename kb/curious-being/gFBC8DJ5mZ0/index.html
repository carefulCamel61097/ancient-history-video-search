Questioning the Luminescence Dating Result of the Giza Pyramid

Tina · 17 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-23

datingmainstreamhowluminescence-datingwater-erosion-and-weatheringpyramid-construction

Radiocarbon cannot date stone. Luminescence dating can, and that is why the method matters to anyone arguing about megalithic sites 00:00–00:36.

The principle 06:32–07:55: quartz and feldspar accumulate energy in their crystal lattices from ambient radioactivity, and light or heat empties that store to zero. Cutting and transporting a block exposes its surfaces to sunlight, which resets the clock to a certain depth. Once the block is laid so that a face receives no more light, the signal begins re-accumulating and keeps accumulating until the sample is taken. Dating a buried face therefore dates the last time that surface saw daylight — in principle, when the wall went up.

What the Giza results say

The chart comes from a **2014 paper, Surface luminescence dating of some Egyptian monuments, which she credits on screen with its journal and DOI 01:37–02:12. The samples were taken before 2010, from four sites on the Giza Plateau. She works with the three structures for which Egyptologists have offered a conventional date — the Valley Temple of Khafre, the Sphinx Temple and the Menkaure Pyramid** — leaving aside the fourth, the Osiris Shaft, whose archaeological age the paper gives as a question mark. The paper's own summary is that the ages confirm most conventional dynastic dates, while in some cases predating them by hundreds of years 00:59–01:10.

Her first observation is not about the mainstream timeline at all — it is that samples from the same structure disagree by about 2,000 years 02:17–03:41:

  • Valley Temple — the granite sample dates over 2,000 years older than the limestone sample. She notes the ordering is backwards: the granite is an outer sheath applied over the limestone core, so it should be the younger of the two. The limestone sample came from an inner wall of the roof, and she wonders aloud whether that part of the roof was original or replaced in a later renovation.
  • Sphinx Temple — again about 2,000 years between oldest and youngest, and here both samples are granite and both were collected close to the temple floor and the bedrock.

The comparison she draws 03:41–04:02: mainstream academia has the Great Pyramid, of millions of blocks, built in twenty years. Two much smaller temples returning a 2,000-year internal spread does not fit alongside that.

The ordering the dates invert: granite applied over a limestone core should be the younger of the two.

The Menkaure sample

The only pyramid tested at Giza 04:16–05:39. One sample was taken from the granite casing stone at the base, and it dates to 3450 ± 950 BC — she puts the margin on screen beside the photograph of the sampled course. It is the oldest of all the Giza samples by at least a few hundred years, and its margin is the widest of the nine.

The Old Kingdom runs 2700–2200 BC and is called the Age of the Pyramids; Menkaure is conventionally the last of the three great pyramids, at 2510 BC. Her reading of the number: the result puts the pyramid well before the Old Kingdom, and with its error margin potentially almost 1,900 years older than the conventional date. Her line is that the Age of the Pyramid Builders may have been the Age of the Pyramid Discoverers.

The single oldest Giza result, and the width of the margin attached to it.

She makes the same point about the two temples' oldest results — a few hundred and potentially closer to a thousand years before the 4th Dynasty — and then states the tension in the paper directly 06:02–06:12: the text says the results sit within the conventional timeline; the data proposes earlier construction.

Then she attacks the data she has just used

The second half is an argument that these results are unreliable, and it is aimed at the sampling rather than the laboratory 07:55–13:38.

Two things have to go right. The signal must have been fully bleached before burial, and the sample must be collected without letting any light reach it afterwards.

On bleaching: 1 to 100 seconds of direct sunlight resets quartz. She notes a finding that artificial sources including some darkroom lights bleach rock samples to varying degrees in as little as 15 minutes, so samples must be handled only under safe lighting.

On collection, the direction of the error is the whole argument: light exposure produces age underestimation. A contaminated sample reads younger, never older. She reports reading multiple recent papers, all of which stress this.

Her quantitative anchor is an outdoor experiment in southern Sweden 09:58–10:47: samples bleached rapidly in both sunny and cloudy conditions, at average rates of 15% per second in direct sun and 9% per second under cloud across the first five seconds of exposure. She then puts Sweden and Egypt on a global solar irradiation map — Sweden near the bottom of the range, Egypt near the top — and asks what one second of Egyptian sun would do if one cloudy Swedish second removes 9% of the natural dose.

Why a sampling standard developed in northern Europe is a weak floor for fieldwork in Egypt.

The current standard she measures against 11:25–12:16: light-proof sampling tubes hammered into the side of a trench, excavated out, and sealed at the exposed ends with opaque caps or tape. Where tubes cannot be used, samples are taken under an opaque tarp or at night, and finding truly light-safe tarp material is itself difficult enough that multiple layers may be needed.

What was actually done at Giza

She quotes the paper's method 12:38–13:07:

Surfaces were detached from walls gently by a hammer and chisel and pieces of inner surface with an area around 2 × 2 cm. The procedure is made with due caution and by the moment the detached piece becomes loose the external part is covered by a black plastic bag which covers the whole removed piece marking the outer part and with an arrow the inner surface.

Hammer, chisel and a black plastic bag, not a light-proof tube — and she notes the paper's accompanying photographs show the sampling being done in bright daylight. Her reading is that tubes were not available when these samples were taken over eleven years earlier, so the method was not to today's standard.

Her highlight stops one sentence short of the end of the paragraph. The next sentence is legible in her own screenshot: "The sample is unmasked from the plastic bag in the dark room under fume cupboard and red light." It is not read out, and the video's later statement that samples should only be worked on in a light-safe lab is put as a standard the study did not meet.

The conditions the sampling was done in, from the study's own record of it.

Her objection is about the interval 13:38–15:19. However carefully it is done, there is a gap between the piece coming loose and the bag going over it. She works through the practicalities: the samples are small and came from buried or corner positions, awkward for two people to work side by side while one swings a hammer, so the second would have to wait for the first to clear — and a single worker would have to put the tools down before reaching for the bag. Either way the sample sees light.

She declines to press further points — sample depths, the bags and lights used, preparation with older techniques — and says the hammer-and-chisel method alone is enough to question the results. That, she suggests, may also be why samples from one building differ so widely: some were bleached more than others.

Where it leaves her

Because contamination only ever makes a date younger 15:52–16:53, her conclusion is that the published ages are floors rather than estimates. Even with possible bleaching, Menkaure came out over 900 years older than the conventional date, and the two temples likely predate the Old Kingdom as well. Her closing question is whether Giza can be sampled again with current technique, and what that would show.

What you can check yourself

  • The paper is cited on screen with its DOI. Title, journal, year and dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.culher.2014.05.007 appear under her simplified chart, so every number in the first half can be taken back to the source.
  • The error margins are published and she displays them, both in the paper's Table 1 and in her own chart. Any reader can do the arithmetic on whether two results actually differ.
  • The internal spreads are in the published table — whether two samples from one structure differ by 2,000 years is a matter of arithmetic, not interpretation.
  • The stratigraphic point at the Valley Temple is visible on site. Whether the granite is an outer sheath over a limestone core is a question about a standing building, photographable by anyone.
  • The quoted method is the paper's own words, and the sampling photographs are published with it. Whether the work was done in daylight with hammer and chisel is checkable in the paper.
  • The direction of the sampling error is textbook. That light exposure causes age underestimation is standard in the luminescence literature and does not rest on her authority.
  • The Swedish bleaching-rate experiment is a published result with a number attached.

What the video does not settle

  • The margins are shown and then argued around. Menkaure is 3450 ± 950 BC, and the paper's own range for it is 4400–2500 BC. She reaches "almost 1,900 years older than conventional" from the 4400 end; the 2500 end sits within ten years of the conventional 2510 BC, which is not mentioned. Her later figure of "over 900 years older" uses the central value alone. On the paper's own numbers the sample does not separate the two dates.
  • The margins also absorb much of the internal spread. The Sphinx Temple's oldest and youngest are 3100 ± 540 and 1190 ± 340, and the Valley Temple's are 3060 ± 470 and 1050 ± 540. Those are genuinely far apart, but they are not the clean 2,000-year gaps the narration implies, and the video never converts any of the spreads into a statement about significance.
  • The paper's own bleaching work is in the table she displays and goes unmentioned. Table 1 carries a Bleaching column giving per-sample exposure experiments in hours and seconds, distinguishing sunlight from a solar simulator. That the authors investigated bleaching at all is visible on screen and is not weighed against the argument that they were careless about it.
  • The method paragraph continues past her highlight. The paper states the sample is unmasked in a darkroom under a fume cupboard and red light. The concern she raises — the interval between detachment and bagging, out in the sun — is untouched by that, but the video presents the study as having no light-safe laboratory step when its own screenshot shows one.
  • The tube comparison is the wrong standard, and her own figure shows why. The illustration she uses has a tube being mallet-driven into a soft sediment face. Detaching a 2 × 2 cm piece from a standing granite wall is a different operation, and the video does not establish that tubes were ever an option for rock surfaces.
  • The granite sample's position is not what the argument assumes. The stratigraphic point requires the Valley Temple granite to be exterior sheathing over the limestone core. The paper's table locates RHO-98 in the temple's upper magazine, an interior space, and the video does not address the difference.
  • The sampling reconstruction is imagined, not observed. The two-person and one-person scenarios, and the gap before the bag, are inferred from the wording of a method paragraph. The video does not establish how long any sample was actually exposed.
  • The argument is used in one direction only, and this is where it bends. If the sampling is unreliable enough to invalidate the results, the results cannot then be relied on to show that Menkaure is 900 years older than accepted. The asymmetry is defensible — contamination only makes dates younger, so a surviving old date is a floor — but it holds only if light exposure is the sole error source in play, which the video asserts rather than shows. Insufficient bleaching before burial, the other failure mode she describes at 07:55–08:25, pushes dates the other way, and it is not revisited once the argument reaches Giza.
  • Nothing here identifies a builder. An earlier date for Menkaure's casing would be a problem for the accepted chronology. It is not by itself evidence for a lost civilisation, and the video moves between those two claims without marking the step.

What we have not checked

  • The 2014 paper has not been located or read. It is fully identified on screen — Surface luminescence dating of some Egyptian monuments, Journal of Cultural Heritage (2014), dx.doi.org/10.1016/j.culher.2014.05.007 — but no author is named, and Table 1, the method paragraph and the sampling photographs are known here only from her screenshots.
  • The figures are read off her screenshots of the paper and have not been traced to the paper itself: the nine results and their margins, the ranges 3530–2590, 3640–2560 and 4400–2500 BC, the 4th Dynasty estimate of 2613–2494 BC, and 2510 BC for Menkaure.
  • Whether the samples reported as being from one structure came from parts of it built at the same time has not been checked, and the paper's location strings — "upper magazine", plain "limestone" — are all that is available here.
  • The southern Sweden bleaching experiment — 15%/s and 9%/s over the first five seconds — is quoted without a citation and has not been checked.
  • The finding that darkroom lights bleach samples within 15 minutes is unsourced here.
  • The claim that sampling tubes were not available when the Giza samples were taken has not been verified, and matters, since the whole objection turns on the method having been below the standard of its own time rather than merely below today's.
  • Whether the Valley Temple granite is demonstrably an applied sheath rather than integral has not been independently confirmed; it is stated as something many have pointed out.
