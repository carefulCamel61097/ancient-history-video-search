Did Humans Accidentally Domesticate Ourselves with Shrinking Brains?

Tina · 18 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-09-27

deflatedmainstreamwhyhuman-brain-evolutionlost-civilisation-hypothesis

The other side of the brain sizes video. There, the shrinking human brain is the premise of a lost-civilisation argument. Here she takes the explanation mainstream science offers for the same fact and puts three questions to it.

What is being argued against 01:05–03:48. On the account of Richard Wrangham, a primatologist at Harvard, and Brian Hare, professor of evolutionary anthropology at Duke, the decline in human brain volume is selection against aggression: we domesticated ourselves.

The evidence behind that is real and she sets it out fairly. Domesticated animals — sheep, pigs, cats, dogs — have brains 10 to 25% smaller than their wild ancestors, and lower encephalization quotients as well. A wolf-sized dog's brain is about 10% smaller than a wolf's. Reduced brain size, reduced encephalization and a tamer temperament together make up the domestication syndrome.

The example she leads with. The palaeontologist Ana Balcarcel and colleagues compared wild and domestic cattle and found domestic brains about 25% smaller than their wild forebears — and that bullfighting breeds, selected for aggression, have brains nearly as large as wild aurochs.

Human brains today are 10 to 17% smaller than they were 40,000 years ago, which is the same range. So the hypothesis fits. Her three questions are about whether the fit means anything.

One — is domestication the only route to working together?

04:01–08:56 Hominins have always been social. Chimpanzees live in communities of up to 150, bonobos up to 120, and 150 is Dunbar's number — the correlation Robin Dunbar drew between primate brain size and group size, which holds for hunter-gatherer bands and for offices, factories and military units alike. Primates have relied on one another for millions of years. Did they domesticate themselves?

She widens it: lions, hyenas, elephants, whales and dolphins all cooperate, generation after generation, and none is called self-domesticated. Lionesses in a pride bond, hunt together, share food and nurse cubs that are not their own, and are "as feral as feral can be".

Where do we draw the line between self-domestication and the natural animal instinct of group work?

Then the calculation. Hare suspects bonobos are a domesticated form of chimpanzee — quieter, more tolerant, smaller-brained. She checks the encephalization rather than the brain size. From a 2012 paper, average chimpanzee brain volume 398 cc at a body weight of 52.25 kg; average bonobo brain volume 358 cc — 10% smaller — at 37.73 kg.

Divide brain by body and bonobos come out more encephalized than chimpanzees, not less. Which is the opposite of what the domestication reading predicts. She notes that larger samples are needed and does not overstate it.

The table she shows makes clear how small: the figures are the means of three bonobos and four chimpanzees, listed individually by name. Six further animals appear below them with brain volumes but no body weights, so they cannot enter the calculation at all.

The figures behind her strongest objection, and the sample they come from.

Two — in humans, does aggression track brain size at all?

08:56–12:01 In animals the pattern is that the wild, aggressive ones have the bigger brains. She argues humans run the other way.

  • Psychopaths are not unusually intelligent. The criminologist Brian Boutwell and colleagues analysed 187 published papers on intelligence and psychopathy, covering both prison populations and people in high-flying careers, and found psychopaths scored notably lower on intelligence tests. The correlation between psychopathy and intelligence is close to zero, and the "evil genius" is a Hollywood invention.
  • A study scanning nearly 700 volunteers from birth found that life-long offenders have smaller brains.
  • And her informal side of it: scientists and people with advanced degrees are typically mild-mannered; the most aggressive people tend to settle things with fists.
This is a 180 reversal from the domesticated animal syndrome.

Three — what about the Neanderthals?

12:01–17:02 Hominins did not evolve the way most animals did. Other species gained speed, teeth or bite force; hominins became encephalized instead, and traded muscle for the brain's energy — 2% of body weight consuming nearly a quarter of intake. Had strength been the deciding factor, she says, the Neanderthals would still be here: they were stronger, more muscular and larger-brained, though with a bigger body and so a smaller EQ.

If self-domestication is a real process, other hominins should show it. Neanderthals appeared in Europe 400,000 years ago, lived alongside modern humans and died out about 28,000 years ago, and the archaeology has them painting, caring for their wounded and burying their dead.

A 2019 study puts human self-domestication after the split from Neanderthals and Denisovans, about 600,000 years ago — which implies its authors do not count Neanderthals as self-domesticated. And that is where her strongest-stated objection lands:

Then we should have seen a brain size decline starting 600k years ago. But this is not the case — because human brain size and encephalization continued to grow bigger for 500,000 years and peaking around 100k years ago… How come the domesticating process caused two opposite effects?

Her closing position is measured 17:20–17:34: more than one factor probably drove the reduction, self-domestication may have operated in some periods, "but it's far from conclusive."

What you can check yourself

  • Wrangham's and Hare's position is published and can be read in their own words rather than in summary.
  • Balcarcel's cattle result is the one controlled comparison in the video, the control being the fighting bulls. Both halves are checkable.
  • Dunbar's number and the research behind it are well known and widely argued about.
  • Boutwell's meta-analysis covered 187 papers, which is a specific claim about a specific publication.
  • The bonobo arithmetic is the whole of her first objection and a reader can redo it: 398 cc at 52.25 kg against 358 cc at 37.73 kg. Note that she divides brain by body weight directly, where a formal encephalization quotient scales body mass by an exponent — the direction of the answer does not change under the usual exponents, which is worth confirming rather than taking on trust.
  • Domesticated animals' 10 to 25% smaller brains is a general finding across species.
  • The brain's share of body mass and of energy use — about 2% and about 25% — is standard physiology.
  • Neanderthal dates, endocranial volumes and body mass are all published, as is the evidence for burial, pigment use and care of the injured.

What the video does not settle

  • The second question sets differences between individuals today against a change in a species average over evolutionary time. That violent offenders score lower on IQ tests is offered against a claim about selection across a population, and the two are not distinguished.
  • The scientists-and-bullies comparison has nothing behind it. It sits between two sourced findings and is offered without a source of its own.
  • What the hypothesis claims is not set out before it is answered. The lion example is offered against it; whether the proposal concerns reactive aggression or cooperation is never stated.
  • The Dunbar comparison is made by head-count. A chimpanzee community of 150 and a human band of 150 are set against each other, with nothing said about what Dunbar's argument measures or how the two kinds of group are organised.
  • The 600,000-year objection is stated and not followed up. What the hypothesis's own authors say about the timing is not reported.
  • Every finding rests on one paper, and none is named by title. A 2012 paper for the primate volumes, a 2019 study for the split date, "another study" for the 700 volunteers. The 187-paper meta-analysis is given by author and count only.
  • EQ is used as a measure of intelligence here and questioned elsewhere. Neanderthals are "probably less intelligent than us" on an EQ argument here; in the brain sizes video she reports that the effect of brain size change on cognition is unknown.
  • The sample is seven animals and she says so. The averages come from three bonobos and four chimpanzees — which the table on screen shows plainly. She flags the need for larger samples without letting it soften the conclusion she draws.
  • The evidence for the hypothesis is illustrated; the evidence against it is shown. The primate table is the only figure in the video. The cattle result, the psychopathy meta-analysis and the domestication syndrome itself all run over stock photographs.
