An Eye-opening Interpretation on Göbekli Tepe "Handbags" & Vulture

Tina · 20 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-23

deflatedwhymainstreamgobekli-tepehandbagsshared-symbols

Pillar 43 at Göbekli Tepe — a surrounding pillar in Enclosure D, and the most argued-over stone in the field. Three handbag-shaped objects sit along the top. Because a large bird dominates the register below, it is also called the Vulture Stone 00:00–00:56.

The readings she is arguing against: that the pillar records the Younger Dryas comet impact; that the bags held crop seeds to replenish the earth after the flood; that the disc beside the vulture is the head of the headless man carved below it. Her promise is a different reading, tied to the new excavation results — and a spoiler: "these so-called handbags might not be bags at all" 00:56–01:42.

She begins by noting the pillar's difficulty honestly 01:42–02:16. It is graphically complex, with elements scattered over three sides: the vulture, the bags, birds, a circle, a scorpion, snakes, a headless man, a big cat. The lower portion is damaged and the carvings are incomplete, so nobody is seeing the whole picture.

The carvings themselves, close enough to judge every element of the reading.

What she is building on

A recap of the previous video 02:16–03:16: keyhole trenches to bedrock showed Göbekli Tepe was a permanent settlement with houses, human burials, cisterns and channels for rainwater and hearths for fire; the site holds countless stone points, grinding tools and stone vessels for processing food; it was a populated town rather than a ritual centre visited occasionally; and the fill may be sediment and erosion rather than deliberate burial.

To that she adds her own hall-of-fame proposal — that the enclosures commemorate hunting, the way a modern hunting club or the Explorers Club displays trophies — and grants immediately that it does not cover Pillar 43 03:16–04:51. "The bag carvings seem unrelated to hunting. What are they?"

The vulture and the disc

First the bird 04:51–07:16. The vulture is carved with its wings spread almost touching a disc. Against the reading that the disc is the missing head of the headless man below, she makes an argument from the site's own corpus: the human heads and masks found at Göbekli Tepe all have prominent noses and brow bones, and where archaeologists have suggested a carved shape is a head it is because those features are there. The disc on Pillar 43 is a plain circle.

So a sphere in the air: sun or moon. She says sun, because of what the bird is doing.

The bird's posture, and what it is doing.

Spreading the wings wide in the early morning is a signature vulture habit — the horaltic pose — which increases the body's surface area so the sun can warm the bird, dry its feathers and kill bacteria and parasites. On her reading the carving is a vulture in that pose beside the rising sun: a scene that is striking to watch now and would have been then.

Then the move that carries the rest of the video. Could it be symbolic instead? "Maybe. But if that's the case we would have seen similar repeated carvings on site." The vulture-and-sun appears once. A symbol recurs; a record need not. And the same test applies to the bags — a symbolic item should appear as a repeated motif, not once on one pillar.

Against the seed bags

The excavation analysis says the grain processed, cooked and fermented at Göbekli Tepe was wild grain 07:16–08:00. If advanced beings had brought domesticated seed in bags, there should be domesticated grain in the deposits — and over 2,000 years of occupation there was ample time for it to be used and spread. There is none. So the seed-bag idea does not hold.

Reading the carving

Now the detail 08:00–09:26. Above and below the three bags runs a woven or hatched band. Each bag is accompanied by a small animal figure: the right-hand one an upside-down quadruped; the left one something like a bent bird with its head facing down; the middle one a big cat-like animal falling down a slope.

She anticipates the obvious objection to the middle figure — that it could be a standing quadruped, a gazelle or long-horned goat facing the bag's handle — and answers it from the site's own artistic convention: at Göbekli Tepe the thickness of an animal's limbs is rendered consistently and truthfully. If that animal were standing or charging, its hind legs would have to be far thicker than its front legs, which is not how these masons worked. So it is an animal facing down, tail over its back.

Which gives her the observation the video turns on: all three animals are facing down, as if falling over something.

Traps

In what situation does an animal fall? 09:26–12:20

Traps, she argues, are an underdiscussed subject in Stone Age archaeology, where the assumption is that hunter-gatherers chased animals and killed them with stone points. Humans are not strong or fast and have no fangs or claws, so how did they compete with Pleistocene megafauna and become the top predator?

She cites a 2021 study reconstructing Stone Age human nutrition, which concluded humans were apex predators for two million years, arguing from stomach acidity — high compared with omnivores and even with other predators — from the structure of human fat cells, and from the genome. The study does not exclude starch-rich food from as early as 100,000 years ago, but has meat as the majority of the diet; and it proposes that only the extinction of large animals and the decline in animal numbers towards the end of the Stone Age pushed humans into grain and vegetables and finally into domestication and farming.

What the apex-predator claim is cited from.

Her answer to the question is the brain rather than the body: better methods, avoiding injury and maximising the catch. And the method is trapping, which — unlike active hunting — works while nobody is there.

Two pieces of evidence 12:20–12:58. In 2019 anthropologists found two human-dug pits in Mexico, at least 15,000 years old, used to trap mammoth, containing the remains of fourteen woolly mammoths. And across the Middle East, the Levant, North Africa, Central Asia and Arabia, over 6,000 mega hunting traps have been identified, built at different times over the last 10,000 years — several of them not far from Göbekli Tepe and the other Taş Tepeler sites, one just south of Karahan Tepe.

That Stone Age people dug pits to catch very large animals.

Desert kites

The traps are desert kites 12:58–15:12, named by aviators in the 1920s who first spotted them from the air because they look like old-fashioned children's kites with streamers. On the ground they are low, obscure stone walls.

How the traps work, and where the pit sits in them.

The design: long driving lines of low walling converge into a funnel and deliver animals to a trap — a pit or a precipice. They are among the largest structures of their era, laid across the regular paths of herding animals and stretching over large areas, with entrances and ends placed where the slope breaks so the animals do not see what is coming.

The detail she emphasises is that the walls did not need to be high. Migrating animals like antelope and gazelle avoid unusual structures — rows of stones, shallow ditches — so a low obstacle steers them without frightening them off. The guide walls run from hundreds of metres to five kilometres, ending in pits usually lined with stone and up to 4 m deep. A kite can have anywhere from one to more than twenty pits, sometimes several side by side. The pits were covered with latticework or other material, so that from the ground they were not visible.

The proposal

"The shapes of these pits look quite familiar, don't they? They resemble handbags, wouldn't you agree?" 15:12–16:10

The proposal itself - the carvings set against excavated pit traps.

Her reading: the three carvings are pit traps; the woven bands directly above and below them are the lattice covers; and the three downward-facing animals — two of them with their heads touching the ground — are animals falling in. She extends it to other carvings: snake traps and baited traps for predators, and another Göbekli Tepe pillar with multiple birds as a record of bird trapping with nets.

The rest is a chain from trapping to the town 16:10–19:16. The number of kites implies abundant game and mass hunting; mass hunting yields more meat than can be eaten fresh, so it has to be dried, cured and stored, which requires settling near the traps; an investment that large implies long-term plans; a bigger community means more hands to build and maintain the traps; and so the settlement grows. Could that be the origin of Göbekli Tepe? Her closing thought is that traps able to hold animals alive for a while might be where animal domestication in the Near East began.

What you can check yourself

  • The three carvings are photographed close up. Each is a rectangular body under a semicircular arch, in a row, with a hatched band above and below and a small animal figure beside each. Whether the three animals face downwards is decidable by looking.
  • The horaltic pose is a real and well-documented behaviour, and the comparison photograph shows it plainly.
  • The heads-have-noses argument is checkable against the site's finds. Göbekli Tepe has produced carved human heads and masks, and whether they consistently emphasise nose and brow is a question about a published assemblage.
  • The apex-predator study is identified on screen as a Tel Aviv University release of 5 April 2021, summarising a review that drew about 25 lines of evidence from about 400 papers.
  • The Mexican mammoth pits are dated and counted: two pits, at least 15,000 years old, fourteen animals.
  • Desert kites are mapped, published and visible in satellite imagery. The 6,000 figure, the wall lengths, the 4 m pit depth and the lattice covers are all claims about a well-studied class of monument.
  • The wild-grain finding is the strongest single point against the seed-bag reading, and it belongs to the excavators rather than to her.

What the video does not settle

  • The kites are younger than the pillar, and the video knows it. She says Göbekli Tepe is older and that the traps "may well overlap" with the stone-hill towns. The 6,000 traps are given as built "over the last 10,000 years", and none of the ones near Göbekli Tepe or Karahan Tepe is dated on screen. If the excavated pits she compares are millennia later than Pillar 43, the resemblance is to something the carvers had not seen.
  • The comparison is between a plan and an elevation. The excavated pits are photographed from above and are rounded rectangles and ovals lined with stone. The carvings read as an object seen from the side, and the semicircular arch above each rectangle — the part that makes them look like bags at all — has no counterpart in any of the four pit photographs.
  • The lattice-cover reading is asserted without an example. No photograph or reconstruction of a latticed pit cover is shown beside the hatched bands.
  • Her own repetition test cuts both ways. She rejects a symbolic reading because the motif appears once, which is fair. But a record of a hunting technique used across the region for millennia might also be expected to recur, and the uniqueness that argues against symbolism is not evidence for traps either.
  • The apex-predator study is shown as a press release and is a contested claim. The summary on screen frames the question — specialised carnivores or generalist omnivores — as open, and the release is a university's account of its own researchers' review. The video reports the conclusion as settled and does not mention that it is disputed.
  • The limb-thickness argument is good and is applied once. It is a real observation about the carvers' convention, and it is used to settle the orientation of one figure without being tested against a set of examples.
  • The disc is decided in half a sentence. Sun or moon, and the moon is dropped without an argument; the case for the sun rests entirely on the bird's posture.
  • The chain from traps to town has no link at the site. Every step is plausible and none is evidenced at Göbekli Tepe: no trap is dated to the occupation, no meat-processing installation is cited, and the domestication coda is offered as a possibility on top of a possibility.
  • The damaged lower portion is acknowledged and then set aside. Having said that nobody can read the whole story because part of it is missing, the video proceeds to read the top of the pillar as a complete statement.
  • "Their brain to body ratio was higher than ours" is unsourced and is used to support the claim that Stone Age hunters were strategic.
  • The comet reading is dismissed by reference to the previous video rather than argued here.

What we have not checked

  • No excavation report or paper has been read. The wild-grain finding, the keyhole trench results, the desert kite dimensions and the 6,000 trap count are as she reports them.
  • The 2021 study is identified only through a news release — Tel Aviv University, 5 April 2021 — and the paper itself has not been located. Its conclusion is contested in the literature and the video does not say so.
  • The 2019 Mexican discovery is described without a site name, institution or publication. The two pits, the 15,000-year date and the fourteen mammoths are as stated.
  • No desert kite near Göbekli Tepe or Karahan Tepe is dated in the video, and none has been dated here. This is the load-bearing gap in the whole proposal.
  • The four pit photographs are uncredited and unlocated, and their scale is judgeable only from the excavators and scales in two of them.
  • The claim that Göbekli Tepe's carved human heads consistently show pronounced noses and brows has not been checked against the published finds.
  • The vulture photograph shows turkey vultures, a New World species, illustrating a pose that is general to vultures. The bird on the pillar is not identified to species by anyone in the video.
