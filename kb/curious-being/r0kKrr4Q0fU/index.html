Ancient Chinese Cave Tomb Shares Similarities with Osireion in Egypt?

Tina · 15 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-23

cross-continentalout-of-sequencetwo-industrieshowrock-cut-sitesmachining-and-tool-markspolygonal-masonry

Beidongshan is a king's burial place at Xuzhou, China, from over 2,100 years ago 00:00–01:11. The city holds at least eight Han dynasty royal tombs; the Han ruled from 206 BC to 220 AD. The site was looted repeatedly, but jade plaques from the king's burial suit survived, and coins found in it date between 175 and 128 BC. The tomb is cut into a granite hill and is considered the most complex of the Han royal tombs.

Two levels, two technologies

The observation the entry exists for 01:28–04:15.

  • The main level held the king's coffin, cut into the granite hill in a semi-symmetrical layout. Several chambers with gable ceilings made to what she calls amazing accuracy; the stone finish so smooth it looks polished; traces of cinnabar paint; and conjunction lines she asks the viewer to look at closely, which are sharp and precisely executed.
  • The lower level, the auxiliary complex, reached by a staircase outside the tomb gate, is described by Chinese archaeologists as the king's entertainment and supply centre for the afterlife. It is built from stone blocks of varying sizes assembled with mortar, some walls larger blocks, some end walls carved. She calls it crude and primitive against the clean-cut main level.

Two ways of making the same room in one monument - the observation the entry exists for.

Two things she does before arguing, which are worth recording. She shows an old photograph taken before excavation in which the entrance looks different from today's, and concludes the front sections may have been reconstructed — so she restricts her argument to the chambers behind the main entrance, which she says are minimally restored. And she points out a cement ceiling in one photo as a recent addition to be ignored.

Her question: if the king's masons built both, why in two such different ways, and why would a king accept it?

Her answer is that they were built by different people in different eras — the cave found and reused, the lower level rebuilt from what was lying there 04:28–05:00.

Why the better work is the older work

The reasoning against the intuitive reading 05:02–06:18. The obvious objection is that crude means early: the lower level came first, the cave later with improved technique. She rejects it on two grounds.

If the masons could carve smooth chambers in granite, they would have used that method on the lower level rather than recycling a crude structure — "like building a new mansion while letting the old attached garage remain as is." And the gap between the two is too large to cross inside one king's lifetime: she puts it at roughly 2,000 years of development for us to go from stacked blocks to precisely carved stone chambers.

Four things it shares with the Osireion

1. Stone joints 06:28–08:32. At Beidongshan, two stacked 8-ton blocks used to seal the hallway meet in a precise joint with sharp right angles. The Osireion's blocks are far larger but the joints, she argues, look identical — both joggle joints, interlocking for stability; the Osireion's of the mortise and tenon type, Beidongshan's tongue and groove. Her point is the same one she makes about the Osireion: these are woodworking joints, rarely cut in stone, requiring a long narrow trench in one block and a matching protrusion on the other.

She adds an argument about context: the ancient Chinese were not stone builders in the way the Egyptians, Greeks or Romans were, and the few surviving ancient Chinese stone structures are neither as old nor as impressive — yet these joints suggest otherwise.

The first of her four shared features, with both halves marked so a reader can judge the comparison.

2. Gable ceilings 08:32–09:55. Stone gable ceilings, she argues, are rare among ancient sites. She finds them in China at Beidongshan and Guishan, and in Egypt concentrated in pyramid chambers — naming the Queen's chamber, and the pyramids of Teti and Unas at Saqqara, which carry the Pyramid Texts beneath their ceilings. The Osireion is the closest resemblance outside a pyramid. The Beidongshan ceilings are carved directly from the rock; the Osireion's are built from tightly fitted blocks and larger.

The shared roof form, across three sites and two continents.

3. Cyclopean walls 10:11–11:39. Some main-level walls at Beidongshan are built of differently shaped blocks with very tight fittings, looking custom made or moulded, every block fitting perfectly — including an L-shaped joint detail, a slight undercut on one block. She notes the same L-shaped seams between the Osireion's giant blocks, and the same walling in Peru, Egypt, Greece, Italy and Japan. She says this is the first Chinese site she has found with such a wall, and that there are not many photographs of Beidongshan to work from.

4. Ridge and groove tool marks 11:39–13:03. Most of the main level is smooth, but some ceilings carry the uniform parallel marks she has tracked across other sites — Baalbek, Petra, the Yangshan monument — and which she argues resemble modern machine marks. Her reading: the chambers were roughed out by something with teeth and then finished smooth. Close-ups of the Osireion show the same marks on certain blocks. Since both sites show unfinished stone, she suggests both may be incomplete projects.

Where she lands

Her conclusion is careful, and she states the disclaimer herself 13:24–14:55: "I know that Beidongshan and Osireion are probably not related in any way at all." The similarities, she argues, may instead be evidence of an unknown advanced global civilisation.

Her closing reasoning is arithmetic about available time. Known history runs back about 6,000 years; anatomically modern humans with brains of today's size existed by at least 130,000 years ago; which leaves 124,000 years unaccounted for. Göbekli Tepe at 10,000 years old already shows civilisations beyond what was expected. Our 6,000 years is about 5% of modern human history — "What did our ancestors achieve with their 95%?"

What you can check yourself

  • The two levels are the checkable core, and the contrast is entirely photographic: a polished carved granite chamber above, mortared rubble below, in one monument.
  • The coins date the burial — 175 to 128 BC — and the jade suit plaques place it firmly in Han royal practice.
  • The joints are on the blocks and can be photographed and measured.
  • Whether stone gable ceilings are as rare as she says is a question anyone can test by looking, and she invites viewers to name others.
  • The pyramids of Teti and Unas have published sections, and their gable ceilings and Pyramid Texts are documented.
  • Guishan, the other Xuzhou cave tomb, is a second example of the same construction in the same city and can be compared directly.
  • The pre-excavation photograph she uses to argue the entrance was reconstructed is a check anyone can repeat: compare the old photograph with the site today.

What the video does not settle

  • The Queen's chamber is in Khufu, not Khafre. The narration places it in the Pyramid of Khafre. Khafre has no such chamber, which is the central point of another entry in this corpus — that Khafre is solid. It is a slip rather than an argument, but it is in a list being used as evidence.
  • "Crude below, fine above" has a mundane reading the video does not test. A tomb chamber holding a king and an ancillary storage complex are different jobs with different budgets, and finishing the burial chamber to a standard the supply rooms do not get is ordinary practice, not a technological discontinuity. The mansion-and-garage analogy assumes the two were meant to be comparable.
  • The 2,000-year development claim is asserted. No account is given of what it is measured between, and the history of stone carving does not obviously support it.
  • The argument about Chinese stonework is an argument from absence. That few ancient Chinese stone structures survive is expected of a civilisation whose monumental tradition was timber, and it says little about the capability of Han masons — who are otherwise well evidenced, including in the several other royal tombs at Xuzhou she mentions.
  • The frames do not show the polish the narration describes. "The stone finish is so smooth it looks polished" is said over images of chambers whose walls and ceilings are visibly rough, mottled and tool-textured. What the images do support is the geometry — a clean gable ridge and squared doorways cut into living rock — which is the more interesting claim anyway, and the entry should rest on that rather than on the finish.
  • Both levels use the same form. The lower level's ceiling is also a gable — built up from stacked slabs rather than carved from the rock. Two builders separated by an unknown gulf of time arriving at the same roof profile is at least as consistent with one design intention executed two ways for two purposes, and the video does not address it.
  • The evidence base is thin and she says so. "There are not many photos of Beidongshan for us to do more research on" is her own comment, and the cyclopean-wall claim rests on it.
  • Similarity is not contact, and she does not claim it is. She states plainly that the two sites are probably unrelated. The entry should keep that hedge visible, because the "global civilisation" conclusion is a different and much larger claim than the four observations that precede it.
  • The 124,000 years argument is about opportunity, not evidence. That there was time for something to happen is not evidence that it did, and Göbekli Tepe shows that expectations can be wrong rather than that a global civilisation existed.
  • The tool marks are asserted to resemble machine marks and are not measured. Spacing, depth and profile would distinguish a rotary cutter from a hand pick, and nothing of the kind is offered here or in the entries that make the same claim elsewhere.

What we have not checked

  • Nothing about Beidongshan has been verified independently. The site is little photographed — a limit the presenter states herself — and the layout, the finish and the joints are known here only from her images.
  • The names and dates are as given and have not been confirmed: Beidongshan and Guishan at Xuzhou, at least eight Han royal tombs, coins dated 175–128 BC, the jade suit plaques.
  • The attribution of the lower level's purpose to Chinese archaeologists is unsourced.
  • The Queen's chamber is attributed to the Pyramid of Khafre, which appears to be an error for Khufu; the on-screen labelling has not been separately checked against the narration.
  • The claim that stone gable ceilings are rare among ancient sites is her survey and has not been tested.
  • The 8-ton figure for the hallway blocks is as stated.
  • The 130,000-year figure for anatomically modern humans and the 10,000-year date for Göbekli Tepe are given without citation.
  • Whether the chambers behind the main entrance are genuinely minimally restored, as she argues from the pre-excavation photograph, has not been confirmed against an excavation record.
