Massive Chinese Cave System could be a Residential Complex

Tina · 14 min · watch on YouTube · summarised 2026-08-23

cross-continentalhowmainstreamwhyrock-cut-sitesmachining-and-tool-markscross-site-similarity

Guyaju — the name means ancient cliff dwelling — was found in 1984 on the steep granite slopes of a gorge near Beijing, above a plain and close to a large lake. It is at least a thousand years old. There are no historical records of it, and she says no dateable archaeological evidence either, so who built it is unknown 00:00–00:27.

The complex is 117 caves containing about 350 chambers, cut on both sides of a secluded gorge in three clusters over 24 acres. Archaeologists have found remains of people who lived there in different periods, but concluded that those occupants were not the original builders 00:27–01:05.

The site as a whole - how many chambers there are and how they sit in the rock.

Her two opening questions 01:05–01:53. Granite is igneous, weathering-resistant and hard to carve — how was this done at this scale? And why do it at all: it remains baffling to her that people around the world chose labour-intensive stone when wood, clay, brick or rammed earth were to hand. The tool marks, she says, are the same large uniform marks found at sites across Asia, the Middle East and Europe, which she has argued elsewhere resemble modern machine marks.

The tool-mark claim, with all three panels visible at once.

The fortress reading

The approach is a sequence of obstacles: a rock guardhouse at the entry, a long uphill walk to a second guardhouse, a steeper climb to the real entrance, another checkpoint, then a narrow way into the interior. Guyaju, she says, is designed like a fortress 02:15–02:51.

Inside there are about eight storeys of chambers, and the floors and walls between them are consistently 20–30 cm thick — a detail she reads as engineering knowledge rather than luck. Many dwellings run over several levels linked by corridors, steps and stairs, and the interiors have high thresholds, windows, room divisions, stoves, wall niches and a drainage system. Some carry beam holes and faded awning marks. These, she says, are not primitive caves 02:51–03:45.

The original stairs are cut into the rock at between 45 and 60 degrees. Beside them run two channels she describes as looking like cart rails, and the first steps are shaped like footprints — a detail she returns to later 03:45–04:23.

The channels beside the original stair, which she names and does not pursue.

The rooms are small

The chambers are honeycombed and vary in shape: rectangular, round, square. By modern standards they are small. Doorways are about five feet, so most people must stoop; some rooms are 4 m², the larger ones a few hundred square feet; ceilings run 1.5–1.8 m. Scholars, she reports, therefore take the community to have been no taller than about 1.6 m 04:23–05:03.

How big the rooms and doorways actually are.

She defends that as unremarkable rather than strange. People today are much taller than people were sixty years ago; early dynastic Egyptians stood about 5 ft 3, Greeks and Romans 5 ft 5 to 5 ft 7, ancient Chinese about 5 ft 3. So small rooms fit our smaller ancestors 05:03–05:41.

The beds

Many chambers have low channels with ledges on four sides, which historians identify as kang — the heated sleeping platform used by northern peoples including the Manchu. She accepts that reading and explains it: a kang works like a Roman hypocaust, circulating hot air beneath, except that a hypocaust heats a floor and a kang heats a bed for several people. Lay a wooden board across the ledges and it becomes a warm bed. Kang are still in use in north-east China, as a place to sleep, gather and eat 05:41–07:07.

The identification of the low channels, checked against a kang that is still in use.

The sizes of the kang at Guyaju, she notes, match the small rooms, low doorways and low ceilings.

The largest structure is the Guantangzi, or chief's court: eight chambers over two floors with stone kang on both sides of the main room, at the foot of one of the clusters, which she suggests served for meetings or ceremonies 07:07–07:36.

The comparisons

Seven or eight smaller cave systems lie in the nearby ranges, one cut into a near-vertical face with a high entrance reached by a carved ladder — awkward to climb, which she reads as defensive: an attacker arriving that way is in a poor position 07:36–08:23.

Then the wider set 08:23–10:32:

  • Bandelier National Monument, New Mexico. Caves cut in a steep cliff, doorways high up, and long lines of beam holes across the face. And, she says, the same footprint-shaped steps.
  • Vardzia, Georgia. She calls the rock dwellings almost identical to Guyaju's. Her point about it is the useful one: a major earthquake in the 13th century sheared away the outer walls of many of the Vardzia caves and exposed the rooms behind — and she proposes that something similar happened at Guyaju, removing part of the outer wall and leaving the interiors open.
  • Footprint stairs at a second Georgian cave city, which she calls cousins of both.
  • Hasankeyf on the Tigris, and Derinkuyu, whose stairs, niches and interior windows she reads as the same kinds of space.
  • Cave houses in Spain and Italy, some still lived in or let to visitors, with rounded doorways, separate rooms, niches — and, on the Italian ones, exterior beam holes.

The two comparisons the cross-continental case rests on.

Two things, she says, all of them share: proximity to water, and a position on a steep slope. Guyaju is near a big lake, Vardzia by a river, Hasankeyf on the Tigris, Bandelier by a stream. Water is essential to any settlement; height is what a castle is for. She runs the analogy out — the cave system on a slope as a motte, the narrow hidden gateway as a gatehouse, the top level as the keep with the best view and the best protection 10:32–11:43.

Her conclusion from the set is modest by the standards of the channel: that a widespread cave-carving technology, and a widespread civilisation to go with it, may have existed 11:43–12:00.

The current explanations

Three, each given briefly 12:00–13:22.

The Xi, a Mongol tribe of the Tang dynasty, about 1,300 years ago. Records have them in the region for only thirty years before moving north-east. She allows they may have used the caves but finds it hard to credit that they built them — and asks why no comparable cave systems have been found where the tribe later settled.

Food storage. She calls it absurd: storage is done in a barn or a silo, not in hundreds of rooms on eight levels.

Beacon towers along the Great Wall. A real beacon tower, she shows, is a single four-walled brick structure, nothing like Guyaju.

One of the standard explanations, set against the thing it is supposed to explain.

What you can check yourself

  • The site is open and heavily photographed. The tiers of chambers, the fronts sheared off, the interior partitions visible in section, the beam holes, the stairs and the channels beside them are all in visitor photographs.
  • The room dimensions are measurable and the scale images are unambiguous. Her own frames show an adult sitting in a chamber with the ceiling barely above their head, and a man seated in a doorway that reaches his shoulders.
  • The kang comparison can be checked against a living example. She shows a photograph of an elderly man kneeling on a part-dismantled kang with its flue channels open, beside the rock-cut channels at Guyaju. Kang are in current use, so the identification is testable against something still being built.
  • Vardzia's 13th-century earthquake is a documented event, and whether it removed the outer walls of the caves is a matter of record. That makes her Guyaju proposal a real hypothesis: if the fronts were sheared off, the rock should show it, and the debris should be somewhere below.
  • Bandelier's beam holes are photographable, and so are the footings of the built rooms that once stood in front of the cliff — which is what beam holes in a cliff face usually mean.
  • The stature figures are published anthropometry. Whether early dynastic Egyptians, Greeks, Romans and Han Chinese averaged between five foot three and five foot seven is in the literature.
  • A Great Wall beacon tower is a standing building and the comparison she draws with it is visible at a glance.

What the video does not settle

  • Her own evidence points somewhere ordinary and she stops short of it. She accepts the historians' identification of the kang, notes that the kang sizes match the rooms, and then shows that the rooms fit people of the stature ordinary historical populations actually had. A granite complex furnished with northern Chinese heated sleeping platforms, sized for people around 1.6 metres, is a description of a normal residential site. The video assembles that case and does not draw it.
  • The claim of no dateable evidence contradicts the next sentence. At the opening there is "neither historical records nor dateable archaeological evidence"; twenty seconds later, archaeologists have found remains of occupants from several different periods. Relics from different eras are dateable evidence. Which finds, from which levels, and on what grounds archaeologists decided the occupants were not the builders, is not stated and no source is given.
  • The mark comparison does not match in the respect it needs to. Her three panels put Baalbek's long straight vertical striations, Petra's broad sweeping curves and a Guyaju surface side by side. They run in different directions at different coarseness — and the Guyaju panel is under such heavy colour treatment that its texture cannot be assessed at all.
  • No close view of a worked surface at Guyaju is shown. The rock in every frame is coarse, granular and deeply weathered. Whether a fresh cut face carries tool marks, and what kind, is the question the hardness argument turns on, and there is no image of one.
  • The machine-mark conclusion is imported. It is argued in earlier videos and asserted here, so the hardest claim in the entry arrives as a premise.
  • "Doesn't resemble other Chinese sites of this era" sits oddly with the kang. The furniture is identified as a specifically northern Chinese domestic form still in use. The video does not reconcile a site that is unlike Chinese architecture with a site fitted out in Chinese domestic practice.
  • The resemblances shown are resemblances of function. Rooms cut in a soft cliff near water, on a defensible slope, with beam holes for a timber front, niches for storage and stairs between levels, are what people who live in cliffs produce. Convergence on the obvious solution is compatible with a shared technology and equally compatible with independent invention, and the video does not offer anything that separates the two.
  • The rock is different in every comparison. Guyaju is granite on her own account. Vardzia and Bandelier are volcanic tuff, Derinkuyu and Cappadocia likewise, Petra and Hasankeyf sandstone. Sites cut in soft tuff are not evidence about what it takes to cut granite, which is the question she opened with.
  • The footprint steps are asserted as a match and shown separately. No side-by-side of the Guyaju steps against the New Mexico or Georgian ones appears; each is shown in its own frame, and the comparison is made in words.
  • The "cart rails" are named and not pursued. Two channels flanking a cut stair are shown once and never returned to; no measurement, no gauge, nothing about where they run from or to.
  • The food storage explanation is dismissed in one sentence. Nothing is offered against it beyond the observation that granaries are usually barns.
  • The Xi objection runs one way only. That no similar caves have been found where the tribe settled later is a fair question. That thirty years of recorded occupation is short is not evidence of anything, and a group taking over an existing site is ordinary.
  • "A widespread civilisation" is the only claim here without a picture. Everything else in the video is argued from images. That one is argued from an impression.

What we have not checked

  • No archaeological report on Guyaju has been located or read. The 117 caves, 350 chambers, three clusters, 24 acres, eight storeys, 20–30 cm floors, 4 m² rooms and 1.5–1.8 m ceilings are as she gives them.
  • The "at least 1,000 years old" date is unsourced, and so is the statement that archaeologists concluded the known occupants were not the builders. That second claim is doing a great deal of work and rests on nothing shown.
  • The kang identification is attributed to historians in general and not to a named source.
  • The Xi tribe, the thirty years, and the move north-east are as stated and have not been traced.
  • Vardzia's 13th-century earthquake is reported here from the video only.
  • The rock types at the comparison sites are not stated in the video. That Vardzia, Bandelier and Derinkuyu are cut in volcanic tuff and Petra and Hasankeyf in sandstone is general knowledge applied here, not something the video says or shows, and should be confirmed before it is repeated.
  • The stature figures are quoted without a source.
  • The Guyaju panel in the mark comparison is not assessable. It is reproduced under a strong colour gradient, and no judgement about what its surface shows is possible from it.
  • The transcript's "Baalbeck" is corrected to Baalbek. Guantangzi is transcribed as heard and its characters have not been checked.
